Turning the Casebook into a 3D game

You already have a working game. What you don’t have yet is a third dimension, a pipeline, and a scope you can actually finish by December 2026. This is the route.

01

Start from what already works

Your game trial is not a placeholder — it is a finished design. Five chapters per episode, four choices, a thirty-second clock that auto-picks A, three stats (Sanity, Awareness, Wisdom), a teaching after every choice, a core lesson at the end, and a D-to-S rank. That loop is proven and it is good.

The reframe

You are not designing a game. You are re-rendering a game you already have. Every chapter you write for the 2D trial is a chapter the 3D version can run unchanged — the choice data is just JSON. The 3D work is presentation, not design. That is the difference between a two-year project and a one-year one.

The prototype linked above runs your exact loop in 3D: you walk a void deck at night, find the note, the clock starts, the teaching lands, the file is sealed with a rank. It is one HTML file. It works on your phone. Nothing in it is fake.

02

The one decision that decides everything else

Build encounter dioramas, not an open world. Each chapter is one small, dense, beautiful location you spend three to six minutes inside — a void deck, a temple courtyard, a hospital corridor, a stretch of Old Upper Thomson Road. You can walk around it. You cannot walk out of it.

  • Roughly ten times cheaper to build than a connected world.
  • It matches the source material — a case file is one place, one night.
  • It is the difference between shipping and not shipping. This is the decision that most first projects get wrong.

On “AAA graphics”

Don’t. Chasing photoreal will end the project. What you actually want is atmosphere, and horror is the cheapest genre on earth to make look expensive: darkness, fog, two or three strong lights, and good composition do almost all of it. The prototype is boxes, cylinders and cones with textures drawn in code — no models at all — and it reads as a Singapore void deck at 2am. Spend your budget on lighting and mood, not polygon counts.

Three.js, running as a plain website. Not a game engine — a JavaScript 3D library. That distinction is the whole reason it’s the right pick for you.

04

What AI does, and what stays yours

The honest split. Everything on the left is a conversation; everything on the right is you.

AI Handled for you

  • All the code — movement, camera, touch controls, the choice system, the timer, stats, saves, the entire UI.
  • Scene assembly: placing objects, setting up lights, tuning fog and colour.
  • Performance work — this is where a non-specialist would drown, and it’s exactly what AI is good at.
  • Converting and compressing models via command line, so you touch Blender as little as possible.
  • Deployment, domains, bilingual text, analytics.

You Nobody else can

  • The writing. Master Z’s teachings, the case scripts, the tone. This is the whole product.
  • Judging whether a scene actually feels unsettling. AI cannot feel dread.
  • Cultural and religious accuracy — what an offering looks like, what a person would really do, what would be disrespectful.
  • Choosing reference images so the AI has a target to hit.
  • Deciding when something is good enough to ship.

The skill you do need

Not coding — describing. “It looks flat and the shadows are too soft, here’s a photo of what I mean” gets you further than any tutorial. Screenshot what’s wrong, paste it, say what you want instead. That is the entire job.

Where the art comes from

Three sources, and the split matters more than the quality of any one of them.

ShareSourceUsed for
~60%Built in code Architecture. Void decks, corridors, walls, pillars, roads, stairs — these are boxes, and the AI can build them faster than you could find them. The prototype is entirely this.
~25%Free libraries
Poly Haven, Quaternius, Kenney, Fab		 Background dressing nobody looks at closely. Foliage, street furniture, bins, generic clutter, plus all your textures and sky maps. Check the licence is CC0 or clearly commercial-use.
~15%AI-generated + hand-fixed The things that carry meaning: the specific amulets, the urn, the talisman, Master Z himself, each spirit. Nobody notices a generic drain cover. Everybody notices a wrong amulet.

Proven in the prototype

The first-person hands are a real .glb file — four meshes, two PBR materials, ~23,000 triangles — built outside the game and loaded at runtime. Custom models drop in exactly this way, whether they come from Blender, from Meshy, or from a modelling script. Two traps that catch everyone: metal renders black until you give the scene an environment to reflect, and .glb stores colour in linear space, so a colour picked the normal way and written straight into a model file comes out pale and washed out. Both cost an hour the first time and thirty seconds after that.

Characters are the genuinely hard part — human faces and rigs are where AI 3D is still weakest. Plan to either buy one stylised character pack and re-dress it for every role, or keep spirits abstract: robes, silhouettes, light, distortion. Abstract is cheaper and scarier.

07

The route to December 2026

Sixteen months from now. Comfortable at part-time pace if you build in this order — each phase is only allowed to start once the previous one is on the internet and playable.

Sep — Weeks 1–2

Vertical slice

One chapter, end to end, ugly on purpose. Grey boxes, your real Chapter 1 script, real choices, real teaching, shipped to a real URL. The prototype is already most of this.

Done when  you can send a friend a link and they finish Chapter 1 on their phone.

Oct — Nov

The look

Art-pass that one chapter until it feels like the series. Real models, real textures, sound, the lighting recipe. Do not move on until one chapter looks the way you want all of them to look.

Done when  a still from the game could be the poster.

Dec — Feb

The kit

Build roughly thirty reusable pieces — HDB parts, temple parts, road parts, foliage, props, two or three spirit types. Everything after this is assembly, not creation.

Done when  a new location takes a day, not a week.

Mar — Aug

Content

Chapters 2 through 5 (and Episodes 2–3) built from the kit. This is the long, unglamorous stretch. It is also the stretch that only works if the three phases above were done properly.

Done when  all episodes are playable start to finish.

Sep — Nov

Polish and release

Voice, music, the 中文 pass, saves, progress tracking, an accessibility and content-warning pass, testing on cheap phones, and a launch that feeds the Facebook series.

Done when  December 2026 arrives and it’s live.

08

Two things only you can decide

The amulet problem

“Equip the right amulet to survive the ghost” is a great game mechanic and a poor teaching. It turns practice into a power-up and reads as superstition — the exact thing your disclaimers push back on. Your own Episode 1 core lesson gets this right: observe before reacting; do not blindly believe, blindly dismiss, or provoke. Make the correct action the answer — notice, withdraw, don’t take what isn’t yours, calm the mind, generate merit — and let objects be secondary, with the game explaining what an amulet actually means rather than what damage it blocks.

Education or funnel

The consultancy sells charms and remedies. A game where the answer is repeatedly “buy the right item” becomes an advertisement, and players feel it instantly. Decide this deliberately and early rather than letting it happen by drift — a game that is unmistakably not selling anything will do more for the consultancy than one that is.
