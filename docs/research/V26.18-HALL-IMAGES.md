## 1 · The white Buddha (D1)

**Names.** Gesture: Sanskrit *abhaya mudra*; Thai ท่าประทานอภัย / อภัยมุทรา (tha prathan aphai); English "gesture of fearlessness / reassurance". The seated image with this gesture and the left hand in the lap: **ปางห้ามมาร** (pang ham man), "holding back Mara".

**What it is.** One open hand raised, palm out: "the right hand is raised to shoulder height, with the palm facing outward and the fingers pointing upwards", meaning protection, peace and the dispelling of fear (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abhayamudra). The Thai list of named attitudes describes ปางห้ามมาร word for word as the hall's image: "ประทับ (นั่ง) ขัดสมาธิราบ พระหัตถ์ซ้ายวางหงาย อยู่บนพระเพลา (ตัก) พระหัตถ์ขวายกขึ้นป้องเสมอพระอุระ (อก) แสดงอาการห้าม" (https://th.wikipedia.org/wiki/ปางห้ามมาร, which cites Prince Damrong's ตำนานพุทธเจดีย์ and the 1957 royal-printing book เรื่องพระพุทธรูปปางต่างๆ; the same description at https://www.dmc.tv/a16847). Its story: after the Enlightenment, Mara's three daughters tried to tempt him under the banyan and he sent them away; in his last year he answered Mara that he would pass away in three months (same page).

**Three names are in use; say so, do not pick silently.**
- ปางห้ามมาร: the standard list above. The labelled bronze of that name at Wat Phra Pathom Chedi is the hall's posture exactly (photograph saved: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:พระพุทธรูปปางห้ามมาร_วัดพระปฐมเจดีย์ราชวรมหาวิหาร.jpg).
- ปางประทานอภัย (pang prathan aphai, "granting pardon"): defined by the raised right palm, "มีทั้งท่ายืน ท่าเดิน และท่าขัดสมาธิ", fingers upright, level and never bent (https://th.wikipedia.org/wiki/ปางประทานอภัย). But the same Phra Pathom set labels as ประทานอภัย the seated image with BOTH hands raised (dmc.tv page above; photograph saved).
- ปางประทานพร (pang prathan phon, "bestowing a blessing"): the popular name. The big image of Wat Tham Suea, Kanchanaburi, right hand raised palm out and left hand palm-up, is known as หลวงพ่อชินประทานพร (https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Luang_Pho_Chin_Prathan_Phon; photograph saved). In the textbooks ประทานพร is a different hand: the right hand on the knee, palm out (dmc.tv page; Luang Pho Khao at Klang Dong, "พระพุทธรูปปางประทานพรที่ใหญ่ที่สุดในโลก", https://th.wikipedia.org/wiki/วัดเทพพิทักษ์ปุณณาราม).

**Near twins that must not be built by mistake** (all from the Thai Wikipedia attitude pages, https://th.wikipedia.org/wiki/ปางพระพุทธรูป and its linked articles): ปางประทานเอหิภิกขุ (same seat, fingers curled slightly down); ปางอธิษฐานเพศบรรพชิต (raised palm turned to the left, edge-on; dmc.tv); ปางปัจเจกขณะ (a bowl in the lap); ปางขอฝน seated (beckoning hand, bathing cloth); ปางโปรดสัตว์ (left hand palm-up on the KNEE); the teaching forms (thumb and forefinger joined).

**Is a white Buddha with a gold cloth a known type in Isan or Laos?** Partly.
- The face is of the Lao (Lan Xang) family that Isan shares: flame finial, pointed curls, brows as a raised double line, long ears curving out, the small "Lan Xang smile", fingers of equal length. Compare the anthropology centre's art database on Lan Xang images: "รัศมีเป็นเปลวไฟ ... พระโอษฐ์กว้างยิ้มแบบล้านช้าง ... นิ้วพระหัตถ์ ... ยาวเท่ากัน" (https://seaarts.sac.or.th/artwork/389), "พระกรรณโค้งงอน" (https://seaarts.sac.or.th/artwork/392), "พระขนงโก่งเป็นสันยกขึ้นมาเป็นเส้นคู่" (https://so01.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/buraphaJ/article/view/276657, on Phra Phuttha Ratsami of Udon Thani).
- In Lao art the raised palm is best known on STANDING images with both hands raised (the Phra Bang; the Isan replica is described as "ประทับยืนปางห้ามสมุทร", https://seaarts.sac.or.th/artwork/387). I found no named historic Isan or Lao principal image in the hall's seated one-hand form.
- White Buddhas are loved in the region (วัดหลวงพ่อขาว above), but I found no source that names a pearl-white finish as a regional type. Treat the pearl paint as this hall's own finish. Not determined.
- The gold cloth is a separate textile laid on the statue, a general custom: both Phra Pathom bronzes wear an orange lattice brocade over the left shoulder and Phra Lao Thep Nimit in Amnat Charoen a yellow satin (photographs saved; a public cloth-draping ceremony, พิธีห่มผ้า, is reported at https://www.thairath.co.th/lottery/news/2926870 and only the custom is to be used from that page, never its lottery angle).

**Seen.** Right hand before the chest, palm out and turned a little to the statue's own left, four fingers straight, together, tips level, thumb upright beside them and not touching the forefinger. Left hand palm-up in the lap. Cross-legged. Right shoulder bare, robe edge modelled from the left shoulder. Large pointed knob curls, tall flame finial, a set gem between the brows, three neck rings, ears turned out at the lobes, small smile, downcast eyes with dark pupils. Pearl silver-white all over. Gold diamond-lattice brocade across the chest from the left shoulder, plain gold satin over the left arm and lap. A yellow garland hangs on the raised THUMB. Lotus: one round drum, two offset rows of big UPTURNED petals in crinkled gold leaf with silver-grey edges.

**What must never be wrong.** The RIGHT hand is the raised one. Palm out, fingers straight, together and level (bent tips or a finger ring make it another attitude). Left hand palm UP in the LAP. Right leg over left. The cloth over the LEFT shoulder. A flame finial, not a crown.

**Not determined.** What the hall calls this image; its maker and material; whether the gem is red or clear (it reads both ways in two frames).

## 2 · The green crowned Buddha (L17, L16)

**Names.** พระแก้วมรกต (Phra Kaeo Morakot), formally พระพุทธมหามณีรัตนปฏิมากร; English "the Emerald Buddha"; a copy is องค์จำลอง. The costume: **เครื่องทรงฤดูร้อน** (khrueang song ruedu ron), the summer or hot-season costume.

**What it is.** Thailand's palladium: a green stone Buddha seated in meditation (ปางสมาธิ), 48.3 cm wide and 66 cm high (https://th.wikipedia.org/wiki/พระพุทธมหามณีรัตนปฏิมากร). It has three gold costumes, changed by the King on the first waning day of lunar months 4, 8 and 12, about March, July and November (same page; https://www.trueplookpanya.com/dhamma/content/54826/-edu-his-relbud-rel-cul-).

**Which season.** Summer. The three differ plainly (https://mgronline.com/daily/detail/9590000115687; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emerald_Buddha):
- Summer: "เป็นเครื่องต้นอย่างพระมหากษัตริย์ ทำด้วยทองคำลงยา ประดับเพชรและมณีต่าง ๆ มงกุฎที่ทรงเป็นเทริด ยอดประดับเพชรเม็ดใหญ่": a king's regalia in enamelled gold with gems, and a crown. English Wikipedia lists "a stepped, pointed crown", a breast pendant, a sash, a necklace, armlets and bracelets. A maker of the 1996-97 set names พระมงกุฎ, ทับทรวง (pendant) and รัดพระองค์ (belt) (https://mgronline.com/dhamma/detail/9540000054370). Worn from about March to July.
- Rainy: "ทรงอย่างห่มดอง", a gold robe over one shoulder, and a headpiece of enamelled curls with a flame. Winter: a gold mesh shawl over both shoulders.

**What a correct summer costume looks like** (my reading of the saved photographs; the piece names beyond the three quoted are mine): a tall tiered crown with ear pieces; two long leaf sprays rising behind the shoulders; a broad collar; crossed chest bands meeting at a pendant; a belt with a large blue stone; armlets; stacked bracelets; rings; anklets; gold pieces with upturned tips on the feet, which lie at the knees. The torso is bare green stone under the jewellery. A jewelled cloth panel hangs down the front of the gold base.

**Seen.** All of that, simplified: crown with spire and temple pieces, pointed shoulder ornaments, crossed chains with a round pendant, armlets, bracelets, girdle with a short panel, big gold pieces wrapping each knee, hands in the lap. Tiered gold base with a red-and-gold brocade panel. Two tiny sprays (one silver, one gold) in small blue vases on its legs.

**What must never be wrong.** Hands in the lap, right over left (never on the knee). Green body. A crown in place of the flame finial, never both. No robe cloth with this costume.

**Not determined.** The Royal Household's own list of every piece's name (its pages did not load from here).

## 3 · The gold seated Buddha (L9)

**Names.** **ปางมารวิชัย** (pang maravichai; also ชนะมาร); Sanskrit *bhumisparsha mudra*; English "victory over Mara", "calling the earth to witness".

**What it is.** "นั่งขัดสมาธิ พระหัตถ์ซ้ายหงายวางบนพระเพลา พระหัตถ์ขวาวางคว่ำลงที่พระชานุ นิ้วพระหัตถ์ชี้ลงที่พื้นธรณี": the moment before the Enlightenment when he pointed to the earth and Mara's army fled. The page calls the common name "สะดุ้งมาร" a mistake (https://th.wikipedia.org/wiki/ปางมารวิชัย). It is the usual posture of a principal image.

**Correct example.** Right hand lying over the right knee, fingers pointing down, not gripping. Left hand palm-up in the lap. Right leg over left. Flame finial. A shoulder flap down to the navel. In the Lao style, fingers of equal length and the lotus base's petal tips curling out (https://seaarts.sac.or.th/artwork/391). **Seen:** exactly that, with a gold satin cloth over the left shoulder and lap, on a tiered gold base that carries lettering (a donor's inscription: not to be copied).

**Confidence** high. **Not determined:** its school and age.

## 4 · The green wheel and its standing Buddha (L12)

**Names.** ธรรมจักร (thammachak), Pali *dhammacakka*; English "Wheel of the Dhamma". Made as an offering craft it is a **บายศรีธรรมจักร** (bai sri thammachak), listed among today's kinds of bai sri in a university journal (https://so06.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/IVECJournal/article/download/280786/195366/1369846).

**What it means.** "ธรรมจักรเป็นสัญลักษณ์ของพระธรรมคำสอนแห่งองค์พระสัมมาสัมพุทธเจ้า", borrowed from the wheel-jewel of the universal monarch; a wheel has hub (ดุม), spokes (ซี่ / กำ) and rim (กง) (https://seaarts.sac.or.th/artwork/162). "the 8 spoked wheel represents the noble eightfold path" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharmachakra).

**Seen.** It is ribbon work, not a carving: an outer ruffle of pleated green satin edged with white pearl beads, a gold braid ring, a broad green satin rim, broad green spokes edged in gold braid, open between the spokes. I saw no red on it (the build sheet says red details). Before the hub stands a small gold Buddha, both arms down, the hands meeting low in front, with a pointed finial; incense sticks lean before it.

**The small Buddha.** Hands crossed before the thighs, right over left, is **ปางถวายเนตร** (pang thawai net), the Sunday image: standing, gazing at the Bodhi tree for seven days (https://th.wikipedia.org/wiki/ปางถวายเนตร). Medium confidence: the figure is about 60 pixels wide. The rival is ปางอุ้มบาตร (both hands holding a bowl at the waist); ปางรำพึง crosses the hands on the CHEST and is ruled out.

**Correct example.** Spokes in a meaningful number (8 here: count them on the model), a clear hub and rim. The Buddha stands straight, feet together, eyes open.

## 5 · The gold seated monk (L11)

**Names.** รูปหล่อ (rup lo, cast image) or รูปเหมือน (rup muean, likeness) of a revered monk: พระเกจิอาจารย์ or, in the north-east, ครูบาอาจารย์ / หลวงปู่. The word เกจิอาจารย์ is today used for teachers believed skilled in incantations and in consecrating amulets (belief; https://th.wikipedia.org/wiki/เกจิอาจารย์).

**What kinds stand on such altars.** National figures: Luang Pu Thuat (https://th.wikipedia.org/wiki/หลวงปู่ทวด), usually an aged, stooped monk seated in meditation; Somdet To of Wat Rakhang (his old photograph shows him seated with a palm-leaf book across his joined hands). Forest masters of the north-east such as Luang Ta Maha Bua of Udon Thani (statues and relics at Wat Phothisomphon, photograph saved). And the hall's own lineage teachers. Photographs of each type are saved.

**Seen.** A gilded statue of an elderly monk about 0.43 m: shaven head, **round spectacles modelled on the face**, robe over BOTH shoulders with a vertical band of pleats down the middle of the chest, hands low at the belly. The lap is hidden by offerings.

**Can it be identified? No.** No inscription is readable and many revered monks are cast in spectacles. The gilded Luang Ta Maha Bua statue I saved has no spectacles and a diagonal sash, so it does not match. Ask the owner who it is. The small WHITE figure before the green Buddha's throne is also a seated monk (the build sheet calls it a figure): not identifiable either.

## 6 · The printed backdrop

**The Palilayaka scene, for reference.** **ปางปาลิไลยก์** (pang palilai; commonly ป่าเลไลยก์, pa lelai): "ประทับนั่งบนก้อนศิลา พระบาททั้งสองวางอยู่บนดอกบัว พระหัตถ์ซ้ายวางคว่ำบนพระชานุ พระหัตถ์ขวาวางหงายบนพระชานุ มีรูปช้างหมอบใช้งวงจับกระบอกน้ำ และ อีกด้านหนึ่งมีลิงถือรวงผึ้งถวาย". When the monks of Kosambi quarrelled, the Buddha spent a rains retreat alone in the Palileyyaka forest, served by an elephant and a monkey; it is the birthday image of those born on Wednesday night (belief) (https://th.wikipedia.org/wiki/ปางปาลิไลยก์). The great example is Luang Pho To of Wat Pa Lelai, Suphan Buri (photographs saved).

**Is the backdrop that scene? I cannot confirm it, and I think not.** Seen, in four photographs with the contrast raised: the middle is only a Buddha FACE with closed eyes and curls, in mist, with heart-shaped leaves. At the right: a small figure with a round dark head reaching out with a dark object; a larger figure under a wide dark brim, cloth over the shoulders; and at the lower right an **old man's face**, dark cap or head-cloth, lined brow, moustache and beard. A dark diagonal stick above. At the lower left: a head between two raised hands with spread fingers. I can find no trunk, no tusk, no four-legged body, no rock seat and no Buddha with his feet down. Old bearded men in headdresses with a staff are how hermits (ฤๅษี, ruesi) are drawn, but that is a possibility, not a finding.

**Confidence** that it is Palilayaka: low. **Not determined:** what the side groups are. Ask the owner for a straight photograph of the banner or its file. Until then the stand-in is a Buddha face in mist with bodhi leaves and no figures.

## 7 · The orange fan (D7)

**Names.** **ตาลปัตร** (talapat; "palm-leaf fan"); this egg shape, wider at the top, is **พัดหน้านาง** (phat na nang); a fan made for an occasion is a **พัดรอง** (phat rong). It is not a พัดยศ (phat yot), the fan of rank the King gives with an ecclesiastical title.

**What it is and who uses it.** Monks only. "พระสงฆ์จะใช้ตาลปัตรเฉพาะเมื่อตอนให้ศีล ... และตอนอนุโมทนา (ให้พร)", and at funerals during the Abhidhamma chant; it began as a real palm-leaf fan; the phat rong was first made under King Mongkut of cloth over an egg-shaped bamboo frame and today means any fan made as a keepsake of a ceremony, royal, official or private (https://www.trueplookpanya.com/knowledge/content/61805-blo-, the Thai Junior Encyclopedia's text; https://saranukromthai.or.th/oldchild/2727; https://th.wikipedia.org/wiki/พัดรอง). It is a monk's implement: nothing I read has a layman holding one, and here it marks the place where invited monks chant.

**What such fans show.** The emblem of the occasion with its name and date in embroidery (https://th.wikipedia.org/wiki/ตาลปัตร). The ready-made kind sold everywhere shows a seated Buddha on a lotus between scroll borders: Thai Wikipedia's own example, "พัดรองที่มีการทำจำหน่ายโดยทั่วไป", is an ORANGE one of exactly the hall's kind (saved: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Talipot_fan_-_Wat_Hiranyawat_-_Chiang_Rai_-_2017-01-02_-_003.jpg, CC0).

**Seen.** Orange lattice-patterned cloth, a dark blue kanok scroll arc at the top and a larger one at the bottom with a lotus at its middle, a colour picture of a seated Buddha (orange robe, ringed halo, pink lotus seat), right hand raised at the chest, a copper ferrule and a pale pole. **Correct example:** egg shape wider at the top, braid round the edge, one centred motif, scroll arcs top and bottom. Leave the lettering off.

## 9 · The bai sri

**Names and meaning.** **บายศรี** (bai sri): Khmer *bai*, rice, with *sri*, auspiciousness: "ข้าวอันเป็นสิริ". A tiered cone of folded leaf used to call back and strengthen a person's *khwan* (belief) and as an offering (https://th.wikipedia.org/wiki/พิธีบายศรีสู่ขวัญ). The big tree form is believed to model Mount Meru (belief; https://thailandfoundation.or.th/all-wrapped-up-the-cultural-side-of-flowers-and-banana-leaves-in-thailand/). Isan kinds: พาขวัญ, พาบายศรี, หมากเบ็ง (Thai Wikipedia page above).

**Where each belongs.** The small บายศรีปากชาม is for household rites; the big บายศรีต้น is "เครื่องบูชาเทพยดา ... สมโภชในการฉลองพระพุทธรูป ... นอกจากนี้ยังใช้ในการไหว้ครูอีกด้วย" (https://saranukromthai.or.th/oldchild/2104). So a hall dressed for a Wai Khru is where the big ones belong.

- **Gold-foil cones with a crown of gold fronds, in silver bowls (D10).** By form a **บายศรีเทพ** (bai sri thep): main fingers to four or five sides, "กรวยตั้งอยู่ตรงกลาง บรรจุอยู่ในพานหรือภาชนะอื่น ๆ ทำจำนวน 1 ชั้น", set out in pairs in offering rites (IVEC journal article, §4's link). Seen: five or six tall feather-shaped fronds of folded gold foil, every fold tipped with a white bead pin, a slim gold cone with a white flower ring, a gold lotus-bud finial, yellow flowers round the mouth of a mirror-silver bowl on its own stand. Confidence medium; a trade name for the gold-foil kind was not found.
- **Brown plaited ones on gold bowls (D12, L13).** The same forms in a dark brown material with white bead tips. In one photograph devotees wash them in basins, so it is not fresh banana leaf. Material and name not determined ("dried leaf" is my description).
- **The peacock of banknotes (D11).** A **นกยูงกฐิน** (nok yung kathin), in the fanned form นกยูงรำแพน: a money peacock of the family of money trees and bushes (ต้นเงิน, พุ่มกฐิน, พุ่มผ้าป่า) carried in kathin and pha pa merit-making. My only sources are temple-supply sellers, who list "นกยูง" under gear for fixing money to kathin bushes, in two forms, รำแพน and ทรงพุ่ม (https://www.saphanboon109.com/products_detail/view/7304089): weak. Is it of this region? Not shown: it is sold nationwide and I found no study tying it to Isan or Laos. Why a peacock: no source, so none given. Seen: tail of folded notes rimmed with red, green and yellow gems in gold settings, gold cone neck, white crested head, on a lotus of folded notes. The small cones of §8 and the slim cones on the lotus ledge are the same family.

## 10 · The cord (D19)

**Names.** **สายสิญจน์** (sai sin); English "sacred thread"; also ด้ายปริตร.

**What it is.** "เส้นด้ายสีขาวยาว ๆ ที่พระถือขณะประนมมือเจริญพระพุทธมนต์ หรือที่วงรอบบ้านเรือน": a white thread wound clockwise round the house and round the base of the Buddha image, then laid by the senior monk who leads the chanting; believed to protect (belief) (https://th.wikipedia.org/wiki/สายสิญจน์, citing the dictionary คำวัด). In Isan the wrist-tying thread of a bai sri rite is "ด้ายดิบสายสิญจน์", raw cotton (https://saranukromthai.or.th/oldchild/2101).

**Seen.** A thin grey-white thread of several strands wound four or five turns round the base of the raised hand's fingers, then running in one sagging line to the east wall by the exhaust fan. It reads dark only where it crosses the gold cloth and the terracotta wall. **Correct example:** white raw cotton, one unbroken line (photograph saved of threads strung from a principal Buddha across a hall). **Not determined:** where it goes beyond the wall.
