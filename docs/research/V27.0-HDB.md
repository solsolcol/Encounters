# v27.0 · Episode 4 chapter 4 · research note R-C: the HDB flat

Research note for episode 4 chapter 4 (key `e4c4`): milestone M1, item R-C of docs/V27.0-E4C4-BUILD.md, written for
the plan's §7 (the place: her flat). Written 17 Nov 2026. Nothing in the game or the plan was changed by this note.

**The job:** the whole chapter plays inside one real-looking Singapore HDB flat at night (and its corridor), owned by an
elderly Singaporean Indian (Tamil Hindu) widow who has used a wheelchair for years, with her adult family present. The
flat is to be built to true scale, so this note gives numbers wherever a source gives them, and says plainly where it
does not.

**How it was done.**
- Every source is numbered (§ Sources, at the end). Every source cited by number was **opened** and read, unless it is
  marked **(index)**: those were read only through the search engine's index because the page sat behind a bot check,
  had moved, or refused (403/404). An (index) source never carries a build number on its own.
- HDB's own resale-transaction register (data.gov.sg) was downloaded and counted for floor areas [1]. Six real floor
  plans (HDB's 1:100 drawings, as collected and captioned at housingmap.sg) were read for room sizes [3]–[8].
- **Labels.** **SOURCED**: stated by an opened source. **DATA**: counted by me from the register [1]. **SCALED**:
  measured by me off a 1:100 drawing (± 0.05 m). **DERIVED**: arithmetic on sourced numbers. **UNSURE**: sources
  disagree, or only an (index) source says it. **DEFAULT**: no source found; a choice for the build, to be checked
  against the reference photographs (§6) before it is modelled.
- Bot walls and dead links met and **not** worked around: 99.co (403), cos.sg (403), NUS FASS (Incapsula check), the
  Wikimedia API (rate limit: stopped, then used the ordinary file pages, six downloads 15 s apart), MND newsroom pages
  (render empty without script), several HDB InfoWEB and BCA document links (moved: 404 or a web page instead of the
  PDF), NLB Infopedia (article URL now redirects to "not found").
- **The repository is public.** No private person is named or pictured here. The six reference photographs (§6) and the
  six plan images are on the working machine only, in the scratch folder, and are **not committed**. The plan images
  are the collector's copyrighted scans (he asks that they not be copied): they were used only to read dimensions.

## 0 · The short answer

- **Which flat.** An owner who has lived in her flat "for decades" most likely has a **4-room Model A** of 1985–1996:
  49 % of the 4-room flats of 1985–89 and 94 % of those of 1990–94 that changed hands since 2017 are Model A, median
  floor area **104 m²** (p10 103, p90 108–110) [1] (DATA). The 1980s alternatives are the New Generation (92 m²) and the
  Simplified (84 m²) [1][2]. A 5-room of the same years is a 5-room Improved, median **121–124 m²** [1].
- **The plan to build (Plan A):** HDB's 4-Room Model A **corridor unit, built 1993–96, 107 m²** [3]: a frontage of
  **10.20 m** on the corridor and a depth of **10.30 m**; the main door opens straight into the living room; three
  bedrooms (the main one with its own bath/WC), a common bath/WC off the kitchen, a long kitchen/dining room, a
  **1.9 × 1.0 m store**, and an open **"future room"** beside the living room. One bedroom's window looks onto the
  corridor. Full room list and coordinates: the BUILD SHEET.
- **Heights.** Floor to floor **2.8 m** in HDB blocks (3.6 m from the 1st to the 2nd storey) [9]; finished ceiling
  "about 2.6 m" in most flats, with one source giving **2.4–2.5 m** for pre-1990s resale flats [10] (UNSURE). Build at
  **2.55 m** (DEFAULT).
- **The door.** A single timber leaf in an opening of about **1.0 m** (SCALED [3][7]), swinging in; a **two-leaf steel
  grille gate** swinging out over the corridor [3][7]; every plan letters **"100 DROP"** at the main door: the flat
  floor is **100 mm** above the corridor [3]–[8]. Internal doors **0.8–0.9 m**, bathroom doors **≈ 0.75 m** (SCALED [3]).
- **No bomb shelter in a 1980s–early-1990s flat.** Since **1998** every new home has needed a shelter of its own (or a
  shared one on each storey) [12]; earlier flats have a **store** instead [3]–[7]. If the chapter wants the steel
  shelter door, the flat must be a 1997–2000 type, which puts it **beside the main door** [8]: opening
  **700 × 1900 mm**, walls **250 mm**, floor area **2.8 m²**, clear height ≥ **2.4 m** [11].
- **The corridor** (HDB's drawings call it the **access balcony**) is **1.6–1.8 m** wide [3][5][7]; SCDF lets shoe racks
  and (where the Town Council allows) potted plants stand along it, but **1.2 m** must stay clear from the parapet to the
  rack [19]. Corridor tubes have been changed to LED since 2011 [23]; LTA's orange street lamps were all but gone by
  early 2023 (white LED, **3000 K** on minor roads) [24].
- **Pre-1989 flats have the rubbish chute inside the flat, in the kitchen** [20][2][4][5][6]; later blocks have shared
  chutes [2][20]. Laundry went out of the kitchen window on poles in pipe sockets [51]; HDB's upgrading (HIP) replaces
  the sockets with a retractable rack of six stainless poles [14][16].
- **A Tamil Hindu home** (§3): the prayer place in the living room (a wall shelf, a nook or a cabinet) [32]; a brass
  **kuthu vilakku** [30]; deity pictures; **photographs of the departed among the gods** ("typical for a Tamil-Hindu
  household") [28]; garlands of jasmine and other fragrant flowers [27]; a **kolam** at the door, fresh daily in some
  homes and permanent or pre-made in others [26]; a **thoranam** of mango leaves over the door on special occasions [27].
- **Her wheelchair** (§4): an occupied chair needs **750 × 1200 mm** and **1500 mm** to turn in a confined space [17]; a
  standard steel chair is **65 cm** wide, **105 cm** long, 46 cm seat, 24-inch rear wheels [39]. HDB's EASE fittings:
  grab bars (8 or 10 in the first bathroom, 6 in the second), slip-resistant floors, lowered and widened bathroom
  entrances, a foldable shower seat, single-step / portable / customised ramps, rocker switches [15].
- **Night sound** (§5): fans and the fridge (fridges are 17 % of a home's electricity, air-conditioners 24 %) [40];
  the house gecko's "chik, chik, chiky", loudest at sunset and before dawn [45]; the large-tailed nightjar's
  "chonk-chonk-chonk" [42]; the koel's "ku-oo" from dawn [43]; the last trains around **23:30–00:40** and the first
  around **05:20** [41].

Simplified 1984–88 "usually 84 sqm"; 4-Room Model A 1982–1990s "usually 105-108 sqm", two full-size toilets; 4-Room

room big enough to build a 5th room" [2].

**Storeys.** Of the 17,198 4-room Model A flats of 1985–96 sold since 2017, 95 % were on storeys 1–12, 3.6 % on storeys
13–15 and 0.3 % above the 18th [1] (DATA): blocks of about 12–13 storeys are the norm for this flat.

### 1.4 Heights

- **Storey height:** "typical floor-to-floor height shall be 2.8m"; "3.6m from 1st to 2nd storey"; the topmost floor to
  the roof's lowest point is also 2.8 m [9] (SOURCED: BCA's 2014 guide for HDB's precast designs). A storey-n floor
  therefore stands 3.6 + 2.8 × (n − 2) m above the ground floor: the 8th storey at 20.4 m, the 10th at 26.0 m (DERIVED).
- **Finished ceiling:** "In most HDB flats, the standard ceiling height is about 2.6 metres"; the same article's table
  gives "Older resale flats (pre-1990s) | 2.4m - 2.5m" and "Newer HDB and BTO flats | 2.6m"; a false ceiling must keep
  2.4 m clear [10] (UNSURE: a single renovation magazine; other blogs say 2.6 m or more). **Build: 2.55 m** (DEFAULT, inside
  both readings). Downstand beams are not drawn on the plans: DEFAULT 0.3 m along structural walls.
- **Corridor:** the flat floor is 100 mm above the corridor (§1.5), so the corridor's soffit is about 0.1 m higher above
  its own floor than the flat's ceiling is above the flat's floor (DERIVED).

### 1.5 Doors, the gate and the step at the door

- **Main door:** a single leaf swinging into the flat, in an opening of **0.99–1.06 m** (SCALED on [3] and [7]); leaf
  ≈ 0.9 m (DERIVED); in Plan A it is hinged on the side nearer bedroom 3, so it opens against that wall [3]. HDB's
  upgrading offers "Laminated timber doors ... in place of veneer doors" [16]: the original is a timber veneer door.
  Height: DEFAULT 2.1 m.
- **The gate:** every corridor plan draws a **pair of gate leaves swinging out over the corridor** in front of the door
  [3][7] (SOURCED). HIP's replacement is "a steel grille gate with an interior thumb-turn knob", "replacing the current
  mild steel gate" [16]. The corridor photograph [47] shows a gate with scrolled ironwork.
- **The step:** "100 DROP" is lettered at the main door in all six plans [3]–[8]. BCA's guide for current HDB designs
  speaks of a "50mm drop from 2nd storey DU [dwelling unit] floor level to 2nd storey common corridor" [9], which fixes
  the side that is lower: **the corridor is 100 mm below the flat** in these older flats (DERIVED). New flats since
  2025 must keep it ≤ 50 mm [17].
- **Internal doors:** bedroom and store doors **0.8–0.9 m**, bathroom doors **≈ 0.75 m** (SCALED [3]). The 1980s plans
  also letter "100 DROP" at bathroom doors (and at the kitchen in [4]): the wet floors were set lower [4][5] (SOURCED).
  The 1990s plans letter the drop only at the main door (and the balcony) [3][7][8].
- For comparison, new flats: entrance door ≥ 850 mm clear, ≤ 50 mm level change; the bathroom for future retrofitting
  ≤ 25 mm at its doorway and a 1000 mm route to it [17].

### 1.6 Windows

- **Types:** of 35 windows that fell from homes in Jan–Nov 2023, 19 were casement, 15 sliding, 1 louvre; "Since 2004,
  BCA's has mandated homeowners to replace all aluminum rivets in casement windows with stainless steel ones" [18].
- **What the photographs show:** aluminium-framed **louvre** windows on the corridor side of an older block [47];
  outward-opening **casements** above the laundry poles on the back facade [51].
- **Sizes in Plan A** (SCALED [3]): bedroom 3 (corridor) ≈ 2.45 m wide; living room (corridor) ≈ 2.3 m; future room
  (corridor) ≈ 3.4 m; bedroom 2 (back) ≈ 2.3 m; main bedroom (back) ≈ 1.8 m; kitchen casements (back) ≈ 1.8 m, set in a
  frame projecting ≈ 0.7 m; small bath windows high in the projecting bath block.
- Sill and head heights are on no source found: DEFAULT sill 0.9 m and head 2.1 m for rooms, sill 1.4 m for the baths
  and any corridor-side kitchen louvres. Window grilles fitted by owners are common in practice, but no source was
  opened for it (DEFAULT).

### 1.7 Floors

- "In the past, terrazzo tiles were everywhere, from living rooms to bedrooms" [35] (SOURCED, a nostalgia article).
- The National Museum's 1970s–80s HDB kitchen has a **small hexagonal mosaic floor** and patterned wall tiles; its
  living room a parquet-pattern floor [50][49] (observed in the photographs).
- HIP's upgraded bathrooms have "larger wall and floor tiles" and dual-flush WCs [16].
- What finish HDB itself laid in 1990s 4-room flats was **not found** (UNSURE). **Build (DEFAULT):** living, dining,
  kitchen and passage in 300 × 300 mm off-white ceramic tiles (an owner's 1990s renovation, worn); bedrooms in timber
  parquet, worn at the doors; bathrooms in HIP's larger tiles (if HIP is taken as done, §1.11).

### 1.8 Light, fans, power

- "If you buy an old resale flat, there's a high chance that the existing lighting system is made for fluorescent
  bulbs"; "Most HDBs typically come with flush mount lighting" (a single central fitting) [36] (SOURCED).
- **Fans:** a ceiling fan's blades should be at least about 2.1 m above the floor, so ceilings under 2.7 m take a hugger
  (flush) fan [S8] (UNSURE, a retailer). With a 2.55 m ceiling the fan body may hang ≤ 0.45 m (DERIVED). DEFAULT: one
  hugger fan of 1.2 m (48") sweep in the living room, the future room and each bedroom.
- **Switches:** new flats put switches and sockets 450–1200 mm above the floor to their centreline [17]; AIC advises
  switches "approximately 100 cm from the floor", in a colour that contrasts with the wall, for wheelchair users [37];
  EASE 2.0 offers rocker switches, a set of 12 [15] (so the originals are small rocker or toggle switches: DEFAULT).
- **Power:** HIP's essential items include "Upgrading of electrical load" [14]. Sockets: Singapore uses the UK-style
  three-pin 13 A socket at 230 V (DEFAULT: common knowledge, no page opened).

### 1.9 Store, or household shelter

- **Before 1998: a store.** Plan A's store is 1.9 × 1.0 m [3]; others 1.65 × 1.3 m [5], 1.2 m deep [4], 1.7 m deep [6],
  3.0 × 1.2 m [7]. A plain timber door. In the 1970s the store sat "almost always ... near the kitchen"; in the 1980s it
  "is starting to drift around the unit" [46].
- **Since 1998: a household shelter (HS) in every flat** (or a shelter on each storey): "since 1998, we have required all
  residential developments to have either a shelter in every household unit ... or a common shelter on each storey or
  within the staircase of a building" [12]. HDB: the HS "is fitted with a steel door, along with strengthened floor,
  walls and ceiling to protect occupants in the event of a wartime emergency" [13]. In the 1998–2000 plan it is the
  "STORE/PANTRY (APT. SHELTER)" next to the main door [8].
- **The shelter's numbers** (SCDF's 2023 requirements for new shelters [11]; a 1998 shelter was built to the rules of its
  day, not checked):
  - floor area for a home of 75–140 m² gross: **2.8 m²**, volume 7.2 m³; minimum internal width **1200 mm**; maximum
    internal length 4000 mm; sizes in 50 mm steps;
  - clear height **2400–3900 mm**;
  - walls **250 mm** for storey heights ≤ 4 m, clear height 2.4–3.4 m and setback ≤ 6 m (200–300 mm in other cases);
  - **door opening 700 mm wide × 1900 mm high**, a steel door of an approved design in a frame with one or two rebates,
    a concrete nib of at least 150 mm beside the frame; a lever handle (the door's test cycles turn it 90°); an "HS door
    notice" fixed on the door's **inside** face;
  - ventilation-sleeve openings with their centres 1900–3600 mm above the floor.

### 1.10 The kitchen: the chute, the sink, the laundry

- **The rubbish chute:** "For HDB flats built before 1989, rubbish disposal chutes are installed within each flat";
  newer flats have common chutes on every floor, and the rubbish falls to bins on the ground floor [20]. The 1980s plans
  draw the chute as a crossed square in the kitchen's corner, about 1.0 m square overall with its walls [4][5][6]
  (SCALED); the early-1990s types have a centralised chute [2]. HIP offers a "New refuse chute hopper" [14]. In Plan A
  (1993–96) there is **no** chute in the kitchen; in the Simplified variant [5] there is.
- **The sink:** Plan A draws a double sink against the front of bath/WC 2 [3].
- **The laundry:** laundry dried on **poles pushed out of the kitchen window into pipe sockets** [51]. HIP's essential
  items include "Replacement of pipe sockets with new clothes drying rack" [14]: "six horizontal stainless steel poles
  installed externally", which "can be extended and retracted within the frame of the rack", reached from the kitchen
  window [16]. Plan A has no separate service yard: its laundry goes out of the kitchen window. The 1998–2000 plan has a
  2.5 × 1.8 m service balcony [8].
- The museum's 1970s–80s HDB kitchen: a full wall of cabinets in orange, white worktops, patterned wall tiles, a gas
  cooker with an oven, a rice cooker, a blender, a toaster oven, a fridge, a twin-tub washing machine [50]; "the penchant
  to fill an entire wall with cabinets" is one of the 1980s home's marks [34].

### 1.11 What an old flat has had done to it by 2026

- **HIP** (Home Improvement Programme): first for flats built up to 1986 (about 320,000 flats); "In 2018, the programme
  was extended to another 230,000 flats built between 1987 and 1997". Essential (free to citizens): repair of spalling
  concrete; new waste/soil stacks; pipe sockets replaced by the drying rack; electrical load upgraded. Optional (the
  owner pays 7.5 % for a 4-room, $899.25 for the full package): upgraded toilet(s)/bathroom(s), **new entrance door and
  grille gate**, new refuse chute hopper [14]. So a 1993–96 flat may or may not have had HIP by 2026 (UNSURE: not every
  block has been called yet).
- **EASE** (Enhancement for Active Seniors): "launched in July 2012"; ramps for older flats with multi-step entrances
  added in December 2018; "EASE 2.0" from April 2024; offered together with HIP, or by direct application to HDB [15].
  Its items are in §4.
- **Lifts:** older lifts "only stopped at certain floors" (odd or even floors, or every third floor); the Lift Upgrading
  Programme changes them to stop at every floor [22] (SOURCED, but Wikipedia, tagged as needing citations). The 1991–96
  blocks were built with lifts stopping at every floor [2].

## 2 · Outside the door

### 2.1 The corridor (HDB's "access balcony")

- **Width:** 1800 mm lettered on [7]; about 1.6–1.7 m scaled on [3] and [5] (SOURCED / SCALED). **Build: 1.75 m clear.**
- **What may stand in it:** SCDF allows shoe racks "for the sole purpose of keeping shoes", foldable clothing racks that
  "can be easily folded or removed in times of emergency", and potted plants if the Town Council allows them; "A minimum
  clear escape passage of 1.2m clearance is to be maintained along the common corridor ... (i.e. from the parapet wall to
  the shoe or movable clothing rack)" [19]. On a 1.75 m corridor that leaves **≤ 0.55 m** for racks, pots and ramps on
  the flat side (DERIVED).
- **Parapet:** BCA requires safety barriers to be at least **1.0 m** high (Clause H.3.2.1) [S1] (UNSURE: read only via
  the index; BCA's links have moved). The corridor photograph [47] shows a solid parapet with a flat ledge on top, the
  sky and other blocks beyond.
- **Corridor lights:** in 2011 the Town Councils began changing "some 340,000 fluorescent lights in corridors and
  staircases" in 2,000 blocks to LED [23] (SOURCED); since late 2024 they have been fitting "smart" LEDs that brighten on
  motion [S2] (UNSURE). DEFAULT: a 1.2 m LED batten on the soffit every 6–8 m, cool white, on all night.
- **Unit number:** "Property owners are required to display the house/unit number prominently"; an address reads
  "[House Number] [Street Name] [# Unit Number, if any] Singapore [Postal Code]" [25]. DEFAULT plate: "#08-1xx" (storey,
  then unit) on the wall beside the door.

## 3 · A Singaporean Tamil Hindu family's home

The things below are the ones the sources name. "How often" is what the source says, or my DEFAULT where none does. The
altar of Mae Kali herself, her pictures and her lamp, belong to research note R-A (docs/research/V27.0-KALI.md), not here.

### 3.1 The prayer place

- **Where:** retail guides for HDB flats put the altar in "a multi-purpose zone like the living or dining room", "a
  recessed wall nook or a small corner between a window and a TV console", in corner floor cabinets or wall-mounted
  floating units, and mention "underutilized nooks near household shelters" [32] (a retailer's guide). A whole prayer
  room is the exception in a 4-room flat (DEFAULT); Plan A's store (1.9 × 1.0 m) is the obvious one to become a prayer
  room (DEFAULT, no source).
- **The lamp:** the **kuthu vilakku** "stands on a base with a long, thin stem"; the lamp "has space for five wicks, with
  the oil poured in the centre"; a finial rises from the centre (here an annapakshi, a swan-like bird); "often lit for
  auspicious events and festivals"; regarded as a family heirloom [30]. Size not given: DEFAULT 0.40–0.45 m tall, brass.
  Small oil lamps (vilakku) and incense (agarbathi) are lit at the family's prayers [29].
- **The pictures:** deity portraits in the calendar-art tradition, "Sami Padam", painted for Sivakasi's printers and
  "used for marketing in textile and jewellery shops" [31] (an Indian source; no Singapore source found for shop
  calendars: UNSURE). Singapore research on everyday Hindu practice finds home shrines that mix images from several
  traditions ("Ganesha, Jesus Christ, and the Buddha") [33] (UNSURE: index only, behind a bot check).
- **Garlands:** "Garlands of various flowers and leaves are offered to deities and adorn their statues or images";
  "popular fragrant varieties would include jasmine, rose, tuberose, and chrysanthemum"; also tulasi (holy basil) for
  Krishna and Vishnu, vilvam for Shiva, arugampul for Ganesha, neem leaves for Mariamman; garland makers work in Little
  India and "in heartland neighbourhoods across the island" [27].

### 3.2 At the door

- **Kolam** (rangoli; "It is known as kolam in Tamil"): "fresh rangoli may be drawn every day at the home's entrance,
  with the old design cleaned off and replaced with a new one. Some households may also have permanent rangoli drawn";
  "usually drawn at the entrance of households", "usually made by females in the family"; "Some traditional rangoli are
  drawn using rice flour"; the pattern "is made of unbroken lines", thought to keep evil spirits from passing through the
  gaps; prominent at Deepavali; "many might prefer using pre-made rangoli designs over the traditional methods" [26].
  **For an elderly widow in a wheelchair at night:** a white rice-flour kolam on the corridor floor before the gate,
  drawn that morning by a daughter or daughter-in-law, partly scuffed by feet (DEFAULT), or a permanent one.
- **Thoranam:** "On special occasions, the frame of the main entrance of homes may be decorated with a thoranam (a
  garland usually comprising mango leaves and flowers) as a symbol to welcome auspiciousness" [27]. A plastic thoranam
  left up all year is common in practice, but no Singapore source was opened for it (DEFAULT).
- **Shoes:** a shoe rack and slippers outside the gate (SCDF allows the rack [19]; shown in [47]).

### 3.3 The departed

- "It is typical for a Tamil-Hindu household to have photos of the deceased in their home shrine, among photos of Hindu
  Gods"; the family puts "flower petals on the photo or stone" to honour them [28] (SOURCED, a Tamil diaspora essay; not
  Singapore-specific).
- In Singapore Tamil families, the yearly **padayal** (usually "within a week of Deepavali"): a banana leaf is laid out
  with dishes; the family gathers "around the photographs" of the departed; drinks are poured into glass jars in front of
  them; "The agarbathi is burning, the vizhakkus have been lit" [29].
- The material of the garland on the photograph (fresh flowers, sandalwood, plastic) and the mark on its glass are on no
  source opened (DEFAULT: a framed photograph of her husband with a fading fresh garland and a dot of kumkum).

### 3.4 The rest of the flat

- **A 1970s–80s HDB living room** (the National Museum's) [49]: a wooden-framed sofa set (a three-seater and armchairs)
  with olive-green cushions on curved arms, a wooden coffee table with a slatted shelf under its top, a parquet-pattern
  floor, orange walls.
- **Marks of an 80s Singapore home** [34]: "seashell curtains" (their "click-clacking sound"), cornices, a wall of
  kitchen cabinets, a bar counter, "zippered plastic sheet wardrobes", a wooden fork-and-spoon wall hanging,
  multi-tiered computer tables, "Good morning towels", Tupperware, "a small table dedicated to holding the corded
  telephone".
- Display cabinet with glass doors, plastic stackable chairs for visitors, the TV console, a dining table, a sewing
  machine, a mosquito net: **no source opened** for how common each is in Tamil HDB homes (DEFAULT; the build sheet
  marks which ones it uses).

## 4 · Life in a wheelchair in the flat

### 4.1 The chair

- BCA's anthropometrics [17]: one stationary wheelchair and its occupant need **750 × 1200 mm**; knee/lap height 700 mm,
  toe 230 mm; to turn freely **1800 mm**, to turn in a confined space **1500 mm**; to travel straight 900 × 1200 mm; to
  manoeuvre into a side transfer 900 × 1500 mm.
- Kinds [38]: a **self-propelled** chair has "large rear wheels fitted with push-rims" and "can weigh up to 20kg"; a
  **pushchair** (a transit chair) has "smaller rear wheels and require[s] a caregiver to push", "lighter at 10kg to 15kg".
- A **standard steel chair** (a regional dealer's specification) [39]: total width **65 cm** (30 cm folded), seat 46 cm
  wide and 40 cm deep, backrest 46 cm, total height 90 cm, total length **105 cm**, 8-inch front castors, 24-inch rear
  "magwheels", 115 kg capacity, described as "commonly used in hospitals and other institutions".
- **Build (DEFAULT):** her chair is that steel self-propelled chair, chrome frame, black upholstery, push handles (her
  family pushes it), worn arm pads.

### 4.2 Levels and ramps

- Her flat's levels (§1.5): **100 mm** down to the corridor at the main door; in a 1980s flat also at the bathroom doors.
- BCA's gradients for a change of level [17]: rise 0–15 mm at 1:2; 15–50 mm at 1:5; **50–175 mm at 1:10**; over 175 mm at
  1:12. A 100 mm step at 1:10 needs a ramp **1.0 m** long (DERIVED), which a 1.75 m corridor cannot take while keeping
  1.2 m clear [19]: so the real answer is the steeper portable or single-step ramp HDB supplies (DERIVED).
- **EASE ramps** [15]: "1 single-step ramp" (4-room owner pays $21.25), "1 portable ramp" ($57.20), "1 customised ramp for
  2 to 3 steps" ($130.80), for more than 3 steps ($327.00), "1 wheelchair lifter" ($449.60); added for multi-step
  entrances in December 2018. A customised ramp is said to need 1.2 m of corridor left clear after it is fitted [S6]
  (UNSURE). **Build (DEFAULT):** an aluminium single-step ramp 0.9 m wide and 0.5 m deep outside the gate, its edge
  0.05 m inside the 0.55 m the corridor allows.

### 4.3 The bathroom

- **EASE items and a 4-room owner's share** [15]: grab bars, first toilet ("1st set – 8 or 10 grab bars") $39.20, second
  toilet ("2nd set – 6 grab bars") $22.85; slip-resistant treatment (2 toilets) $35.95; lowering of the toilet/bathroom
  entrance kerb $71.90; widening the entrance $98.10; both $116.05; a wall-mounted foldable shower seat $22.85; a handrail
  at a main entrance with steps $47.40; bidet spray (2 sets) $9.80; rocker switches (set of 12) $20.40; a Home Fire Alarm
  Device $9.00. The Government pays up to 95 % [15].
- Grab bars: **32–45 mm** across, at least **40 mm** clear of the wall [17]. AIC: "Add grab bars", "Consider using a shower
  chair and portable shower head", non-slip mats or treatment on toilet floors [37].
- **Doors against the chair:** Plan A's bathroom doors (≈ 0.75 m) pass a 0.65 m chair with 50 mm each side; bedroom doors
  (0.8–0.9 m) pass more easily; but each bathroom is entered off a room (bath 1 from the main bedroom, bath 2 from the
  kitchen), so the turn in front of the door is what limits her (DERIVED from [3][39][17]).

### 4.4 Arranging the flat

- AIC [37]: "Arrange furniture so that it is easy and safe for your loved one to move about"; "Keep the area free from
  clutter"; "Remove rugs and wires on the floor"; "Install night lights between bed area and bathroom"; mark steps with
  fluorescent tape; switches "approximately 100 cm from the floor" in a contrasting colour; "Widening of doorways";
  "Installation of ramps".
- **Build (DEFAULT, from the above):** a clear lane ≥ 0.9 m from the gate through the living room and the passage to the
  kitchen and bath/WC 2; the sofa set pushed against the walls; a turning circle of 1.5 m kept in front of the altar and
  in the future room; **she sleeps in the future room** (beside the living room, one straight run from the door, nearest
  the common bath), walled off with a curtain or a folding partition, her bed with a bed rail, a commode chair and a
  night light, rather than in a bedroom behind two turns.
