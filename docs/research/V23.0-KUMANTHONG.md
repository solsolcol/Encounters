# v23.0 · Kumanthong (กุมารทอง) — what the sources say

Research note for episode 3 chapter 5, *The Children On My Altar* (docs/V23.0-E3C5-PLAN.md). Written 25 Oct 2026,
against the plan at **rev 4** (commit d4b3592, "the promise for life, and the chapter's purpose"). Nothing in the game
or the plan was changed by this note.

Chad's purpose for the chapter, verbatim: *"The purpose of this chapter is to clear misconceptions about kumanthongs
being yin baby spirits like in other traditions. Kumanthongs made by monks are different, and made out of compassion
and giving them an opportunity to help their owners while receiving merits to get a better rebirth. By using
emotional storytelling, clear the misconceptions, but also emphasize what the owners should know about long term care
and commitment, as well as constant merits. Do not let greed and desire be the reason to keep kumanthongs, that is
wrong. The kumanthongs also work within the bounds of buddhist precepts, and should not be used for evil purposes."*

The brief for this note was to report what the sources say, **including where they differ from that framing or from
each other** — not to confirm it. Where they differ, it is said plainly.

**How it was done.** Thai, English and Chinese sources were read in the original: two academic studies (Megan
Sinnott, Cambridge, 2014, in full; Krisda Mekakad's Chiang Mai University thesis, 2017 — its abstract and chapters
2 to 4), Chris Baker and Pasuk Phongpaichit on
*Khun Chang Khun Phaen*, the Pali canon where it bears on the question, Thai news (Thai PBS, Thairath, Matichon, MGR
Online, Naewna, Post Today, Daily News, Kom Chad Luek, Sanook, Kapook), two monastic sites, Thai care guides and
seller pages (labelled as such), and Chinese-language media from Taiwan, Hong Kong, Malaysia, Singapore and the
mainland. **Every URL below was opened**; pages that could not be opened are listed in §12 and are cited for nothing.

**Labels.**
- **[HISTORY/FACT]** — documented: a date, a person, a text, a dictionary entry, a news event, a survey.
- **[BELIEF]** — what devotees, keepers, sellers or teachers believe. True as a report of belief; not proven.
- **[CONTESTED]** — the sources disagree with each other (or with the plan).
- **[UNCONFIRMED]** — I could not find it in any source I opened.

Source types are marked where it matters: *(scholarly)*, *(canon)*, *(news)*, *(monastic)*, *(seller)*, *(popular
explainer)*.

## 0 · The short answer

1. **What it is.** A *kumanthong* (กุมารทอง, "golden boy") is a small figure believed to house the spirit of a child,
   kept like a child of the family — fed, spoken to, given toys — in return for protection, help with trade, luck and
   guarding the house. Thai sources file it under folk magic (ไสยศาสตร์), not Buddhist doctrine.
2. **Where the frightening picture comes from.** A literary episode in *Khun Chang Khun Phaen* (a foetus cut from a
   murdered mother and roasted — a 19th-century addition to the written poem), the old "black magic" recipe from
   foetal remains, news of trafficked foetuses (Bangkok 2012, Rayong 2018, Ang Thong 2026), and Chinese-language
   "raising little ghosts" (養小鬼 / 養鬼仔) stories in Malaysia, Singapore, Taiwan and Hong Kong: blood feeding,
   jealousy, revenge, "backlash" (反噬) if you stop.
3. **Monk-made kumanthong are real and well documented** (Luang Phor Tae of Wat Sam Ngam from about 1942, his disciple
   Luang Pu Yaem, many others). **But the documented purpose of Luang Phor Tae's was protection**, and they were
   moulded from soil that included earth from seven cemeteries. The account that monks make them *out of compassion,
   to give child spirits a home and a chance at merit* is a **belief** held by some devotees, shop owners, sellers,
   some monks and many Chinese-language explainers. It is **not** the documented historical origin.
4. **Chad's "help their owners while receiving merit toward a better birth"** is attested, very closely, as
   devotee belief: the child earns merit by helping, the keeper earns merit by caring (Sinnott: "a central
   rationale"). It is belief, not doctrine — one Thai guide says outright that no Buddhist criterion can confirm it.
5. **"They keep the precepts" appears in no source I found.** What the sources do say: keepers must never ask a
   kumanthong to harm or control anyone or for anything illegal; the "deity" kind is believed to dislike wrongdoing;
   a kumanthong grows like the person who raises it. There is also old lore of kumanthong made specifically to kill.
6. **Care.** The rule the Thai guides state most firmly is **plain water, never allowed to run out**. Then a sweet red
   drink (with a straw is popular), sweets, toys; call them to eat; talk to them; keep every promise; place them
   lower than the Buddha; special offerings on วันพระ (the weekly holy day). **The number of incense sticks is
   disputed — one Wat Sam Ngam guide says never one stick; another guide says one stick for a child's spirit.**
7. **Commitment.** Disputed. Several Chinese-language sources say it is for life and that stopping brings retribution. Thai
   sources mostly give respectful ways to end it — return the figure to the temple or shop, a release rite — and say
   never to dump one. Dumping at temples is documented (2016: over a thousand unwanted kuman at one temple).
8. **Buddhist critics.** A Dhammakaya monk calls kumanthong *sambhavesi* (beings seeking rebirth) and says keeping
   them does more harm than good; the national Buddhism office says the Sangha forbids monks to consecrate charms that
   breed superstition; the 2016 "child angel doll" (ลูกเทพ) craze ended with dolls dumped at temples and a famous monk
   calling it "belief trash".
9. **The plan holds up better than most fiction would.** The three children's backstories, the red drinks and toys,
   the danger of being possessed, the shop that takes them back — all have real parallels (§10). The main fixes: do
   not deny that they are child spirits (Thai belief says they are, and the plan itself says so a line later); aim the
   correction at what is actually the dark practice (spirits trapped by spells, foetal remains, blood, use for harm);
   frame "made out of compassion", "merit carries them to a better birth" and "they keep the precepts" as belief; add
   a cup of water; settle the incense rule and the words "Buddha Day"; and give the promise "for life" its Thai
   counterpart — if you truly cannot, bring them back, never abandon them.

## 1 · What a Kumanthong is

**The name.**
- **[HISTORY/FACT]** *Kumāra* is Pali for "a young boy, son" (the Pali Text Society dictionary, entry *kumāra*)
  ([PTS][pts]). Thai กุมาร (kuman) is that word; ทอง (thong) is gold. Sinnott translates kumanthong "golden boy"
  ([Sinnott 2014][sin]).
- **[HISTORY/FACT]** (one scholar's account) The "gold" is explained as the gold leaf laid on the figure. Thai Wikipedia says the old recipe
  ended with the dried body being lacquered and gilded (ลงรักปิดทอง), "hence the name kumanthong" — but that article is
  flagged as needing citations and its statements are unsourced ([th.wikipedia][thwiki]). Sinnott gives the same
  explanation on the authority of Professor Lom Pengkaeo, a Thai literature scholar, by personal communication
  ([Sinnott 2014][sin]).
- **[HISTORY/FACT]** Dictionary definitions, as quoted by Sinnott (I did not open the dictionaries themselves): the
  Matichon Dictionary of the Thai Language (2004) defines kumanthong as "the ghost of a child that is made with magic
  and is raised to be used for its benefits"; Pallegoix's 1854 Thai dictionary does not have the word, but defines
  *phrai* as "abortive child that sorcerers keep in their house like a demon" ([Sinnott 2014][sin], footnotes 8 and
  10). The Royal Institute's online dictionary would not load (§12).

**The kinds.** Thai and Chinese sources sort them differently, and the sorting itself is contested.
- **[BELIEF]** *Kuman thep* (กุมารเทพ, "deity child") — a heavenly child "invited from the heavens"; and *kuman phrai*
  (กุมารพราย) — the ghost of a foetus or infant, a "pitiful wandering spirit". One collector added *kueng thep kueng
  phrai* (กึ่งเทพกึ่งพราย, "half-deity half-ghost"), "basically well-intentioned and striving to make merit for their
  propitiators". *Kuman phrai* are "potentially dangerous if they are not cared for properly" ([Sinnott 2014][sin]).
- **[CONTESTED]** Whether the thep/phrai split is old: Professor Lom Pengkaeo told Sinnott it is "probably a modern
  adaptation", and that earlier all of them were simply *phi* (ghosts), a word that did not then carry today's horror
  ([Sinnott 2014][sin]). A Taiwanese amulet blog says the three-way Chinese split 天童 / 人童 / 地童 ("heaven, human,
  earth child") is a Chinese reinterpretation; Thai practice has two lines, thep and phrai ([KUMIHO][kmh]).
- **[BELIEF]** Female child spirits are *kumari* (กุมารี) or *kuman kaew* (กุมารแก้ว) ([Sinnott 2014][sin]); Thai
  Wikipedia and Thai horoscope sites instead call a kept girl's spirit *hong phrai* (โหงพราย) ([th.wikipedia][thwiki];
  [MThai][mthai]). **[CONTESTED]** terminology.
- **[BELIEF]** Related charms: *luk krok* (ลูกกรอก) — "amulets that are made from a perfectly formed, preserved foetus";
  *rak yom* (รักยม) — two small wooden figures in oil, a love charm ([Sinnott 2014][sin]).
- **[BELIEF]** Old texts also describe a fierce kind — "executioner" kumanthong called เพชรภูติงาน or เพชรปราบ,
  "kept for killing or harming enemies", made by summoning violent-death ghosts — set against the "luck and loving-
  kindness" kind kept to guard the house and call customers, which anyone can keep safely; Luang Phor Tae of Wat Sam
  Ngam's are given as the classic example of the gentle kind ([MThai][mthai]; [Mekakad 2017, ch. 2][mek2], citing
  Kanlong 2014).

**What keepers believe it does.**
- **[BELIEF]** It protects the owner and family, helps trade, warns of danger and guards the house from thieves; it
  must be raised "like your own child", given rice and water and called to eat ([th.wikipedia][thwiki]; [Kom Chad Luek
  2021][kcl1], quoting a Suphanburi spirit-master). The everyday Thai reason for keeping one is help with trade, luck
  and guarding the house: the care guides' own invocation reads "Father brings you to this house to help with trade,
  help with work, guard the house, bring luck" ([web-pra][webpra]; also [TNews 2017][tn1]; [Sanook 2019][snk19];
  [Thairath 2020][tr20]).
- **[BELIEF]** Benefits range from lottery numbers given in dreams and parking spaces to protection and friendship; a
  keeper's asthma eased with a kumanthong "sitting with her" at night ([Sinnott 2014][sin]).
- **[HISTORY/FACT]** It is not part of the scriptures or of mainstream doctrine: a Thai guide says "the Tipitaka does
  not say which realm kumanthong belong to" ([dooduangdi][ddd]); English Wikipedia says the practice "is not part of
  mainstream Buddhist practices" (weakly sourced) ([en.wikipedia][enwiki]); a Taiwanese blog puts it as tolerated by
  temples, "but tolerating is not endorsing" ([KUMIHO][kmh]).

**What a kumanthong is, in Buddhist terms.** The sources do not agree.
- **[BELIEF]** Most are "spirits of deceased children ... wandering ghosts, or *phi re-ron*" (ผีเร่ร่อน), with the
  important exception of *kuman thep* ([Sinnott 2014][sin]).
- **[BELIEF]** A Dhammakaya monk, Phra Maha Somchai Thanavuddho: neither devas, humans nor *peta*, but *kai
  sambhavesi* (กายสัมภเวสี) — beings "floating around us" ([Kalyanamitra 2023][kal1]). *Sambhavesi* "means one seeking
  a birth"; in everyday Thai use, someone who has died and "is still drifting, looking for a birth" ([Kalyanamitra
  2004][kal2]).
- **[CONTESTED]** Whether there is any such waiting state at all: Theravāda Abhidhamma texts such as the
  *Kathāvatthu* "traditionally reject the view that there is an intermediate or transitional state", holding that
  rebirth is immediate; but the belief is "very common among some monks and laypersons" and, according to Bhikkhu
  Sujato, "widely accepted among Thai forest tradition teachers" ([en.wikipedia, Bardo][bardo]). So "children with nowhere to go, waiting for their
  next birth" is sound Thai **belief**, and a point on which Theravāda scholarship is divided.

## 2 · Where the misconceptions come from

### 2.1 *Khun Chang Khun Phaen* — the literary origin

- **[HISTORY/FACT]** In the Thai epic, Khun Phaen kills his pregnant wife Buakhli (บัวคลี่) for plotting against him,
  cuts out the male foetus and dries it over a fire to make a *kumanthong*, the "Goldchild", who then accompanies,
  protects and advises him ([en.wikipedia][enwiki]). Sinnott quotes the translation: "He meditated to focus his mind, and sat grilling the Goldchild,
  heating the whole body, turning it over front and back so the fat dripped and sizzled" ([Sinnott 2014][sin], citing
  Baker and Phongpaichit 2010).
- **[HISTORY/FACT]** **This episode is a late addition to the written poem.** Baker and Pasuk: "In chapter 7, Khru
  Jaeng introduced the story of Khun Phaen marrying Buakhli and then killing her to make a protective spirit (kuman
  thong) from the unborn child. Prince Damrong noted this story was already popular among troubadours, but had not
  earlier appeared in written versions of the text." Khru Jaeng's revision is dated "probably ... in the Fourth Reign"
  (1851–68) ([Baker & Pasuk 2009][bp09]; Sinnott footnote 6 agrees, [Sinnott 2014][sin]).
- **[HISTORY/FACT]** In the poem the Goldchild is one of Khun Phaen's "personal team of spirits", which protect him
  and attack his enemies' protection ([Baker & Pasuk 2013][bp13]).
- **[BELIEF]** The story is "almost always referenced in public discussions" of kumanthong, and "clearly displays the
  notion of kumanthong ... as resources to be exploited by adults"; a Suphanburi temple, Wat Khae, still celebrates
  Khun Phaen as a hero ([Sinnott 2014][sin]). A Taiwanese blog: "that is literature, not history" ([KUMIHO][kmh]).

### 2.2 The old recipe, and black magic

- **[HISTORY/FACT]** "Traditionally, kumanthong were understood to be made by men, specifically experts in magic and
  ghost manipulation, or *mor-phi*" (หมอผี): "a male foetus is grilled and dried out, and then covered in gold leaf ...
  Magical incantations capture the foetal spirit and put it in service" ([Sinnott 2014][sin], from Professor Lom
  Pengkaeo). Thai Wikipedia and a 2021 Kom Chad Luek column say the same in Thai: a child that died in the womb with
  its mother (ตายทั้งกลม), the body roasted dry before dawn and gilded ([th.wikipedia][thwiki]; [Kom Chad Luek
  2021][kcl1]).
- **[HISTORY/FACT]** (the usual Thai account; Thai Wikipedia gives no source) When real remains became impossible to
  obtain, makers turned to "soil from seven cemeteries"
  (ดินเจ็ดป่าช้า), rak or mayom wood and metal, consecrated (ปลุกเสก) to "set the four elements and call the 32 parts
  of the body" (ตั้งธาตุทั้ง 4 เรียกอาการสามสิบสอง) into a child's spirit ([th.wikipedia][thwiki]; [Sanook
  2014][snk14]). The statue is "probably a modern invention to replace the preserved foetal body" ([Sinnott
  2014][sin]).
- **[HISTORY/FACT]** Today "the practice of making kumanthong from foetuses currently is reviled as a form of child
  exploitation and cruelty", and "a crucial distinction is made between magically-restrained kumanthong versus
  volunteer kumanthong produced and housed in Buddhist temples, or 'collected' by individuals". Devotees "distinguish
  these practices from the evil doings of those who practice 'black magic' (*mon dam*) to control and capture child
  spirits to do their bidding" ([Sinnott 2014][sin]).
- **[BELIEF]** Kom Chad Luek (2021) still calls kumanthong "Thai occult belief" whose origin is "keeping ghosts and
  spirits for use" ([Kom Chad Luek 2021][kcl1]). MGR Online (2012) says it was long regarded as "an amulet of the dark
  line" (วัตถุมงคลในสายมืด), "not worshipped openly", until celebrities made it fashionable ([MGR 2012][mgr12]).

### 2.3 The news

- **[HISTORY/FACT]** **Bangkok, 18 May 2012.** "Six roasted fetuses covered with gold for black magic rituals were
  found in a Bangkok hotel room"; the arrested British national, Chow Hok Kuen, 28, told police he planned to sell
  them in Taiwan for up to 200,000 baht each and that the rituals "are believed to bring good fortune for
  businesspeople" ([CNN 2012][cnn]). Thai PBS: the bodies were inscribed and tied with *sai sin* thread, and advertised
  abroad as having gone through rituals in Thailand ([Thai PBS 2012][tpbs12]). Hong Kong's Oriental Daily headlined
  it as "養鬼仔" ("raising ghost children") and reported, without a source, that kumanthong sold in Hong Kong are
  "mostly clay figures mixed with infant ashes" ([Oriental Daily 2012][od]). MGR Online's headline: "Kumanthong: faith,
  or making a living from corpses?" ([MGR 2012][mgr12]).
- **[HISTORY/FACT]** After that case the word itself became awkward: temple vendors told Sinnott there were no
  "kumanthong" shrines, only one for a "child spirit" (วิญญาณเด็ก) ([Sinnott 2014][sin]).
- **[HISTORY/FACT]** **Rayong, Dec 2018**: more than ten newborns' bodies missing from a cemetery, suspected taken to
  make kumanthong and luk krok for Chinese buyers ([Thairath 2018][tr18]). **Ang Thong, Feb 2026**: infant skeletons
  dug up from an unofficial cemetery strewn with toys, sweets and offerings; Thai PBS asked "why, in the 5G era, do
  Thais still believe in luk krok and kumanthong?" ([Thai PBS 2026][tpbs26]).
- **[HISTORY/FACT]** Online sellers in mainland China advertised figures "containing luk krok foetal powder" and
  "consecrated in a graveyard"; platforms removed them as "feudal superstition" (封建迷信) goods, and a lawyer said
  sellers may be committing fraud ([The Paper 2020][tp]). In Taiwan in August 2026 a sexual-exploitation case involving
  two schoolgirls lured with Thai amulets exposed a kumanthong craze among pupils, and a wave of "expert warnings"
  followed ([DailyView 2026][dv]; [CTWANT 2026][ctw]; [ETtoday 2026][et26]).

### 2.4 "Raising little ghosts" (養小鬼 / 養鬼仔) — the Chinese-language picture

These are the claims the plan's "yin baby spirits like in other traditions" points at. **All [BELIEF] unless marked**,
and most come from popular media:
- **Wealth and luck**: a Taiwanese hostess bought one "to increase her income" ([ETtoday 2020][et20]); a Thai monk
  told Mekakad that Singaporean customers "want the kuman that give gambling luck" ([Mekakad 2017, ch. 3][mek3]).
- **Love and popularity** (桃花, 人緣) for Taiwanese schoolgirls; protection from — or retaliation against — bullies
  ([DailyView 2026][dv]).
- **Revenge**: the "earth child" (地童) can deal with enemies "frighteningly fast"; an anecdote of a colleague falling
  down the stairs the day after a keeper complained to her kumanthong ([GoTrip HK 2026][gt]).
- **Blood**: a seller taught a schoolgirl to feed hers blood as the "ultimate wish package" ([ETtoday 2026][et26]); a
  Malaysian medium says a neglected kumanthong demanded the couple's blood and accepted a symbolic blood draw ([China
  Press 2021][cp]); a Singaporean owner chose Fanta Red "because it represents blood" ([AsiaOne 2022][a1]). The
  Malay *toyol*, a different spirit, is the one that is fed with the owner's own blood ([Yahoo SG 2016][ysg]) — the
  two are easily confused in Malaysia and Singapore.
- **Jealousy**: a couple stayed childless for years because the kumanthong "was jealous" ([ETtoday 2026][et26]); a
  kumanthong "went out of control" when its keeper had her own baby ([ETtoday 2020][et20]); one "killed the foetus"
  after the pregnant wife neglected it ([China Press 2021][cp]).
- **Backlash** (反噬 / 反撲) if you neglect or abandon it, "even death"; "easy to invite a god, hard to send one away"
  (請神容易送神難); "it is essentially a form of *jiangtou* sorcery (降頭)" ([CTWANT 2026][ctw]; [SETN 2019][setn];
  [GoTrip HK 2026][gt]).
- **"Yin"**: the Chinese-language sources describe kumanthong in yin terms — "yin energy is heavy, so never keep it in
  a bedroom" (陰氣比較重 ... 唔可以放喺睡房) ([GoTrip HK 2026][gt]); "yin materials" (陰料) versus "yang materials"
  (陽料) ([DailyView 2026][dv]). **Thai sources I read do not use yin and yang**; they speak of *phi*, *phrai* and
  *thep*. The case file's "regarded as Yin" matches the Chinese-language framing.

**The same Chinese-language sources also defend the "proper" kumanthong** — which shows the distinction Chad wants
is already a live one in that world:
- **[BELIEF]** "It is not the 'raising little ghosts' of rumour, but an infant spirit placed by a Buddhist master out of
  compassion, helping it accumulate merit while protecting the keeper" — a Taiwanese practitioner, 22 years in the
  trade ([China Times 2025][ct]; see §11 on this source).
- **[BELIEF]** "Modern, proper kumanthong have evolved into a Buddhist rite of compassion: monks bring the spirits of
  children who died in childbirth or by accident, unable to be reborn, into the figure, so that ... they accumulate
  merit and are reborn sooner" ([DailyView 2026][dv]); high monks "pity" children who died before their time and
  house them for kind people to care for ([GoTrip HK 2026][gt]); temple monks "pity infants and foetuses who died
  early, wash away their resentment", and modern kumanthong contain no remains ([SETN 2019][setn]).
- **[CONTESTED]** A careful Taiwanese blog warns that to say "kumanthong have nothing to do with raising little
  ghosts" is "an oversimplification": one end of the category (luk krok, phrai) does overlap with it; the other end
  (thep) "has been pushed to the edge of the Buddhist framework". Its distinction: the temple kind "is invited", the
  sorcerer's kind "is imprisoned" (一個是邀請，一個是囚禁) ([KUMIHO][kmh]). Sinnott says the same in scholarly terms
  ([Sinnott 2014][sin]).

## 3 · Kumanthong made by monks

### 3.1 Luang Phor Tae of Wat Sam Ngam — the best documented

The brief named "Luang Phor Tae / หลวงพ่อแต้". **The Thai spelling is หลวงพ่อเต๋** (Tae). Chinese sources write 龍婆爹
([pyttgfp][pyt]).

- **[HISTORY/FACT]** Luang Phor Tae Khongthong (หลวงพ่อเต๋ คงทอง), Phra Khru Phawana Sangwarakhun
  (พระครูภาวนาสังวรคุณ), abbot of Wat Sam Ngam (official name Wat Aranyikaram, วัดอรัญญิการาม), Don Tum district,
  Nakhon Pathom ([Naewna 2014][naewna]; [amuletheritage][ah]). Banmuang also gives his Pali name as Khangkhasuwanno
  (คังคสุวัณโณ) ([Banmuang 2024][bm]).
- **[HISTORY/FACT]** Born Monday 15 June 1891 (B.E. 2434) at Ban Sam Ngam ([Naewna 2014][naewna]; [amuletheritage][ah];
  [thaipra-siam 2012][tps]). Died peacefully on 25 December 1981 (B.E. 2524) ([Naewna 2014][naewna];
  [amuletheritage][ah]). **[CONTESTED]** his age and years as a monk: Naewna says 90 years and 69 vassa; amuletheritage
  and thaipra-siam say "80 years, 6 months, 10 days, 59 vassa" — the dates give 90 years, 6 months, 10 days, so the
  "80" is almost certainly a slip. His body is kept at the temple ([amuletheritage][ah]).
- **[HISTORY/FACT]** He learned from his uncle Luang Lung Daeng (หลวงลุงแดง), from his preceptor Luang Phor Tha of Wat
  Phaniang Taek, from Luang Phor Chaem of Wat Takong, and from a Khmer brahmin, a former general, at Khao Taloong in
  Kanchanaburi, whom he honoured every year ([amuletheritage][ah]).
- **[HISTORY/FACT]** **What his kumanthong were, in the Thai sources.** The recipe came from Luang Lung Daeng: soil from
  seven *pong* (โป่ง, salt-lick springs), from **seven cemeteries** (ดิน 7 ป่าช้า) and from crab holes (ดินขุยปู),
  moulded and **given to villagers to take home for protection** (แจก ... นำไปเพื่อคุ้มครอง) ([amuletheritage][ah];
  [thaipra-siam 2012][tps]). His amulets were made in the Second World War, "not for beauty but for their power, to be
  carried for protection from every danger" ([amuletheritage][ah]). A 2024 column: given out to the faithful for
  "protection from danger, calling wealth, calling luck" ([Banmuang 2024][bm]).
- **[BELIEF]** The legend of the consecration: he would mould them, lay them down, and chant over them until they rose
  up by themselves ([amuletheritage][ah]).
- **[HISTORY/FACT]** First batch about 1942 (B.E. 2485) ([Naewna 2014][naewna]; [Banmuang 2024][bm]; a Chinese
  seller lists "龍婆爹 2485年" ([pyttgfp][pyt])). Old pieces now sell for hundreds of thousands to millions of baht
  ([Naewna 2014][naewna]); the owner of a kumanthong website calls his "the start of their popularity among Thais
  before it spread to foreigners" ([MGR 2016c][mgr16c]), sought especially by Malaysian and Singaporean collectors ([thaipra-siam
  2012][tps]).
- **[HISTORY/FACT]** His chief disciple, Luang Pu Yaem Thanayutto (หลวงปู่แย้ม ฐานยุตโต, Phra Khru Prayutnawakan),
  "took over the making of kumanthong" after him; born 5 January B.E. 2458 (1915, or 1916 if the old Thai new year
  of 1 April is applied), died 27 August 2017, "aged 102" ([Post Today 2017][pt]; [Daily News 2022][dlynews]; they
  disagree on his vassa, 82 or 79).
- **[HISTORY/FACT]** In 2024 Wat Sam Ngam issued "Kumanthong 2", cast from the first batch's mould, to fund repairs to
  the temple; the consecration was set for 11 April 2024 at 12.39 — midday — before Luang Phor Tae's body, in batches
  of up to 82,000 pieces ([Banmuang 2024][bm]).
- **[UNCONFIRMED]** **That Luang Phor Tae made kumanthong out of compassion for child spirits, to give them a home and
  a chance at merit.** No Thai source I opened says so. They call *him* compassionate — "a monk of great loving-
  kindness" (มีความเมตตาสูง), kind to every disciple, feeding the temple's animals before he ate ([Naewna
  2014][naewna]; [amuletheritage][ah]) — but the purpose they give for his kumanthong is protection.

### 3.2 Other makers the sources name

Listed because the plan (§2.3 12) asks; **none of them should be named as the makers of the game's three** (the plan
agrees).
- Luang Phor Kuay (หลวงพ่อกวย), Wat Khositaram, Chainat — "his kumanthong has heavenly ears and eyes ... helps trade,
  warns of danger", in the words of Luang Phor Paew of Wat Dao Rueang ([Kom Chad Luek 2021][kcl1]).
- Luang Pu Hong (หลวงปู่หงษ์), Wat Phetchaburi, Surin — a "deity" kumanthong ([108hora][h108]).
- Phra Khru Palat Sitthiwat, "Luang Phi Nam Fon" (หลวงพี่น้ำฝน), Wat Phai Lom, Nakhon Pathom — keeps a "kuman nursery"
  for unwanted figures ([Sanook 2016][snk16]).
- "Phra Somporn" (a pseudonym), a Chiang Mai monk who consecrates kuman, and "Phi Thep" (a pseudonym), an amulet-shop
  owner — the two main informants of Mekakad's thesis ([Mekakad 2017, ch. 3][mek3]).

### 3.3 Materials and consecration

- **[CONTESTED]** **"Monk-made" does not mean "free of graveyard materials".** Luang Phor Tae's used cemetery soil
  (§3.1). Mekakad's monk informant said his materials were "almost 100%" *athan* (อาถรรพ์, uncanny) materials —
  seven-cemetery soil, ash from cremation pyres, "child's ash" and corpse oil (น้ำมันพราย) under the base, a dead
  person's hair, the *sai sin* thread used to lead a coffin in a funeral procession, burial cloth — and that consecration "must be done at a
  cemetery only, at the pyre", finishing at the temple ([Mekakad 2017, ch. 3][mek3]). Chinese explainers draw the line
  differently: the "heaven child" (天童) by "high monks" with temple soil and incense ash, the 派童 / 地童 by lay
  *ajarn* with grave soil and bone ash ([DailyView 2026][dv]; [GoTrip HK 2026][gt]); Chinese Wikipedia adds that lay
  ajarn make the thep kind too ([zh.wikipedia][zhwiki]). The Chinese "monk = clean, ajarn = dark" rule does not match
  Thai practice.
- **[HISTORY/FACT]** Most devotees obtain them "from temples or spirit mediums who specialise in the investment of
  power into objects (*pluk-sek*)"; most use a spirit medium to invest the statue; fees range "from less than one
  hundred Baht to thousands of Baht" ([Sinnott 2014][sin]).
- **[BELIEF]** A 21-year-old collector told Sinnott the most valuable kumanthong "were those that had been created by
  monks rather than laypeople". Sinnott's comment: "the magic that is required to make a kumanthong is thus positioned
  within established Buddhist institutions and distanced from the negative associations of ... 'black magic'", and
  "the negative associations of magic are downplayed, or repositioned as part of what determines the commodity's
  value" ([Sinnott 2014][sin]).
- **[HISTORY/FACT]** The *sai sin* (สายสิญจน์) is the long white thread a monk holds in his joined hands while chanting
  blessings, also run round a house and the base of a Buddha image ([th.wikipedia, สายสิญจน์][thwiki-ss]).
  **[UNCONFIRMED]** a source describing a sai sin running from a monk's hands to kumanthong figures in their
  consecration — it is consistent with ordinary consecration practice, but I found no description of it for
  kumanthong.

### 3.4 Compassion, merit and a better birth — the belief, and who holds it

**[BELIEF], well attested:**
- "The need for merit making was a central rationale for the existence of the kumanthong": "caring for the child
  leads to an accrual of merit on behalf of the 'parents', and the assistance the kumanthong provides his/her
  guardians leads to an accrual of merit preparing the child for their next birth". Temple shrines "provide a field
  of merit ... to care for a child, make merit for that child, and to also provide an opportunity for that child to
  make merit for him/herself by aiding the propitiator" ([Sinnott 2014][sin]). **This is Chad's framing, as
  devotee belief.**
- A monk at Wat Pradu's shrine (stillborn conjoined twins): the practice does "not bind the child spirits", they
  "came of their own free will", and "when it was time for the spirits to go ... they would go"; since the twins were
  stillborn "they never had the opportunity to make merit" ([Sinnott 2014][sin]).
- "Phi Thep", the shop owner: "on its own a kuman cannot make merit, while we can, so we should spread loving-
  kindness and chant, dedicating merit every time" (ลำพังผีกุมารเองเขาไม่สามารถทำบุญได้) ([Mekakad 2017, ch.
  4][mek4]).
- A seller: kumanthong are housed "to help the keeper like one of the family and make merit together — better than
  leaving a child's spirit to wander — believing that when the spirit has gathered enough merit, it can be reborn"
  (*seller*) ([saturday5amulet 2021][s5a]).
- A Thai guide: when the karmic tie ends, "they receive a share of merit that lets them be reborn" — and, in the same
  article, "no scientific method or Buddhist criterion can say that one of these has been reborn" ([dooduangdi][ddd]).
- Singapore: "they have to accept Buddhism and do no harm before they are given the status of 'Kumantong'", and
  "the keepers transfer their merits to these souls and pray that they are liberated" ([Yahoo SG 2016][ysg]).
- A Thai consecrator: a kumanthong with enough merit becomes a "semi-deity"; "they too are spirits that need merit"
  ([MGR 2012][mgr12]).

**Where the sources differ from it:**
- **[CONTESTED]** The Buddhist, compassionate framing is the *contemporary* layer. Sinnott traces "how the kumanthong
  shifted from being purely a resource to be exploited by a magical practitioner/parent to being a child in need of
  caretaking"; categories "have shifted to minimise the associations of the spirit with ghosts ... and to place
  kumanthong in more lofty, Buddhist-influenced categories" — "a bourgeois cleansing" ([Sinnott 2014][sin]).
- **[CONTESTED]** In much Thai practice merit is not what carries the child on; it is what makes the kumanthong
  stronger: "the more often you make merit, the more it adds to the kumanthong's power, so that requests are granted
  faster" ([Amorerana 2019][amo]). And the everyday purpose is the keeper's benefit (§1).
- **[CONTESTED]** A Taiwanese spiritual writer reads keeping as binding: "they could go to a better place, instead of
  being held here by limiting beliefs or spells" ([GVM 2026][gvm]).
- **[CONTESTED]** Monastic critics say not to keep them at all (§6).

## 4 · Precepts and limits

**The canon.**
- **[HISTORY/FACT]** The five precepts: refrain from killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, false speech, and
  intoxicants ([Access to Insight, pañcasīla][pan]). **Gambling and wishing for wealth are not among them.** The
  Sigalovada Sutta lists "indulgence in gambling which causes heedlessness" among the "six channels for dissipating
  wealth" ([DN 31][dn31]).
- **[HISTORY/FACT]** Among the kinds of "wrong livelihood" a monk avoids, the canon lists "promising gifts to deities
  in return for favors; fulfilling such promises; demonology" ([DN 2][dn2]). Promising a spirit a reward for a favour
  is exactly the Thai *bon-ban* / *kae-bon* (บนบาน / แก้บน) that the care guides teach keepers. (The rule is for
  monastics, not lay keepers.)
- **[HISTORY/FACT]** A Sangha announcement of 1952 (B.E. 2495), quoted by the National Office of Buddhism in 2016,
  calls monks who consecrate charms or teach spells for money guilty of *ajiva-vipatti* (อาชีววิบัติ, failure in right
  livelihood), to be disrobed; the Sangha Supreme Council "has an order forbidding monks to consecrate charms that make
  people superstitious" ([MGR 2016a][mgr16a]; [Matichon 2016][mtc16]).

**What keepers and teachers believe about limits** — **[BELIEF]**:
- Never "ask it to harm or control another person"; make no vow you cannot keep and no wish that breaks the law;
  "make merit and dedicate it more than you seek benefits from the kumanthong" ([dooduangdi][ddd]).
- Never make "malicious wishes": forcing a spirit to harm others costs the keeper "heavily in health or fortune"
  ([DailyView 2026][dv]); "no demanding or evil thoughts, or the kumanthong cannot help, and it may harm your own
  merit" ([China Times 2025][ct]); keepers must keep them "without violating the Buddhist precepts or the law"
  (*seller*) ([pyttgfp][pyt]); a Singaporean owner was told "not to do bad things to others" ([AsiaOne 2022][a1]).
- **A kumanthong grows like its keeper.** "Phi Thep": "a kuman tends to take on the character of the one who raises
  it, like raising a child. Teach it to be good, it is good; teach it to bear grudges against others, and it is
  no different from a venomous snake — some even turn back and harm their owner" ([Mekakad 2017, ch. 4][mek4]); "you
  must teach the kuman good ways" ([thaipra-siam 2012][tps]). A Taiwanese blogger who decided not to keep one:
  "if it follows you, what it absorbs is not merit but your desires — that is not helping it, it is harming it"
  (如果祂跟著你，沾染的不是功德，是你的慾望) ([KUMIHO][kmh]).
- **The "deity" kind dislikes wrongdoing.** "Kuman thep do not like immoral matters" (ไม่ชอบเรื่องผิดศิลธรรม, as spelled there); keepers
  should live morally, chant and meditate ([108hora][h108]). The thep kind "cannot be forced into service" and helps
  "out of free will" ([Sinnott 2014][sin]). In Taiwan, the temple kind's spirit is said to have taken the Three
  Refuges ([KUMIHO][kmh]).
- **What they cannot do.** "Whatever you ask that does not go beyond karma, they can bring about; what goes beyond
  karma, they cannot, because they too are spirits that need merit" — and "do not become obsessed, expecting them to
  help with everything" ([MGR 2012][mgr12]). A keeper: "whether you sell well depends on the owner's own past merit;
  the kumanthong only helps it along" ([Thairath 2020][tr20]).
- **Precepts given to them.** In one release rite the keeper gives the kuman the five precepts "the way a monk gives
  them" before burying the figure ([Mekakad 2017, ch. 3][mek3]). Offerings exclude alcohol and cigarettes, "in which we
  embed a dhamma principle" — the monk informant ([Mekakad 2017, ch. 3][mek3]; also [MThai][mthai]; [108hora][h108]).
- **Using one for harm comes back on you.** A woman on a Thai TV ghost programme admitted using a kuman — "her own
  child" — to harm others, and her life ended in suffering, read as the karma of "forcing the kuman to do what is
  against morality" ([Mekakad 2017, ch. 3][mek3]).

**Counter-lore** — **[CONTESTED]**: the "executioner" kumanthong made to kill (§1); kumanthong that "anger and punish
their guardian ... even pushing somebody down the stairs" ([Sinnott 2014][sin]); the Hong Kong anecdote of a kumanthong
punishing a colleague ([GoTrip HK 2026][gt]).

**[UNCONFIRMED]** **"Kumanthong keep the precepts."** I did not find it in any source. The supportable versions are
above: keepers must keep the precepts and must never ask for harm; the temple / deity kind is believed not to lend
itself to wrongdoing.

## 5 · Care and commitment

### 5.1 Offerings

- **[BELIEF]** **Plain water, every day, never allowed to run out** — the rule the guides state most firmly: "water (never
  missing from the kumanthong's shelf, there every day)" ([TNews 2017a][tn1]; [TNews 2017b][tn2]); "water must never
  be missing" ([108hora][h108]; [Sanook 2014][snk14]); "at least one glass of water in front of the kumanthong, never
  let it run out" ([Mekakad 2017, ch. 4][mek4]).
- **[BELIEF]** **The red drink**: "nowadays worshippers like to offer red water" ([th.wikipedia][thwiki]); Luang Phor
  Kuay's kumanthong "like small bottles of red Fanta" ([Kom Chad Luek 2021][kcl1]); keepers offer "red soda, snacks,
  and toys" ([Sinnott 2014][sin]); because a kumanthong is believed to be a child's spirit, "red water with a drinking
  straw is very popular" (น้ำแดงพร้อมหลอดดูด) ([Thairath 2026, red water][trred]). The plan's red sodas with straws
  are well grounded.
- **[CONTESTED]** Red is not required: a monk who makes them: "people believe it must be red water, but it need not be
  red water or milk — a kuman is a child, he eats what we eat" ([Mekakad 2017, ch. 3][mek3]); offerings to the deity
  kind "are usually plain water, or other beverages only" ([Sinnott 2014][sin]).
- **[BELIEF]** No savoury or raw food (ห้ามถวายของคาวของดิบ), no alcohol ([TNews 2017a][tn1]; [Sanook 2019][snk19];
  [MThai][mthai]; [108hora][h108]); sweets, cakes, fruit, milk ([Sanook 2014][snk14]; [MThai][mthai]), toys ([Sanook 2019][snk19]; [Sinnott 2014][sin]); "call them
  to eat" ([th.wikipedia][thwiki]); do not let offerings rot ([dooduangdi][ddd]); clear them once the incense has burnt
  out — "and you may eat them yourself" ([Amorerana 2019][amo]).
- **[CONTESTED]** How often: daily ([MGR 2012][mgr12]; [web-pra][webpra]); change the water and red water "every two
  or three days is fine" ([TNews 2017a][tn1]); weekly or fortnightly ([Kom Chad Luek 2021][kcl1]; [Mekakad 2017, ch.
  3][mek3]; a Singaporean owner, every Thursday, [AsiaOne 2022][a1]); every *wan phra* "for convenience" ([Sanook
  2019][snk19]; [Amorerana 2019][amo]). What matters is keeping whatever was agreed: "a kumanthong is a subtle body
  and holds truthfulness (*sacca*) to be the most important" (ถือเรื่องสัจจะเป็นสำคัญ) ([MThai][mthai]).

### 5.2 Why red — and why not to say "blood"

- **[CONTESTED]** Explanations differ: red is auspicious (a Chinese idea), it is sweet, it may stand in for blood in
  old sacrifices, it may descend from a red herbal tonic, or plain water turned red from incense sticks
  ([Matichon Academy 2018][mta]; [Thairath 2026, red water][trred]). A spirit-master on a TV programme says old
  kumanthong rites used the owner's fresh blood and teachers substituted red water as a "skilful means" (กุศโลบาย)
  ([Kom Chad Luek 2022][kcl2]). Sanook (2026): the blood idea "should be understood as a hypothesis ... no proven
  historical evidence"; for kumanthong, sweet drinks and toys are offered "because they are thought of as children"
  ([Sanook 2026][snk26]).

### 5.3 Incense

**[CONTESTED]** — no agreement, and some guides contradict each other:

| source | what it says |
|---|---|
| Wat Sam Ngam / Luang Pu Yaem guide ([TNews 2017a][tn1]) | ordinary offering **2 or 3 sticks**; 5 for a vow; **"never light one stick"** (ห้ามจุดธูป 1 ดอก) |
| [web-pra][webpra] | **2** for Wat Sam Ngam's kumanthong; for others, ask the master who consecrated them |
| [Sanook 2014][snk14] | 2 to call them; 5 for a vow |
| [Sanook 2019][snk19] | 5 when inviting one into the house |
| [MThai][mthai] | "1 to 5, or 9, as convenient", with two candles |
| [toprich24][tr24] | a kumanthong **from a temple: 5**; **a child's spirit: 1** (วิญญาณลูก 1 ดอก) |
| [Thairath 2026, incense][trinc] | one stick is for "the dead, local guardian spirits, ancestors' spirits or household spirits" |
| [Amorerana 2019][amo] | famous masters say offerings "may follow each person's belief — there is no right or wrong" |

Welcoming a kumanthong into a house is often done with 9, 12 or 16 sticks to ask the house spirits' leave
([TNews 2017a][tn1]; [web-pra][webpra]).

### 5.4 Talking to them, and where they sit

- **[BELIEF]** Think of them often, invite them to family meals, tell them when a visitor is coming ("Please don't
  tease the visitor, okay?") ([Sinnott 2014][sin]); tell them when you leave and when you come home ([GoTrip HK
  2026][gt]); talk with them and spread loving-kindness ([Mekakad 2017, ch. 4][mek4]; [Amorerana 2019][amo]); never
  scold them ([TNews 2017a][tn1]; [dooduangdi][ddd]). A keeper, Krit: the keeper needs "a good base of merit —
  morality guiding his life, truthfulness first"; time to talk with them and "to invite the kuman along to make
  merit"; and enough means, "because keeping a kuman is like keeping a child, who needs love, warmth, food, toys"
  ([Mekakad 2017, ch. 4][mek4]).
- **[BELIEF]** Placement: lower than the Buddha ("the Buddha image raised higher than the others", [Sinnott
  2014][sin]); on a separate shelf, lower than Buddha images and deities but not down on the floor ([Amorerana
  2019][amo]); not facing west, not under stairs, not at one's feet (ปลายเท้า), not facing a toilet door ([TNews
  2017a][tn1]; [108hora][h108]). A Hong Kong taboo list adds: not in a bedroom ([GoTrip HK 2026][gt]).

### 5.5 Merit and the holy day

- **[BELIEF]** "On Wan Phra (วันพระ), a day devoted to Buddhist ritual based on a lunar calendar, special offerings are
  made" ([Sinnott 2014][sin]; also [Amorerana 2019][amo]; [MThai][mthai]; [108hora][h108]). The sources pair the holy
  day with **offerings to the kumanthong**; merit dedication (อุทิศส่วนกุศล, กรวดน้ำ) they recommend generally — "keep
  the five or eight precepts regularly, spread loving-kindness, pour water and dedicate merit to the kumanthong"
  ([dooduangdi][ddd]; also [Mekakad 2017, ch. 4][mek4]).
- **[HISTORY/FACT]** The canonical basis for giving on behalf of the departed is the *Tirokudda Kanda*: "As water
  raining on a hill flows down to the valley, even so does what is given here benefit the dead. As rivers full of
  water fill the ocean full, even so does what is given here benefit the dead" — the second is the *Yathā vārivahā*
  stanza that chapter 4 and the plan's ending D use — and "when this offering is given, well-placed in the Sangha, it
  works for their long-term benefit" ([Pv 1.5][pv15]).

### 5.6 For life? — the sources disagree

- **[BELIEF]** For life: "keeping a kumanthong is a lifelong matter; never end the relationship casually, or it will
  backlash" ([SETN 2019][setn], reporting a Malaysian audio clip); "once you invite one home, it is a lifetime's
  responsibility" ([GoTrip HK 2026][gt]).
- **[CONTESTED]** Not necessarily for life: "the parental-child relationship exists so long as it is mutually
  beneficial and is not necessarily a relationship for life"; kumanthong "can be returned if proved unsatisfactory",
  "may be returned to the temple or the spirit medium from which they were obtained", and "if the guardians do not
  attentively tend to the spirits they will leave on their own" ([Sinnott 2014][sin]). Thai guides give respectful
  endings: tell them politely you can no longer care for them and wish them a good birth; ask the temple or master
  to take them back; do not float them away or leave them under a tree ([dooduangdi][ddd]); a "release ceremony"
  with a master or monks — "never push the burden off by throwing a kumanthong away like an object" ([Amorerana
  2019][amo]); bury with flowers after giving them the five precepts, then make merit and dedicate it ([Mekakad 2017,
  ch. 3][mek3]; similar, [DMC 2011][dmc]).
- **[BELIEF]** And they may move on by themselves when their time comes: "when it was time for the spirits to go, that
  is be reincarnated, they would go" ([Sinnott 2014][sin]); dreams of a child saying goodbye are read as a sign it has
  been reborn ([dooduangdi][ddd]).

### 5.7 Neglect, and what happens to unwanted ones

- **[CONTESTED]** Neglect: "problems and obstacles, easy illness, trade that never prospers" ([Sanook 2014][snk14]);
  "direct harm to the keeper" ([Sanook 2019][snk19]); the phrai kind "potentially dangerous if not cared for"
  ([Sinnott 2014][sin]); but the deity kind "gives no harm to its keeper even if not properly cared for" ([Amorerana
  2019][amo]).
- **[HISTORY/FACT]** **Returned because the luck did not come.** Mekakad's shop owner: "some customers who bought kuman
  from the shop **kept them and got no result, or had no time to care for them — the shop gladly takes them back,
  because Phi Thep says he pities the kuman ghosts**, does not want them to go hungry, and does not want people to
  leave them by the road or at temples" (เลี้ยงแล้วไม่เกิดผล ... ทางร้านก็ยินดีรับคืนกลับมา) ([Mekakad 2017, ch.
  3][mek3]). The same owner once gave a kuman free to a crying student, telling it, "Go and live with the student. Be
  good, don't be naughty" ([Mekakad 2017, ch. 3][mek3]).
- **[HISTORY/FACT]** **Left at temples.** February 2016: "child angel" dolls left under bodhi trees and stupas at a
  Nakhon Chai Si temple, some with burnt-out incense and bottles of red drink, "probably because the keepers kept them
  and did not succeed" ([Kapook 2016][kpk]; [MGR 2016b][mgr16b]); Wat Phai Lom's abbot refused to cremate a doll and
  took it into his "Kuman Sombat nursery", where "kuman people did not want, broken, or naughty, have been left —
  more than 1,000" ([Sanook 2016][snk16]).

## 6 · Buddhist critical views

- **[BELIEF]** *(monastic)* Phra Maha Somchai Thanavuddho (Dhammakaya): kumanthong are *sambhavesi*; "we should not get
  involved with these things ... keeping kumanthong, Nang Kwak and the like brings no benefit and more harm",
  binding the keeper to sorcery into the next life ([Kalyanamitra 2023][kal1]). The Dhammakaya site DMC: keepers come
  to treat them as a refuge, their thoughts circle around them, and "the thought of giving, keeping precepts,
  meditating, or reaching the Triple Gem is not there"; the release rite ends with "now the Triple Gem has arisen in
  my heart" ([DMC 2011][dmc]).
- **[HISTORY/FACT]** *(state)* The National Office of Buddhism, Jan 2016: a monk carrying a "child angel" doll in public
  is "not fitting", and monks consecrating them break the Sangha Supreme Council's order — up to disrobing ([Matichon
  2016][mtc16]; [MGR 2016a][mgr16a]). The Nakhon Pathom Buddhism office director: people adore them while the fashion
  lasts, then "throw them away as rubbish by the road, under bodhi trees ... and at temples" ([Sanook 2016][snk16]).
- **[BELIEF]** Phra Phayom Kalyano: the dolls would soon be "belief trash" (ขยะความเชื่อ), like the Jatukham amulets
  before them ([MGR 2016b][mgr16b]). Buddha Issara: religion had declined and people were looking for something to
  hold on to ([MGR 2016a][mgr16a]). The health minister: anyone who converses with a doll should see a doctor ([MGR
  2016a][mgr16a]).
- **[HISTORY/FACT]** Suan Dusit Poll, 26–27 Jan 2016, 1,029 people: 71.22% saw the dolls as "superstitious, backward,
  relying on sorcery"; 51.90% disagreed with consecrating them, "especially with monks involved, which goes against
  Buddhist principles"; 98.54% were not interested in buying one ([MGR 2016b][mgr16b]).
- **[HISTORY/FACT]** *(scholarly)* Mekakad: monks appear as consecrators of kuman "amid criticism from rationalist
  Buddhists and intellectuals", and Thai society asks whether a monk consecrating kuman "promotes superstition among
  Buddhists instead of building wisdom" ([Mekakad 2017, abstract][mek-abs]; [ch. 3][mek3]). Kanlong's Chulalongkorn
  thesis: belief in kumanthong reflects a shift "from a religion-centered holiness to the economy-driven secular
  world" ([Kanlong 2014][kan]). Wu Shengyang on its spread to China ([Wu 2014][wu], abstract only). A business
  researcher put the Thai amulet market at 35 billion baht in 2012 and said sellers "exploit foreign businessmen's
  ignorance" by adding "it must be a child's corpse" ([MGR 2012][mgr12]). Baker and Pasuk: King Mongkut's reforms
  "shifted the balance, but far from completely. To this day, monks ... bless amulets" ([Baker & Pasuk 2013][bp13]).
- **[BELIEF]** Taiwan: "kumanthong, and sorcery like it, mirror human greed, anger, delusion and fear" ([GVM
  2026][gvm]); a folk expert called the online trade "all fraud" ([CTWANT 2026][ctw]).
- Not consulted (no open copy found): Pattana Kitiarsa's and Peter A. Jackson's books on Thai prosperity religion,
  and any *Silpa Wattanatham* article.

## 8 · Claims in the current plan to fix

Against rev 4 (commit d4b3592). Each: where, what it says, what the sources say, a suggested fix. The fixes are
proposals; the words are Chad's.

**F1 · What the misconception is** — §2.6 1, z5yinq, ow5yin, the core's first sentence, ending B's old member.
z5yinq bundles three ideas: "yin", "baby ghosts", "the kind people raise for luck... or to hurt someone". The owner
answers all three with "No, no... a different thing altogether", then a moment later (ow5monk) says they are "small
ones who passed on too young" — a child's spirit. **The sources:** kumanthong are believed to be the spirits of
children who died (§1); keeping them for luck and trade is the everyday Thai reason (§1); what Thai and Chinese sources
themselves call the dark practice is the spirit **captured or forced by spells, foetal remains, blood, use for harm**
(§2.2, §2.4). **[CONTESTED]** as written. **Fix:** let the owner say yes to the true part and no to the false part:
*"Children, yes. Ghosts to be afraid of, no. The kind you're thinking of — trapped with black magic, fed blood, sent to
hurt people — that's something else."* Then his Luang Phor line. Core: *"...are not the trapped, blood-fed spirits of
frightening stories."* B's old member: *"They're not a horror story, brother. They're children a monk gave a home
to."* (Chad's purpose, "not yin baby spirits", is kept in substance; only its target is sharpened.)

**F2 · "Made out of compassion"** — ow5yin, §2.6 2, the core ("They are made out of compassion"). As a statement
about **these three**, from the owner, it is fine. As a general or historical statement it is **[UNCONFIRMED]**: the
best-documented monk-maker's kumanthong were made for protection (§3.1), and the compassionate framing is a
contemporary devotee belief (§3.4). **Fix:** keep it in the owner's mouth; in the core, frame it: *"Kumanthong given a
home at a temple are kept, by those who keep them well, out of compassion..."* or *"Many who keep them see it as
compassion: ..."*.

**F3 · "They help a kind person a little, and with the merit... one day, they can go on to a better birth"** —
ow5monk, §2.6 3. **[BELIEF]**, well attested — it is nearly Sinnott's sentence (§3.4). **Fix:** add "we believe" or
"the old people say" once; nothing else.

**F4 · "They keep the precepts"** — ow5never ("They keep the precepts... so must you."), z5C7 ("They kept the
precepts... even when I didn't."), §2.6 6. **[UNCONFIRMED]** — in no source (§4). Teach C already frames it as belief
("they are believed never to help with anything against the precepts") and can stay, perhaps without "never".
**Fix:** ow5never — *"The ones from a temple, we believe, won't help you do wrong. So don't you ever ask."* z5C7 —
*"Nothing answered. They wouldn't do wrong... even when I asked them to."* (his reading of a silence, which is what C
shows).

**F5 · "One incense stick each"** — §2.1 2 (Chad's decision), the card, z5incense. **[CONTESTED]**: a Wat Sam Ngam
guide says never one stick, others say two, three or five, one guide says one for a child's spirit, and one source says
every master differs (§5.3). A Thai player may also read a single stick as an offering to the dead. **Fix:** none
needed if Chad keeps it; make it the owner's or his Luang Phor's instruction ("one stick each, the way Luang Phor
taught"), and never have a line call it *the* rule.

**F6 · No water on the altar** — §2.2 10, §4.1 1 (the altar's contents), the card. The plan has three red sodas and
no plain water, and plain water is what the Thai guides say most firmly must never be missing (§5.1). **Fix:** a small
cup of water before each child (or one shared), topped up as part of every night. Keep the red sodas and straws — well
grounded (§5.1). Add no line connecting red to blood (§5.2).

**F7 · "EVERY NIGHT — a fresh drink each"** — the card. Frequency varies (daily to weekly to *wan phra*), so as the
owner's rule it is fine (§5.1). A small true touch the guides give: the old drinks may be drunk by the keeper once
cleared ([Amorerana 2019][amo]). Ending A's "drinks old and flat, never changed" matches what neglect looks like.

**F8 · "EVERY BUDDHA DAY — make merit in their names"** — §2.5 3, the card, the cork board, §2.3 11. The sources tie
*wan phra* to **offerings to the kumanthong** and recommend merit dedication in general; "make merit in their names on
every holy day" is a fair synthesis, **[UNCONFIRMED]** as a stated rule. The words: in English "Buddha Day" is a
common name for Vesak, the once-a-year festival, a public holiday in Singapore since 1955 ([en.wikipedia, Vesak][vesak]);
the weekly day is วันพระ (*wan phra*), "a day devoted to Buddhist ritual based on a lunar calendar" ([Sinnott
2014][sin]) — the *uposatha* observance ([en.wikipedia, Uposatha][upo]; that the two are one day is common knowledge,
not stated on the page). Chapter 3 already uses "Buddha Day" for the
uposatha (CLAUDE.md, v21.2), so the game is consistent with itself, but a player could read "every Buddha Day" as
once a year. **Fix (Chad's call):** "EVERY WAN PHRA (BUDDHA DAY)", or "EVERY HOLY DAY". The temple "merit receipts" on
the cork board were not researched (**[UNCONFIRMED]**, plausible).

**F9 · "For as long as you live"** — §2.5 1, ow5promise, the card, z5epi0, the core. **[CONTESTED]** (§5.6). As the
owner's condition it is a defensible, stricter teaching, and it serves Chad's purpose. But Thai sources give respectful
endings, and the frightening version ("stop and they will punish you") is exactly the Chinese-language picture the
chapter clears. **Fix:** keep the promise; give the owner line 10 of §7 (*"If one day you really cannot, bring them
back to me, or to a temple. Never leave them by the road."*). Ending D's envelope already fits it.

**F10 · ow5red** ("a family took him home for luck. When the luck didn't come fast enough, they brought him back.
Didn't want him any more.") and the red one's "...They didn't want me any more." — **supported** (§5.7): a shop owner
takes back kuman from customers who "kept them and got no result"; dolls were left at temples, temple staff thought,
because keepers "did not succeed"; "they can be returned if proved unsatisfactory". **[HISTORY/FACT]** that it happens. Keep.

**F11 · ow5shy** (an old auntie for twenty years; her family brought him back after she died) — **[UNCONFIRMED]**: no
source on a keeper's family returning one after a death, though returning them to the shop is documented. Plausible;
keep.

**F12 · ow5little** ("somebody left him at a temple gate") — **supported** (§5.7): more than a thousand unwanted kuman
left at Wat Phai Lom; dolls left at a Nakhon Chai Si temple; a shop owner asking people not to "leave them at
temples". Keep.

**F13 · Film M** (§4.0b, §4.1c: a temple hall at night, candles, a monk chanting, a white thread from his joined hands
to the three) — plausible against the sources (§3.3): figures are consecrated by chanting (ปลุกเสก), and a monk holding
a *sai sin* in añjali while chanting is ordinary Thai practice; I found no account of a sai sin to kumanthong
specifically (**[UNCONFIRMED]** detail, consistent with practice). Night is not required — Wat Sam Ngam's 2024
consecration was at midday — but it is not wrong. **Keep out of the image:** a cemetery, a cremation pyre, bones, ash,
oil or anything from a body — real consecrations sometimes use them (§3.3), and showing them would teach the
misconception. A true and gentle optional detail: Luang Phor Tae's legend of figures laid down and chanted over until
they "rose by themselves" ([amuletheritage][ah]).

**F14 · Teach A's Yin and Yang** — the case file's own words; §2.3 14. The vocabulary is the Chinese-language framing
(§2.4); Thai sources describe the same danger as a kumanthong taking over a keeper whose mind gives way ([Thairath
2020][tr20]) and as being bound to spirit matters ([DMC 2011][dmc]). Keep (it is Master Z's); attributing it — "in the
tradition I learned it in" — would stop it reading as the misconception. Chad's call.

**F15 · Teach C: "Kumanthong made with compassion are kept for their sake, not for what they bring"** — this contradicts
the everyday Thai reason for keeping them (§1) and Chad's own "an opportunity to help their owners". **Fix:**
*"...are kept first for their sake; whatever help they give is a gift, not a wage."*

**F16 · Teach D: "That merit is what helps them on to a better birth."** — stated as fact. **[BELIEF]** (§3.4), with
canonical support for giving on behalf of the departed ([Pv 1.5][pv15]). **Fix:** *"That merit, it is believed, is what
helps them on toward a better birth."* "Ask for nothing back" fits the "deity" model, where thep "are not involved in a
direct exchange" ([Sinnott 2014][sin]). z5dedic and z5D4 are already phrased as wishes ("may you go on to a better
birth"), which is exactly right and needs no change.

**F17 · The core: "children with nowhere to go are given a chance to help a kind keeper, and to receive the merit that
carries them to a better birth"** — **[BELIEF]** stated as fact. **Fix:** "...are believed to be given a chance to help
a kind keeper..." (with F1 and F2 for the core's first two sentences).

**F18 · ow5never: "Not for money, not for lottery numbers... if that is why you want them, don't take them."** — as the
owner's rule, fine and in line with Chad's lesson, which has support: gambling "causes heedlessness" ([DN 31][dn31]);

benefits" ([dooduangdi][ddd]); "your desires harm them" ([KUMIHO][kmh]). **But** do not let the chapter imply that
seeking luck is a foreign mistake: lottery requests are made at Thai temple shrines ([Sinnott 2014][sin]) and Wat Sam
Ngam's own 2024 issue promised wealth and luck ([Banmuang 2024][bm]). This links to F1: z5yinq's "the kind people
raise for luck" should not be what the owner denies.

**F19 · The altar's placement** — §4.1 1. **Consistent** with the sources (§5.4): a separate cabinet, lower than the

**F20 · Toys and sweets** (§2.2 10), "talk to them kindly" (the card), "Come... eat" (z5eat) — **supported** (§5.1,

**F21 · The possession premise** — **supported as belief**: "the spirits may possess their guardians either to protect

**F22 · The owner who entrusts rather than sells** (§2.2 1) — **has a real parallel**: Mekakad's shop owner gave one

## 11 · Notes on particular sources

- **Sinnott 2014** and **Mekakad 2017** are the backbone: both are fieldwork with keepers, makers and sellers, and both
  quote their informants. Mekakad's informants are pseudonymous.
- **Thai care guides** (TNews, Sanook horoscope, MThai, web-pra, toprich24, 108hora, Amorerana, saturday5amulet,
  dooduangdi) contradict each other on details; quoted only as belief. saturday5amulet, web-pra and toprich24 are
  amulet sellers.
- **Thai Wikipedia's กุมารทอง** is unsourced; its text is repeated nearly word for word by Kom Chad Luek, Sanook,
  MThai, Thairath (2020) and web-pra, so on those points they are not independent of it.
- **China Times 2025** quotes a Taiwanese "Thai magic master", 周學乾, who supplied the photographs; it reads as
  promotion. The man arrested in Bangkok in 2012 has the same Chinese name in Hong Kong's Oriental Daily ([Oriental
  Daily 2012][od]). **I could not confirm whether they are the same person** and draw no conclusion; the point is only
  not to lean on that article.
- **Chinese-language popular media** (ETtoday, CTWANT, SETN, GoTrip, DailyView, KUMIHO, China Press) report what
  "experts", mediums and netizens say; useful for what the misconception *is*, not for what is true.
