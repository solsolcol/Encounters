## READ FIRST: the short answers

1. **A phrai (พราย) is a kind of ghost, most often said to be a woman who died together with her unborn child**
   (ORST [6]; Phya Anuman Rajadhon [7]). The same word means bubbles rising in water and "sparkling", and names pale
   glowing lights (ORST พรายทะเล, พรายน้ำ). In today's amulet world some use it for any spirit at all [10]. **AGREED.**
2. **In the belief a phrai can be bound by spells and kept for use** (ORST โหงพราย: "ผีที่เขาปลุกเสกไว้ใช้"; พรายกระซิบ;
   เลี้ยงผี; [7]; [9]). The nine are therefore best understood as the dead, used by someone, not as demons. **AGREED.**
3. **Nam man phrai (น้ำมันพราย) is believed to carry such a spirit.** Every source's first use for it is a love charm
   [6][7][8][21]; one 1938 account adds that it can "cause pains in the stomach, headache, or any other ills" [8]; and
   the spirit is said to go where the oil goes, to need feeding, and to turn on a keeper who neglects or insults it
   [8][20]. **AGREED** on the charm and the spirit; the harm use is **ONE SOURCE** plus the general belief in sorcery.
4. **A grudge answered by something placed at a home is a documented Thai belief** (ORST คุณไสย via [4], ปล่อยของ,
   อาถรรพ์ [6]; a buried object that sets a household quarrelling [16]; news of bundles found at houses [24][25]).
   **Phrai oil placed at a house because of a dispute: ONE SOURCE, WEAK** (a television ghost programme's summary [23]).
   **"Spilled": NOT FOUND** outside the casebook.
5. **The signs** people tie to it: illness doctors cannot explain, a household that quarrels, losses, a dulled face,
   changed behaviour, bruise-like marks (ORST พรายย้ำ), wasting [16][17][21][24]. **A foul smell is a general Thai sign
   of a bad presence** [27][28], and the Thai word สาง means both "ghost" and "the stench of a corpse" [6]. **A smell
   tied to phrai oil in particular: NOT FOUND** in a source that could be opened.
6. **Why nine: no meaning found.** Not in the dictionary, the scholarship, or the Thai pages on the number nine. Thai
   counts spirits with the classifier ตน, and the amulet world names pieces by how many phrai they are said to hold (a
   documented "Phrai 59 Ton" [10]). Read "nine Prai" as a count: the dead believed carried by that oil, and the nine
   Pho detected. Write no line that explains nine.
7. **How they are described:** a woman's form; a small pale light that rises and falls; unseen but known by signs.
   They fall quiet before a master, shift from hiding place to hiding place, cry out under holy water, and can shrink
   to sit with a bowed head [7][8][21]. What they want, in the sources' own words: revenge for a wrong (อาฆาต), food,
   company, and release ("ปลดปล่อยผมที") [7][8][17][20]. "Looking for energy" is the casebook's modern wording.
8. **"Yin amulet" is the Chinese-speaking buyers' word** (陰牌, against 正牌), not a Thai category [14][11][2]. Thai
   speakers say the dark line (สายมืด, สายดำ), low things (ของต่ำ), or the ghost's power against the Buddha's (คุณผี,
   คุณพระ) [2][3][20]. Singaporean Chinese are "a significant section of the market" [13].
9. **Why a collector might feel heavy, unlucky or unwell (BELIEF):** each piece is a relationship with a spirit that
   must be kept up; neglected or offended, the thing "enters oneself" (ORST เข้าตัว; 反噬 [14]); twenty are twenty such
   debts. A close parallel is documented: a buyer of "shadow amulets" whose luck spiralled until his teacher took the
   piece into custody [14]. Thai masters themselves add that belief can do it (อุปาทาน [20]). The casebook's own
   warning stands over all of it: health is medically assessed first.
10. **How the clearing is believed to work:** holy water makes the thing on a person or object react and come away
    (S1·15; a practitioner's account of people who "thrash about and cry out as if scalded" [21]; [5]); what comes away
    is bound (ORST สะกดผี) in a sealed vessel and taken away (the old form is a new earthen pot sealed with an inscribed
    cloth, buried or sunk [7]; in the south a cursed object is wrapped in a monk's robe and sunk in a river [16]).
    **The black bag is the casebook's alone.** It is containment, not release ([5], verdict B38).
11. **A phantom smell is also a medical symptom** (phantosmia: NHS [33], NIDCD [34]), often a foul one [44]. This
    supports S1·14's "a fact about my experience, not proof of any particular explanation" and S1·15's "medically
    assessed, and that comes first".
12. **The canon complicates and deepens it:** in the Petavatthu a ghost's stench is its own suffering, the fruit of its
    own harsh speech [35a]; "the scent of virtue is unsurpassed" [35c]; the lay follower who "trusts protective charms &
    ceremonies, not kamma" is the one the Buddha faults [35e]. That is S1·15's last sentence in canonical words.

  spilled, or that anyone saw it; what nine means; what the disturbances were; how the bag was disposed of; whether
  merit was dedicated. Note the casebook's own hedges: Pho "detected", "He believed".

### 0.2 S1·15, The Yin Amulet Collector
- "A customer came to us carrying around twenty Yin amulets ... his luck was very low, his health was bad, and
  everything around him felt heavy. He kept catching a foul smell he could not trace."
- "It was coming from the amulets themselves."
- "we saw many presences attached to the pieces. Each amulet seemed linked to something different: some angry, some
  hungry, some simply looking for energy."
- "As the water touched them, the presences reacted — screaming, coming away from the amulets."
- "The amulets were gathered into a black bag, which he sealed tightly with sacred string and chanted over so that
  nothing could escape. The foul smell went."
- **What it does NOT say:** what kinds the twenty were, where they came from, what became of the bag afterwards, or
  whether the man saw a doctor (the lesson says he must).
- **A difference between the two bags.** In S1·14 the presences are blown INTO the bag. In S1·15 the presences come
  away from the pieces and it is the AMULETS that are sealed in the bag "so that nothing could escape".

## 1 · Phrai and nam man phrai as Thai popular belief

### 1.1 The word
- **FACT (ORST [6]).** พราย: "(๑) [พฺราย] น. ผีจำพวกหนึ่ง (มักกล่าวกันว่าเป็นผีผู้หญิงที่ตายทั้งกลม) (๒) น. ต่อมนํ้าเล็ก ๆ ที่ผุดกระจาย
  ขึ้นจากนํ้า. (๓) [พฺราย] ว. แวววาว, พราว, พร้อย." That is: a kind of ghost (usually said to be the ghost of a woman who died with her
  unborn child); small bubbles rising from water; glittering, sparkling.
- **FACT (ORST).** ตายทั้งกลม: "ตายทั้งหมด, ตายทั้งแม่ทั้งลูก, เรียกหญิงที่ตายพร้อมกับลูกที่อยู่ในท้อง ว่า หญิงตายทั้งกลม" (all dead;
  mother and child both dead; a woman who dies with the child in her womb is called so).
- **FACT (ORST), the compounds.** พรายตานี "ผีผู้หญิงที่สิงอยู่ในต้นกล้วยตานีที่กำลังตั้งท้อง" (the woman ghost in a banana tree in
  bud); พรายทะเล "แสงสว่างที่เป็นแสงเรือง ๆ อยู่ที่เสากระโดงเรือ" (the glow on a ship's mast); พรายน้ำ (a glow in the dark,
  as on a watch dial); พรายกระซิบ and พรายย้ำ (§1.3, §1.6). ตายพราย is said of a plant that dies before it bears; Anuman
  has the same of a banana tree that "died as a prai" [7].
- **Spelling.** RTGS *phrai*; the casebook and the amulet world write "Prai". **ผีพราย and กุมารทอง are not ORST
  headwords** (queried); the ghost is under พราย.

### 1.2 What a phrai is believed to be
- **BELIEF, AGREED.** "The spirit of a woman who dies during childbirth is called a phi prai. Her child if also dead
  becomes a phi prai also. She is a terrible phi much feared by the folk for she harms everybody" [7] (1954; OCR errors
  corrected). The dictionary says the same more cautiously ("usually said to be") [6].
- **BELIEF (Lanna).** Chiang Mai University's Lanna archive gives two kinds: a spirit that "ชอบแฝงเร้นสิงสู่ในร่างคน"
  (likes to hide in and possess a person), said to come from the sufferer's own line when it has been neglected; and
  the spirit of a woman who died in childbirth, who wants her child and husband to join her [17].
- **BELIEF (the water phrai).** Thai Wikipedia: "ผีพราย มีหลายชนิด แต่มีลักษณะที่สำคัญ 2 ประการ คือเป็นผีผู้หญิงหรือเป็นผีที่มีแสงพราวพราย
  อยู่ในน้ำ" (many kinds, with two marks: a woman ghost, or a ghost of shimmering light in water) [18]. **WEAK:** the
  page carries a no-references banner. English Wikipedia's list has both senses too, uncited [43].
- **FACT (a scholar's caution).** "Thai occultists believe that female spirits are more violent than male spirits.
  Female spirits are generally called phrai (พราย) ... However, in more recent times, some Thai occultists have begun
  using phrai as a collective term for all spirits, leading to some confusion" [10] (JSS 2024, note 14). So "nine
  Prai" may mean no more than "nine spirits" in the speaker's usage.
- **UNCERTAIN (terminology).** โหงพราย is "a ghost someone has raised by spells to keep for use" in ORST [6]; a kept
  girl's spirit in the pages [2] cites; a male phrai of the old poem in [18]; a woman dead by violence with her child
  on the amulets of [3]. Do not print a definition of hong phrai.
- **Already covered:** the kuman phrai against the kuman thep, and Pallegoix's 1854 gloss of phrai ([2] §1); the phrai
  pieces as objects ([3]).
- **FACT (a Singapore parallel).** The pontianak, "the infamous long-haired female vampire from Malay mythology" in
  the National Library Board's history of Singapore horror, is likewise "said to be the vengeful spirit of a woman who
  had died during childbirth" [37]. The two beliefs are separate traditions with the same root fear; the game should
  not merge them.

### 1.3 Kept and used
- **FACT (ORST [6]).** โหงพราย: "ผีที่เขาปลุกเสกไว้ใช้" (a ghost that someone has consecrated by spells to keep for use).
  พรายกระซิบ: "ผีพรายที่มากระซิบบอกให้ผู้ที่เลี้ยงตนรู้เหตุการณ์ต่าง ๆ ได้แม้เกิดในที่ห่างไกล" (a phrai that whispers to the one who
  keeps it, so that he knows of events even far away). เลี้ยงผี: "เลี้ยงวิญญาณของคนที่ตายแล้วเพื่อเอามาใช้ประโยชน์" (to keep a
  dead person's spirit in order to make use of it). หมอผี: "ผู้ที่เชื่อกันว่ามีอำนาจเลี้ยง ควบคุม ใช้งานและปราบผีเป็นต้นได้" (one
  believed able to keep, control, use and subdue ghosts).
- **BELIEF.** "A phi doctor or magician likes to keep such phi, both the spirits of the mother and her child, in his
  service"; "A phi prai may be used to guard a house from molestations by other phi or men who come into the house with
  evil designs" [7]. Kept, it is a guard; the same belief has it sent (§1.5).
- **FACT (the literary root).** In *Khun Chang Khun Phaen* the hero "has a personal team of spirits" that "provide him
  with personal protection, and also are able to attack and remove similar protection from opponents", and keeps
  "spirits to act as spies"; "Mastery of oneself gives the adept the ability to control spirits" [9]. ([2] §2.1 has
  the poem's kuman thong.)

### 1.4 The oil: what it is believed to be and to do
- **FACT (ORST [6]).** น้ำมันพราย is defined as oil taken from the body of a woman who died with her unborn child (the
  dictionary's own words on how are left out here), "เชื่อว่าดีดใส่ผู้หญิง ทำให้ผู้หญิงหลงรัก" (believed, flicked onto a woman,
  to make her fall in love).
- **BELIEF, AGREED (a love charm first).** "this ghoulish oil is a well-known love philtre. Its efficacy is never
  doubted by credulous folk" [7]; the title of the 1938 Siam Society note is "About a Love Philtre" [8]; a lay
  practitioner in 2020: it is "นิยมนำไปป้ายให้คนมารัก" (used to dab on someone to win love) [21]; a 2026 report glosses it
  as used "for love magic, attraction, protection, and amulet production" [14] (citing McDaniel 2011, not opened).
  Scholars list "spirit oil extracted from a corpse" among the old poem's devices that "serve as media for
  transferring the power of an adept to somebody else" [9].
- **BELIEF, AGREED (the oil is the spirit's vehicle).** "น้ำมันพรายตามตำราโบราณ ก็คือผี" (nam man phrai, by the old texts, is
  a ghost), a lay master of a sak yant samnak told Thairath in 2024 [20]. In the 1938 account the spirit enters the
  one the oil touches, and "it is difficult to exorcise or cast out the spirit from the person within whom it has
  entered" [8]. In a 2015 television account a woman's spirit followed a man who had thrown such oil away [23]. This
  is the belief's own logic for S1·14: where the oil is, the phrai is.
- **BELIEF, AGREED (it must be kept up, and it can turn).** The vessel is kept "on an altar high above the heads of
  those living in the house" and fed twice a day; "the spirit which has become hungry will enter the body of the
  doctor ... The hunger of a spirit must be satisfied like that of a mortal", whence the saying that the spirit doctor
  dies by a spirit as the snake charmer dies by a snake [8]. In 2024 the lay master gave the same picture: "เปรียบเหมือน
  การเลี้ยงสุนัขพันธุ์ดุ อย่างพิตบูล ก็ต้องมีความเชี่ยวชาญในการเลี้ยงไม่ให้สุนัขทำร้ายเจ้าของ หรือเหมือนกับหมองู" (it is like keeping a fierce
  breed of dog such as a pit bull: one must be skilled in keeping it so that the dog does not harm its owner; or like
  a snake handler) [20]. The dictionary has the general word: เข้าตัว, "อาการที่อาคม ของขลัง หรือถ้อยคำที่ปรารถนาจะกระทำหรือพูด
  ให้ร้ายแก่ผู้อื่นแล้วย้อนกลับมาโดนตัวเอง" (when a spell, a charm or words meant to harm another turn back and strike
  oneself) [6]; the 2020 practitioner warns of exactly that [21].
- **BELIEF (using it wrongly is a moral matter).** The 2020 practitioner: the one who consecrates it must keep precepts
  and truthfulness; used against another's wife "ก็ไม่ดี เป็นบาป" (it is not good; it is a sin); he says he will not
  give it to someone who means harm [21]. Trouble "always follows" a love won by it [8].
- **FACT (how common, and whose).** The 2024 master says it is now very hard to find anyone who makes it, and that
  "คนจีนชอบมาก ... แต่คนไทยมีเฉพาะบางกลุ่มเท่านั้น" (Chinese buyers are very fond of it; among Thais only some groups) [20].
  **ONE SOURCE** for that; it agrees with §3.1.
- **FACT (a scientist's view).** A Chulalongkorn biologist, asked about it: science knows no substance that could be
  called nam man phrai, and "จึงคิดว่าเป็นแค่ความเชื่อมากกว่า" (so I think it is only a belief) [21].

### 1.5 Used against someone, and placed in a home
- **FACT (the words exist).** ปล่อยของ: "ทำพิธีทางไสยศาสตร์ให้ของไปทำร้ายผู้อื่น" (to perform an occult rite so that a thing goes
  to harm another). กระทำ ๒: by spells to make someone "ให้รักหรือให้ป่วยเจ็บ" (love, or fall ill); the one it is done to is
  ถูกกระทำ. อาถรรพ์: rites "เพื่อให้เกิดสิริมงคลป้องกันภยันตราย หรือทำอันตรายผู้อื่น" (for good fortune and protection, or to harm
  others), with ฝังอาถรรพ์, burying (its examples there are a stone post and an offering tray) [6].
  คุณไสย, with its "ฝังรูปฝังรอย" (burying an image or a trace), is in [4] §4.1.
- **BELIEF (the oil used to harm): ONE SOURCE.** "This spirit fluid is of great potency and can be used for many
  purposes, that is, to create a feeling of love ... or to cause pains in the stomach, headache, or any other ills" [8].
  Thai Wikipedia repeats it from the same root [19]. A Thai lifestyle list names nam man phrai among the kinds of
  sorcery [4, its source 36].
- **BELIEF (a spirit sent for a grudge): AGREED.** "A man wishing to injure his enemy may engage a phi doctor ... to
  send one of his phi to obsess the enemy"; the possessed may say she "is a phi taking revenge on some wrong done or
  has been sent" [7]. A northern healer "concluded that her problem resulted from an attack of sorcery or black magic"
  after a quarrel at work [15].
- **BELIEF (a thing placed at the house): AGREED on the pattern.**
  - A southern Thai belief studied in 2026: a certain broken sacred object, buried at a house to curse it, whose
    "presence in a household can bring misfortune ... Residents of such households often experience intense disputes,
    resulting in division and fatal outcomes"; the aim is "to cause harm or discord within families" [16].
  - News, 2023: a family in the south with a run of illness, money trouble and a broken marriage dug up a bundle from
    under their floor, found by a lay master; the homeowner "เดาไม่ออกว่าเป็นใครทำ" (could not guess who did it) and wrote
    "ไม่ขอสาปแช่งนะ" (I am not going to curse anyone) [24]. News, 2021: a bundle left at the front of a house in the
    north-east, removed by a lay master [25].
- **BELIEF (phrai oil at a house): ONE SOURCE, WEAK.** A 2015 summary of a television ghost programme: "บ้านที่ทั้งคู่อยู่นั้น
  เป็นบริเวณที่มีการแย่งชิงกัน จึงมากการเอาน้ำมันพรายหรือกระดูกคนตายมาวางไว้เพื่อสาบแช่งไม่ให้ใครมาอยู่" (the house the couple lived in was
  a place that was fought over, so nam man phrai or the bones of the dead had been placed there to curse it, so that no
  one would come to live there; the page's own spelling) [23]. It is a sensitive's explanation on a show, not a study.
- **BELIEF (the oil's ghost haunting the house where it lies).** 2024: a family blamed four deaths over the years on
  "แรงอาฆาตของผีน้ำมันพรายสีผึ้ง" (the grudge of the ghost of the phrai-oil wax) after a charm holding the oil was
  insulted and thrown down beside the house; the headline's phrase for the household is "บ้านร้อนเป็นไฟ" (the house hot as fire) [20][22]. No one

### 1.8 Why "nine"
- **NOT FOUND: any meaning of nine phrai.** Not in ORST (เก้า is only the number and a lunar month; นพ- compounds are
  gems and planets) [6]; not in [7], [8], [9], [10], [15] or [17]; a Thai feature on the number nine speaks of เก้า and

- **FACT (how Thai counts spirits).** ตน is the classifier for hermits, non-humans and beings of power, and ปีศาจ takes
  it too [6]. "Nine Prai" is พราย ๙ ตน: plain counting.
- **FACT (the amulet world counts phrai).** A documented amulet type is named "Phrai Ha Sip Kao Ton (พรายห้าสิบเก้าตน)",
  fifty-nine phrai, for the number of the dead said to be in it [10]. Product names seen in the search index count
  phrai and "วิญญาณ" in other numbers, nine among them (sellers' pages: NOT OPENED, not listed, cited for nothing).
- **FACT (nine elsewhere in Thai rites).** Nine is a favoured number: the sacred thread is doubled into "๓ เส้น หรือ ๙
  เส้น" (ORST, via [4]); nine incense sticks are one of the counts used to ask a house's spirits' leave ([2] §5.3). The

- **Reading.** "Corpse oil of nine Prai" most plainly means an oil said to carry nine of the dead, and Pho detected
  those nine in the house. Nothing found gives the number a meaning of its own. **Do not write a line that explains
  nine, and do not call it lucky or cursed.**

### 2.1 What people say they look like
- **A woman.** "The phi prai may assume the form of a woman" and may appear as a young girl [7]; the dictionary's
  phrai is a woman who died with her child [6]; "ผู้หญิงใส่เสื้อสีขาว" (a woman in white) in [18] (WEAK).

- **Very tall, then small.** In the 1938 account the spirit stands "in many cases having the height of a palm tree",

  but may appear as if it had a body) [6].

### 2.3 How people say they move and behave
- **Quiet before a master.** "When he appears before her, she becomes calm and sometimes shows signs of awe" [7].

## 3 · Yin amulets as collected in Singapore

### 3.1 "Yin" is the buyers' word
- **FACT.** "In Hong Kong and Chinese-language amulet markets, this distinction is often condensed into the contrast
  between 'proper amulets' (正牌, zheng pai) and 'shadow amulets'" (陰牌, yin pai); and "the classification of shadow
  amulets compresses and flattens a wide array of Thai magical rituals into a single category, with some never having
  anything to do with Buddhist amulets to begin with" [14]. A JSS study records the term in one account of a maker:
  "'vicious amulets' known as yin pai (陰牌)" [11]. [2] §2.4 found the same: "Thai sources I read do not use yin and yang".
  **AGREED (three).**
- **WEAK.** Chinese Wikipedia: a yin pai differs by "陰料" (yin materials) and the method of making; "泰國人基本不戴陰牌"
  (Thais basically do not wear yin amulets); "正牌、陰牌不可同時配戴，且也不能放置同處" (the two kinds are not worn together or
  kept in one place). That section has no citations [40].
- **FACT (the Thai words).** สายมืด, the dark line ([2], its MGR 2012); สายขาว and สายดำ, the white line and the black ([2],
  a seller's article title); ของต่ำ, a low thing ([3], of Phra Ngang); and the pairing a Thai master used in 2024:
  "สีผึ้งมีหลายประเภท ทั้งคุณพระ คุณผี" (wax charms are of many kinds, of the Buddha's virtue and of a ghost's power), "หากอยาก
  เห็นผลเร็วก็เซ่นคุณผี ... เหมือนการว่าจ้างให้ทำอะไรให้เร็วขึ้น" (if you want results fast you make offerings to the ghost's
  power; it is like hiring someone to get a thing done faster) [20]. Thai Wikipedia's ไสยขาว and ไสยดำ (white: for good
  results; black: to harm) is mostly uncited [45]. มนตร์ดำ is not an ORST headword (queried).

### 3.4 What "presences attached" to a piece means
- **BELIEF.** The piece is a spirit's dwelling, by invitation or by binding: [2] §2.4 ("一個是邀請，一個是囚禁", one is
  invited, one is imprisoned); ORST โหงพราย and เลี้ยงผี [6]; the 1938 vessel with its spirit fed daily [8].
- **BELIEF (the model, in a scholar's words).** A master "used the correct magical compounds to trap the troubled ...
  spirit in the amulet. The amulet possessor uses rituals to sustain a mutually beneficial relationship ... The human
  worships and eventually transfers merit to the ... spirit. Then the pleased and nourished spirit helps its owner
  magically" [11]. The casebook's S1·17 is the same contract in one line: "If you receive, you must return."
- **BELIEF (a different power).** "A 'proper' amulet derives its efficacy from Buddhist merit, monastic discipline,
  teacherly lineage, consecration, and the wearer's own ethical conduct ... a 'shadow' amulet is imagined to draw
  power from a more unstable source, such as spirits, violent death, coercive ritual techniques, corpse materials, or
  unresolved grievances" [14].
- "Each amulet seemed linked to something different" (S1·15) fits this exactly: twenty pieces, twenty separate
  beings, each with its own maker's terms.

### 3.5 Why a collector might feel heavy, unlucky or unwell
**As belief:**
- **Neglect and hunger.** §1.4 [8][20]; [2] §5.7 (neglect: "problems and obstacles, easy illness", though the deity
  kind is said to do no harm).
- **Not everyone can carry them.** "because the ... amulets entrap malicious ... spirits, they could be dangerous for
  certain people, since not everyone has enough energy to subdue such spirits and follow the ritual routines. Thus ...
  some devotees might receive unpleasant side effects or suffer misfortunes" [11].
- **Blowback.** "If one places or worships them incorrectly, it may have a serious blowback (反噬, fan shi)", where a
  proper piece handled wrongly "at most ... will not help you" [14]; ORST เข้าตัว [6]; [21].
- **A documented parallel to S1·15.** A Hong Kong man bought "shadow amulets" unknowingly; "After bringing them home,
  he felt that things began to go wrong. His luck began to spiral ... everything in general felt 'off.'" His teacher
  told him the thing was "sinister" (邪門) "and the amulet was put into his teacher's custody" [14]. In the same
  report a former vendor blames "a string of bad luck" on "karmic debt" from selling such pieces. **ONE SOURCE**, but
  first-hand interviews.
- **Things that should not share a roof.** "sacred or supernatural beings, including the phi of course, should not be
  under the same roof with human beings"; even theatre masks kept at home "will afflict the inmates with 'something
  hot to the heart and mind'" [7]. An old Thai feeling, older than the amulet trade.
- **A promise unkept.** S1·17; [2] §5.1 ("holds truthfulness to be the most important").

**Beside the belief:**
- **Suggestion.** The 2024 master himself: it may be "อุปาทานคิดไปเอง" (one's own fixed idea), and a believer's mind, once
  prompted, "ก็ยิ่งเชื่อไปกันใหญ่" (believes all the more) [20]. ORST อุปาทาน: "การนึกเอาเองแล้วยึดมั่นถือมั่นว่าจะต้องเป็นอย่างนั้น ๆ"
  [6]. Scholars of northern healing read the same events as "problems of emotion, social relationships and morality"
  [15].
- **Medicine.** A smell nobody else can smell is a symptom with known causes (§4.5). Poor health is for a doctor first:
  the casebook's S1·15, and [5] §3.4.

### 3.6 What Thai masters and monks say about collecting without understanding
- **A master must be equal to it.** "ต้องมีความเชี่ยวชาญในการเลี้ยง" (one must be skilled in keeping it) [20]. The practitioner
  of [21] says he will not give such things to people whose purpose looks bad.
- **It depends on the keeper's own conduct.** "ทั้งที่ความจริงขึ้นอยู่กับการกระทำของแต่ละคน บางคนทำตัวไม่ดีอาจไม่ได้ผล หรือทำให้ชีวิตแย่ลงก็ได้"
  (in truth it depends on each person's own actions; one who behaves badly may get nothing, or a worse life) [20];
  "a kuman tends to take on the character of the one who raises it" ([2] §4).
- **The slower road is the Buddha's.** The same master's samnak keeps to "คุณพระ": "ต้องถือศีลหมั่นทำความดี แต่ส่งผลได้ช้า" (one
  must keep the precepts and do good, and the result comes slowly) [20].
- **Monastic critics** (already in the repository): keeping such things "brings no benefit and more harm", and the
  Sangha's own rule against consecrating what breeds superstition ([2] §6).
- **An amulet is an affinity, not a possession.** A former vendor: "the amulet is a proof of your affinity with a
  senior monk or with a Buddhist temple. The object can be bought and sold but the relationship ... is not a part of
  that transaction" [14]. And of old Thailand: people who keep Buddha images "as decorations or curiosities are
  frowned upon" [7].
- **The "dark" reputation is partly made for the buyer.** The more bizarre a piece's story, the more it is prized
  [10]; vendors were "leaning on claims more esoteric and outlandish" for Chinese-speaking customers, the power's
  source left opaque [14]. This does not refute the belief; it supports the casebook's question, "where did it come from, and do
  you actually understand what you are keeping?"
- **The other side (CONTESTED).** Disciples of one maker hold that a keeper "should have no worries about magical
  pollution or negative consequences as long as the worshipper keeps to the ritual routine" [11]; the deity kind of
  kuman is said to harm no one even if neglected ([2] §5.7). The casebook's lesson is stricter than much of the
  market; say so as his teaching, not as a fact about every piece.

### 3.7 How a clearing with holy water and a sealed bag is believed to work
- **Holy water:** all of [5] §1.2 and §5.3 stands (what it is, how it is made and sprinkled; at Vesālī the spirits
  LEFT; the fan is in the casebook only). New here: a practitioner's own account that the afflicted, under holy
  water, cry out "as if scalded" [21], which is S1·15's "screaming, coming away" from an independent Thai source; and
  the dictionary's ธรณีสาร katha for holy water against inauspicious things [6].
- **Binding:** ORST สะกดผี [6]; the thread as "the mystical barrier" ([5] §1.6, B27).
- **Sealing and taking away:** the earthen pot with its inscribed cloth, buried or sunk [7]; the robe and the river
  [16]; a museum's account of a northern rite in which a spirit is shut in a pot under an inscribed paper and buried
  away from the village (Spurlock Museum: NOT OPENED, §8). **The black bag: the casebook only.**
- **What becomes of unwanted pieces (documented ways, none of them S1·15's):** returned to the temple or the master,
  never dumped ([2] §5.6, §5.7); taken back by the keeper himself with a sangha dana, the rest burned at a crematorium
  by a monk [23]; held in a teacher's or a shop's custody [14].
- **Containment, not release** ([5], verdict B38), and karma is not washed away by water ([5] §4). The casebook's own
  earlier answer to a presence on a piece was merit in her name (S1·09). A dedication after the sealing would join
  the two; it is not in S1·15, so it is Chad's to add or leave.
- **For the game.** Show the bag sealed and chanted over; do not show a disposal (the casebook gives none); let no
  line promise the man his luck or his health back (the casebook says only that he "felt lighter, and his mind felt
  calmer").

## 4 · Smell as a sign

### 4.1 Thai belief
- **FACT (the language).** สาง ๑: "(๑) น. ผี, บางทีใช้เข้าคู่กันเป็น ผีสาง (๒) น. กลิ่นเหม็นของซากศพ ซากสัตว์ เช่น เหม็นสาบเหม็นสาง" [6]. One
  Thai word is both "ghost" and "the stench of a corpse or carcass". This is the strongest single support for the
  casebook's stench: in Thai the dead and their smell share a name.
- **BELIEF (foul smells).** "ในทางความเชื่อ กลิ่นเหม็นรุนแรงที่ปรากฏขึ้นโดยหาต้นตอไม่พบ มักถูกตีความว่าเป็นลางเตือนหรือพลังงานที่ไม่ดี" (in
  belief, a strong foul smell whose source cannot be found is usually read as a warning or a bad energy) [27]. "Foul,
  decayed, musty, or blood-like odors ... It is believed to indicate vengeful spirits, karmic creditors, or negative
  energy approaching" [28]. **AGREED (two lifestyle pages of 2026; WEAK as a kind of source).**
- **BELIEF (fragrance).** Faint incense: ancestors or dead elders visiting; strong and lingering: "วิญญาณเร่ร่อนหรือผู้ล่วงลับ
  ที่ต้องการส่วนบุญ" (wandering spirits or the dead wanting a share of merit); Thai flowers such as jasmine: "ลางมงคล", a
  good omen, of "สิ่งศักดิ์สิทธิ์ เทวดาประจำตัว หรือนางไม้"; old scented water: a guardian of the place or a dead relative
  [27][28][29]. Thai PBS lists "ได้กลิ่นควันธูป" (smelling incense smoke) second among "4 สัญลักษณ์สำคัญ ที่เชื่อว่า “ผีไทยกำลังจะมา”"
  (four signs believed to mean a Thai ghost is coming), with dogs howling, birds taking fright and gooseflesh [30].
- **BELIEF (smell draws them, too).** A krasue comes "being attracted by a smell of blood or other offensive smell",
  and the birth room was kept "clean and clear from such evil smells" [7].
- **NOT FOUND:** a source reached that ties a corpse-like smell to a house under sorcery or to phrai oil. A page seen
  only in the search index says so (NOT OPENED). The link is natural (the oil is of the dead) but that is this
  note's inference, not a source's statement.

### 4.2 The canon and the doctrine
- **FACT.** In the Petavatthu's "Stinky Mouth" a monk meets a ghost with a fine body whose "mouth is being eaten by
  worms and is very smelly"; the ghost explains, "I was an evil monk and insulted others using bad words"; the advice
  that closes it: "Never tell lies or break friendships with divisive speech" [35a]. In the canon a ghost's stench is
  its own suffering and the mark of its own past speech, not a weapon.
- **FACT.** The smell crosses realms the other way as well: "to the gods, human beings are filthy, stinking,
  disgusting, and repulsive ... The smell of humans reaches the gods even a hundred leagues away" [35b].
- **FACT.** "No flower's scent goes against the wind ... But the scent of the good does go against the wind"; "the
  scent of virtue is unsurpassed" [35c]. The canon's own use of smell is moral.
- **FACT ("the spiritual sense of my nose").** The six higher knowledges (อภิญญา ๖) in ORST are psychic power, the
  divine ear (ทิพโสต), knowing others' minds, recollection of past lives, the divine eye (ทิพจักขุ) and the ending of
  the taints [6]. **There is no divine nose among them.** Pho's phrase is his own teaching as the casebook reports it;
  it should not be given a Pali name. (ORST's ฆาน is simply "จมูก, ประสาทที่รับรู้กลิ่น", the nose, the sense that knows smell.)

### 4.3 Singapore: Malay and Chinese belief
- **BELIEF (Malay, widely known in Singapore).** A pontianak's presence is detected "by smell (frangipani, followed by
  corpse stench) and sound (soft baby cries when they're close by, loud cries when they're far away)" (a senior
  lecturer at a Singapore arts college, interviewed in 2016 [36]). A sweet smell first, then rot. **ONE SOURCE opened**
  (a government heritage page, a parks record and a folklore archive's entry said to carry the frangipani belief: NOT
  OPENED).
- **BELIEF (Chinese).** Incense "symbolizes the arrival of immortals and is an intermediary for the communication
  between gods and humans"; "In contrast to the scent of the divine realm, is the stench of filthy hell"; the old
  biographies of eminent monks record a death with "a strange fragrance in the room" again and again [38] (a 2024
  conference paper, with its primary citations). A journal article on smell in Chinese religion treats *xiang*
  (incense, and "supernatural and symbolic aromas") against "the pollutant load of its antonym *chou*" [39]
  (abstract only).
- **NOT OPENED:** a Singapore news page said in the search index to list, among things getai performers do not do in
  the seventh month, remarking on a rotten or a flower-sweet smell. It would be the one Chinese-Singaporean source for
  "smell something, say nothing". It supports nothing until opened.
- **Summary.** Thai, Malay and Chinese belief in Singapore agree on the shape: fragrance for the holy or the
  ancestral, stench for the unhappy dead, and either can come before anything is seen. Both of the casebook's cases
  begin so.

### 4.4 "Do not remark on it"
- **BELIEF.** Of a foul smell: "คนโบราณบางท้องถิ่นจึงเตือนว่าไม่ควรเอ่ยทัก และอาจตั้งจิตแผ่เมตตาเพื่อให้รู้สึกสงบ" (old people in some places
  warn that one should not remark on it, and may spread goodwill to feel calm) [27]; "traditionally advised 'not to
  acknowledge'" it [28]. ORST's ทัก includes "กล่าวหรือออกเสียงให้ระวังหรือสังเกตว่าจะเป็นภัยตามลัทธิที่ถือกัน เช่น จิ้งจกทัก" (to speak or
  call out so that one takes care, or notices that there may be danger, according to the belief held, as when a house
  lizard "calls") [6]. ([5] §5.4 could not source "see a ghost, don't remark on it"; for smells there are now these
  two lifestyle pages.)
- **Against the casebook?** In S1·14 he ASKS the two followers whether they can smell it. That is the ground truth
  and stays. A Thai elder would hush him; if a line is wanted, a follower's unease or Pho's quiet answer could carry
  the custom. Put to Chad; not to be invented as Pho's rule.

### 4.5 Medicine, beside the belief
- **FACT.** "smelling things that are not there (phantosmia), like smoke or burnt toast"; changes in smell are "most
  often caused by" colds, flu or COVID-19, sinusitis, allergy or nasal polyps; see a GP if it "does not go back to
  normal in a few weeks" (NHS, reviewed 1 Dec 2023 [33]). "Phantosmia ... is the sensation of an odor that isn't
  there"; a smell disorder "can also be a sign of more serious health problems"; "talk with your doctor" (NIDCD [34]).
  Phantom smells are "often described as smelling something that is burned, foul, spoiled, or rotten" [44] (tertiary).
- **FACT.** The Thai pages that give the beliefs give this too: "ยังไม่มีหลักฐานทางวิทยาศาสตร์ยืนยันว่ากลิ่นสามารถใช้สื่อสารกับวิญญาณ"
  (there is no scientific evidence that smells can communicate with spirits), "พบแพทย์หากเกิดซ้ำ" (see a doctor if it
  recurs), and prayer "ไม่ควรใช้แทนการตรวจสุขภาพ" (should not replace a health check) [27]; [28][29][31] likewise.
- A foul smell only one person can smell is thus two things at once in the sources: a traditional sign, and a
  symptom. That is the casebook's own position in S1·14's second lesson.

### 4.6 For the game
- The smell is his alone, comes before anything is seen, and is said as his experience. Nobody is told the smell
  proves anything; Pho gives his reading as his reading.
- In S1·15 the man himself has the smell and poor health. The lesson's "medically assessed ... first" must be HEARD in
  the scene, not left to the closing card.
- No picture of what the smell is "of".

## 5 · The lesson lines, and what supports or complicates them

### 5.1 S1·14 (quoted whole)
> "Three things this case taught me. First, spiritual sensitivity is not only about seeing — it can come through
> smell, through physical sensation, through forms of perception you would not expect. Second, and just as important:
> experiencing something unusual does not automatically prove what caused it. I smelled something the others could
> not, and that is a fact about my experience, not proof of any particular explanation. Third, and this is the one I
> would underline: curiosity is not protection, sensitivity is not mastery, and experiencing something is not the same
> as understanding it. Cultivation, control, experience and discernment — that is the real path, and there is no
> shortcut through any of it."

- **Supports the first.** Smell is an old channel of the uncanny in Thai, Malay and Chinese belief (§4.1, §4.3), and
  Thai has one word for a ghost and its stench [6].
- **Supports the second.** Phantosmia [33][34]; the Thai pages' own caveats [27]; a Thai master's อุปาทาน [20]; ORST's
  gloss of ทางใน, "insight by mental power; figuratively, guessing for oneself" ([4] §4.2); and the casebook's own
  grammar in this case ("Pho detected", "He believed").
- **Supports the third.** Mastery, not sensitivity, is what the tradition credits with command of spirits ([9];
  ORST หมอผี); the spirit doctor who dies by a spirit [8]; S1·17's "sensitivity gives you the signal; cultivation gives
  you the translator".
- **Complicates.** (a) The canon has a divine eye and a divine ear and no divine nose (§4.2): "the spiritual sense of
  my nose" is his teacher's phrase, not doctrine. (b) Thai custom says do not remark on a strange smell (§4.4); he
  asks aloud. (c) The case's premise, "A male devotee had been offending people outside", puts a human quarrel at the
  root. The sources agree that such beliefs live where people have fallen out ([15]; [16]: the remedy is a rite of
  "conflict resolution"). The lesson does not draw that moral; if the game does, it is going beyond the casebook, and
  it must not read as "he deserved it".

### 5.2 S1·15 (quoted whole)
> "The problem here is not simply whether an amulet is Yin or Yang. The real questions are much plainer: what exactly
> are you bringing into your home, where did it come from, and do you actually understand what you are keeping? Do not
> collect spiritual objects the way people collect ordinary things. If you do not understand what you are inviting
> into your environment, do not bring it home — curiosity may make you want to collect something, but understanding
> should come before possession. And please note this part carefully: never automatically assume that poor health or a
> run of bad luck is spiritual. Health problems need to be medically assessed, and that comes first. Wisdom and
> discernment will protect you more than any object will."

- **Supports "not simply ... Yin or Yang".** Yin and yang is the buyers' sorting, and it "compresses and flattens"
  very different things [14]; Thai speakers sort by whose power a thing draws on [20].
- **Supports "what ... are you bringing into your home".** The old Thai feeling that such things do not belong under
  one roof with the living [7]; each piece as a standing relationship with a being [8][11]; the Hong Kong parallel
  [14]; the opacity that the trade itself builds [14][10].
- **Supports "understanding should come before possession".** Thai masters: skill first [20]; intention first [21];
  the vendor's "affinity", not ownership [14].
- **Supports "medically assessed ... first".** [33][34]; [5] §3.4; northern villagers' search "normally begins with a
  visit to a modern clinic" [15].
- **Supports the last sentence, from the canon.** The lay follower the Buddha faults "is eager for protective charms &
  ceremonies" and "trusts protective charms & ceremonies, not kamma"; the "jewel" lay follower "trusts kamma, not
  protective charms & ceremonies" [35e]. And: "They go to many a refuge, to mountains & forests, to park & tree
  shrines: people threatened with danger. That's not the secure refuge" [35d]. (These are teaching texts: per the
  house rule a term or a verse on the lesson card is Chad's to choose.)
- **Complicates.** (a) Many keepers and makers hold that such pieces are safe if their terms are kept (§3.6). (b) The
  sealing is containment: by Thai Buddhist lights the beings are helped by merit, not by a bag ([5] §4; S1·09). (c) A
  critic could say the clearing itself is "protective charms & ceremonies"; the lesson's own last line already puts
  discernment above every object, the fan and the bag included. (d) "Yang" is not a Thai word for any amulet.
