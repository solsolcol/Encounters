# v27.0 · R-A · Mae Kali: her devotion, her altar, her trance, her music

For episode 4 chapter 4, *The Woman Who Rose From Her Wheelchair* (docs/V27.0-E4C4-PLAN.md; §1 there is the case file,
S1·13). The case file says only this about her goddess: an elderly Indian lady in Singapore, unable to walk properly for
years, "had once served as a medium of Mae Kali"; during Grandmaster Pho's blessing "she entered a deep trance
associated with Mae Kali", and as it deepened the spirit oppressing her "let out a loud cry and was forced from her
back". This note is what the sources say around those sentences, so that her devotion and her trance are shown as her
own people would recognise them.

**Status: FIRST PASS, 17 Nov 2026, not yet refuted.** The tracker (docs/V27.0-E4C4-BUILD.md, M1) sends this note to a
second reader who tries to refute it (R-V, `V27.0-KALI-VERDICTS.md`, which wins where the two differ). Until then nothing
below is a fact the game may state; it is what the plan is shaped by.

**The rules it was written under.** Her devotion and her trance are shown with complete respect; no practice and no
practitioner is ever a villain or a fraud; Kali is never the oppressor (the spirit that oppressed her is not the
goddess); supernatural claims are written as popular belief, never as fact; nothing of the trade (no prices, no
lottery numbers, no wish-granting). The repository is public: no private person is named here (interviewees and
practitioners in the sources are left unnamed; scholars are cited as authors).

**How it was done.** Government heritage pages (Singapore's National Heritage Board on Roots, the National Library
Board's BiblioAsia; Malaysia's national culture department), museum records (Asian Civilisations Museum, National Museum

## 0 · The short answer

1. **Kali in Singapore is a mother who destroys evil.** Singapore's first Kali temple, the Sri Veeramakaliamman Temple
   on Serangoon Road (a shrine believed to date from 1855; a temple from 1881), is described by the National Heritage
   Board as the goddess "Destroyer of Evil" who "protects her devotees", and by the temple itself as "Loving and most
   merciful ... merciful like a mother" [1][2]. The Asian Civilisations Museum: "Despite her terrifying nature ... Kali is
   worshipped as a protective mother goddess" [7]. **[FACT as description; BELIEF as content]**
2. **"Mae Kali" is the Thai way of saying it.** Thai names goddesses *Phra Mae* (พระแม่, "holy mother"): พระแม่กาลี,
   พระแม่อุมาเทวี; Bangkok's Tamil temple is วัดพระศรีมหาอุมาเทวี (Wat Phra Si Maha Uma Thewi), whose presiding
   Mariamman is described as an avatar of Uma, with Kali among its processional images [27][28]. A Thai-trained
   narrator says "Mae Kali"; a Singapore Tamil family would say Kali Amman, Kaliamman or simply Amman. **[FACT as usage]**
3. **The home altar is real in HDB flats** ("the family shrine" [15]; a 2021 HDB fire from "lit oil lamps left
   unattended at a praying altar" [50]). Its things are well attested: the standing brass lamp (*kuttu-viḷakku*),
   camphor, incense and benzoin smoke, kumkum and sacred ash, flower garlands (jasmine; neem for Mariamman), framed
   prints of the gods (Ravi Varma's imagery, in India "in almost every home") [10][11][17][49]. **[FACT]**
4. **The days:** the temple names Sunday, Tuesday and Friday, the new and full moons and other lunar days, and the
   festivals of the month of Aadi and Navarathiri as the best days for Kali [2]. **[FACT as the temple's word]**
5. **Trance has its own words**: *cāmiyāṭu-tal* ("to tell oracles through divine possession"), *cāmiyāṭi* (one
   possessed by a deity), *aruḷ* (grace; "the trance of Divine Grace" in Malaysian ethnography), *āvēcam*, *maruḷ* [17][23].
6. **How a trance looks, in the sources:** camphor lit, incense, the *pambai* and *udukkai* drums "vigorously played";
   deep yawns, staring eyes, trembling, the body twisting with the head held up, hands moving, dancing (women's loose
   hair, the upper body circling), then "clear words and utterances" that show the deity has come [18]. Afterwards:
   holy ash on the forehead, prayers, exhaustion; some remember nothing ("It was Amman who possessed me"), some
   remember fully [20][22][24]. **[FACT as description]**
7. **The goddess and the oppressing spirit are opposite categories** in both frames the case file draws on. Tamil: the
   deity's possession is "legitimate, solicited", while possession by the spirits of the dead (*pēy*) is an "unsolicited
   intrusion" regarded as illness, which the deity's power drives out [18]. Thai: deities (*thewadaa*) take mediums,
   while *phii* are "lesser and often evil entities" [25]. So the case file's sequence (her goddess comes, the spirit
   cries and is forced out) has the documented shape: Kali is the protector in it. **[BELIEF, reported]**
8. **A medium who stops serving:** I found **no reliable source** on what Tamil Hindus in Singapore or Malaysia believe
   happens to a medium who stops serving Kali, and none for the idea that stopping leaves a "space" a spirit can fill.
   What is documented: a vow is literally a debt (*nērtti-k-kaṭaṉ*; *kaṭaṉ*, "Duty, obligation" and "Debt"); vows are
   fulfilled as thanksgiving; some village festivals hold that a goddess denied her due brings affliction; Thai
   practitioners call neglect of one's teacher *phit khru* [14][17][19][32]. The case file's "matters left unresolved"
   must stay Grandmaster Pho's perception, never a rule of her religion and never the goddess's punishment.
9. **The picture for her altar:** the Ravi Varma Press chromolithograph *Kali trampling Shiva* in the Wellcome
   Collection (Public Domain Mark), downloaded and checked (§5) [37]. Not for use: Commons' `Kali.png`, which is the
   *demon* of the Kali Yuga cut from a 1912 print of a cow with 84 deities, not the goddess [45].
10. **Her music** is the *uṟumi*'s rubbed "moan" (associated with trance and, in Malaysia, with Kali's worship), the
    *pambai* pair, the hourglass *udukkai*, and the temple's *thavil* and *nadaswaram* [17][34][12]. Never give those
    sounds to the spirit: they belong to the goddess and her people (§6).

## 1 · Kali in Singapore's Tamil Hinduism, and why the case file says "Mae Kali"

### 1.1 Singapore's Kali temples

- **Sri Veeramakaliamman Temple, 141 Serangoon Road.** [FACT] "Singapore's first temple dedicated to Veeramakaliamman
  or the goddess Kali, a fierce incarnation of Shiva's wife and 'Destroyer of Evil.'" It was "originally known in Tamil
  as the 'Soonambu Kambam Kovil' or the 'temple at the village of lime' because of lime kilns found in the area" (the
  lime is quicklime, not the fruit); "It was believed that" Tamil workers of the lime pits at Kampong Kapor raised a
  shrine to Veeramakaliamman "as early as 1855"; "A temple was then built by Bengali labourers in 1881"; in 1908 the
  trustees "shipped in a statue of the goddess Kali from South India and built the main hall and shrine"; it is a
  conserved building [1].
  [BELIEF] "Hindus also believe that she destroys ignorance and protects her devotees by maintaining world order. Many
  pioneering Indian settlers here turned to her for a sense of security in a foreign land" [1]. (The same page says
  settlers also asked her for promotion, freedom from illness and fertility; petitions of that kind stay out of the
  game, which shows no wish-granting.)
- **The temple's own words** (its website) [2]: "Mother Kali is another divine form of Shakthi to destroy all evil and
  protect the Good. Loving and most merciful." Its goddess "appears seated with 8 hands", "in dark green colour",
  carrying "Soolam, Vedhalam, Kedayam, Katkanyam, Demaroom, Pasryam, Kabalam" (my glosses: trident, shield, sword, drum,
  noose and skull bowl among them); "This Badra Kali protects the good and destroys the evil. She is merciful like a
  mother and protects her devotees like children." Its Sanskrit verse for her begins "Syama varnam thini nethranja
  jwala kesa ..." (my reading: dark-hued, three-eyed, hair of flame). The same page adds that she "gives her devotees
  whatever they want": a wish-granting claim, recorded here and not for the game.
- **The temple's devotional reading of her fierce form** [3][5] [BELIEF]: "Many people fear the mysterious black goddess
  Kali. Her symbolism startles them because they try to understand with their brain. One needs to understand with one's
  heart" [5]; her garland of heads stands for "the fifty letters of the Sanskrit alphabet ... knowledge and wisdom"; her
  girdle of severed hands for karma, whose "binding effects ... have been overcome, severed, as it were, by devotion to
  Kali. She has blessed the devotee by cutting him free from the cycle of karma" [3]. [DESIGN] This is the reading the
  game should carry: the case file's "clear the karma" sits naturally beside a goddess her devotees see as the one who
  cuts karma's bonds, never beside one who imposes them.
- **Sri Vairavimada Kaliamman Temple, Toa Payoh** (an HDB town): established in the 1860s near Orchard Road, moved to
  Somerset Road in 1921 and to Toa Payoh in 1982; its sanctums hold "Kaliamman, Vinayagar, Murugan, Swamy Ayyappan,
  Guruvayurappan, Durgai Amman, Periachi Amman, Angala Parameswari and Madurai Veeran among other deities"; its
  Brahmotsavam takes a chariot procession through Toa Payoh [6]. [FACT]
- **Sri Mariamman Temple**, South Bridge Road, "has a shrine for Kali worship" [8]. [FACT]
- **The South Indian form.** The Asian Civilisations Museum's 16th-century Vijayanagara bronze shows Kali "sitting in
  the posture of ease", identified "by her attributes such as the skull cup, the noose, the trident and the damaru
  (drum)" [8]: the seated, trident-and-drum form the Serangoon Road temple also describes [2]. The pan-Indian form (Kali
  standing on Shiva, sword and severed head, garland of heads) is the one in the museum's Northern Indian painting of
  about 1800 [7] and on the 19th-century prints (§5) [9].

### 1.2 Amman: the mother-goddess tradition she belongs to

- [FACT] *Ammaṉ* is "Goddess" in the Tamil Lexicon, and *ammaṉ-koṇṭāṭi* is a "Priest of Māri-y-ammaṉ temple, as going
  about dancing under inspiration"; *kāḷi* is glossed "Durgā, as being black" [17]. The plain words for mother, *ammā*,
  *tāy* and the colloquial *āttā(ḷ)*, are close kin of *ammaṉ* [17]; how far devotees use them to address the goddess is
  in §3.5.
- [BELIEF] The village goddesses guard the human world against the spirits of the dead: in a Puducherry study, the
  settlement's deities "and the tutelary or guardian deities, indigenously called ellait-teyvam-s lay a strong
  protection over the social domain from the evil and malicious spirits and other unfortunate events and diseases" [18].
- [BELIEF] [CONTESTED in emphasis] The Amman's power is also described as fierce and double-edged. In a Kali festival of
  a Tamil fishing village in Sri Lanka, "As it was Kali herself who had caused the epidemic, when she was properly
  worshipped and given due respect, the fatal disease was controlled"; the festival turns her "from a violent,
  epidemic-causing goddess into a benevolent, grace-conferring one", and the villagers "make every effort to cool down
  the goddess" [19]. A scholar of Mariamman titles one study "From fierce to domesticated: Mariyamman joins the middle
  class" [54] (and another "the goddess Mariyamman's fierce grace", §9). [DESIGN] The case file does not draw on this
  side of the tradition and the game must not: the oppressor in this story is a spirit, not the goddess.
- [FACT] Mariamman, Kali and the karagam: in Singapore's Theemithi the decorated water pot (*karagam*) "is believed to be
  a manifestation of the goddess Mariamman, with the lime symbolising the head of the goddess" [12]; the BiblioAsia
  account of the same festival says "the goddess is thus manifested through the mediums of water, fire and even the
  priest" [16].

### 1.3 "Mae Kali": how a Thai-trained Singaporean names her

- [FACT as usage] Thai gives goddesses the title *Phra Mae* (พระแม่, "holy mother"; *mae*, แม่, is "mother"). The Thai
  Wikipedia article on Bangkok's Tamil temple names พระแม่มารีอัมมัน (Mariamman), พระแม่อุมาเทวี (Uma) and พระแม่กาลี
  (Kali), and once เจ้าแม่อุมา [27]. Thai news in English calls her "Mother Kali" [30]. So "Mae Kali" in the case file is
  the narrator's Thai idiom for the goddess his Tamil clients call Kali Amman.
- **Sri Maha Mariamman Temple, Silom, Bangkok** (Thai วัดพระศรีมหาอุมาเทวี, Wat Phra Si Maha Uma Thewi; Tamil "Arulmigu
  Sri Mahamariamman Kovil"; popularly Wat Khaek) [27][28]. [CONTESTED] English Wikipedia: "built in 1879 by Vaithi
  Padayatchi, a Tamil Hindu immigrant" (citing printed histories) [28]; Thai Wikipedia: its history traced to about
  B.E. 2453-2454 (1910-1911) [27]. Its presiding Mariamman "is described as an avatar of Uma"; Kali is among the bronze
  images "carried out in procession at ceremonies and festivals" [27]. The 2026 Navaratri procession honoured "Ganesha,
  Uma Devi and Kali", and the closing rites include "the ceremonial bathing of Brahmin priests and spirit mediums" [29].
  (English Wikipedia notes that *khaek*, the word in "Wat Khaek", is "increasingly perceived as offensive" [28]; the game
  should use the formal name if it names the temple at all.)
- [FACT] Thai Buddhists and Hindu goddesses: "Thai Buddhists increasingly have turned to Hindu deities as benevolent
  sources of miraculous assistance"; among the participants "some are professional spirit mediums who deeply identify
  with the Hindu gods and goddesses that regularly possess them"; the core of the Silom procession is "three Indian
  spirit mediums possessed by different goddesses" [26].
- [BELIEF] Thai devotees "consider Mother Kali, a Hindu deity, as a transformation of the terrifying goddess Uma Devi";
  "using fire in the worship of Mother Kali destroys all evil and bad things" (a 2023 Thai ceremony, in which "several
  attendees seemed to be possessed, with vacant eyes and spontaneous dancing") [30]. (The report frames the gathering as
  a hope for luck: the trade, not for the game.)
- [FACT] Thai words for a medium: ร่างทรง (*rang song*) and the act การเข้าทรง, inviting a sacred being to "ประทับร่าง",
  come to rest in a human body [31]; in the north *maa khii* and in Central Thai *jaw song* (เจ้าทรง), both meaning a
  "horse ridden" by a spirit [25].
- [BELIEF] The Thai categories the case file's narrator would use: deities (*thewadaa*) "pay homage to the Buddha", while
  *phii* are "a lower class of spirit who may perpetrate evil or good", "lesser and often evil entities ... controlled and
  exorcised by Buddhist monks" [25]. [DESIGN] In those terms the goddess who comes to the lady is a deity; the thing on
  her back is a *phii*. The Tamil frame (§3.6) draws the same line.

### 2.2 Which gods would sit beside Kali

No source I opened describes a Singapore Tamil home altar god by god. **[DESIGN, inferred from the temples]** The Toa
Payoh Kali temple lists, after Kaliamman, Vinayagar (Ganesha) and Murugan, then Ayyappan, Guruvayurappan, Durgai Amman,
Periachi Amman, Angala Parameswari and the goddess's guardian Madurai Veeran [6]; the Serangoon Road temple's shrines
include Periachi, Vinayagar, Subramaniyar (Murugan), Lakshmi Durgai and Bhairavar [2]. Periachi there is "Truly a mother
to all living beings", who "protects the woman from endless labour pain" [4]; at the Sri Mariamman Temple, Madurai
Veeran is "the guardian of the goddess" [16]. A household also keeps its lineage deity, *kula-teyvam* [17]. So a
plausible shelf: Kali at the centre, Vinayagar to one side (Ganesha, "whom devotees pray to for help in eradicating
obstacles in their lives" [57]) and Murugan to the other; a smaller Mariamman or Periachi picture possible; her
husband's photograph near the gods or among them (the plan hangs it on the wall; Tamil homes keep such photographs in
the shrine itself [47], and South Indian homes elsewhere in the house too [48]).

### 2.3 What a woman who once served the goddess might keep **[DESIGN]**

Consistent with the sources above, and inferred, not documented for any one household: the Kali print, garlanded; the
brass *kuttu-viḷakku* before it, oil in its bowl, wicks trimmed (her family has kept it for her); a camphor plate and a
small bell; boxes of kumkum and sacred ash; a small brass trident with a lime on its centre prong; a sprig of neem; a
yellow cloth folded at the foot of the shelf; flowers that are fresh (someone still tends it). Things from her years of
service would be kept clean and honoured, not hidden away: nothing in the sources suggests a devotee who stops serving
discards the goddess's things, and her family's care of them is the respectful reading.

### 3.2 How a trance comes on, as the sources describe it

- **A Puducherry healing shrine** (the fullest description found) [18]: the shrine is cleaned and the main image's lamp,
  "the oil lamp in the form of kuttu lamp", is lit first "with the camphor fire"; then "the healer in the ritual costume,
  lights camphor, offers a 'ti:pa:ra:tanai' to the mu:lavar ... The whole assembly is silent, the incense sticks emitting
  smoke, and the musical sound of the traditional percussion instruments of pampai and utukkai (not in all incidents) is
  vigorously playes. The healer closing his eyes in front of the [image], stands or sits squatting." In some shrines a
  leader calls and the assembly answers "Om! Sakthi! Om!" for 108 or 1008 times, throwing vermilion. "The assembly
  gathered in the centre is eagerly awaiting the descent of the deity on the healer."
- **Incense** is itself the old call: *tūpam-pōṭu-tal*, "to burn incense invoking a spirit to possess a person" [17].
- **Thaipusam in Malaysia**: "Once they take their baths and have holy ash smeared on their foreheads, the mystic
  subconscious state of trance takes over" [22]. Malaysia's culture department ties the *uṟumi*'s sound to "trance-like
  states" in the worship of "Lord Murugan, Goddess Kali, and Lord Ayyappan" [34] (§6).
- **The first time** (Thai mediums): "A person's initial possession is involuntary and frightening to new mediums" [25].

### 3.3 What it looks like

- [FACT as description] Puducherry [18]: "deep yawning with noise"; some "gnash their teeth violently, throw staring
  looks; they twist the body, keeping the heads straight upwards"; "They slowly begin to tremble ... shout aloud or make
  loud yelling. They wave the hands"; a bunch of neem leaves is put into the healer's hands; then dancing: "The women
  healers dance vigorously with their unhavelled hair disorderly flunging downwards. The women dancers sometimes rotate
  their upper body alone together with the head violently." Then "clear words and utterances": "It is the clear sign
  when the healer utters some oral statements that the deity has descended on the healer and uttering divine oracles."
- Kali in particular (a costumed devotee in Tamil Nadu, in his family's words): "When Maa Kali descends into his body, his
  eyes turn bloodshot, his face fierce"; he "doesn't eat, doesn't speak—he's in another world" [21].
- A Thai Kali ceremony: "vacant eyes and spontaneous dancing, unaware of their surroundings" [30].
- **Memory.** A woman devotee in Vellore district, after leading an Amman procession in trance: "It wasn't me who danced in
  that frenzy ... I have no memory of it. It was Amman who possessed me"; Amman there "is seen as the manifestation of
  Kali" [20]. A scholar of Malaysian Thaipusam answers a colleague's model of "an amnesic form of divine possession":
  trance, he writes, runs from amnesic possession to experiences "more complex mystical", often "fully recollected by
  the devotee" [24].
- **The end.** The Vellore devotee "collapses as the procession she is leading reaches the dried-up riverbed" [20]; at
  Batu Caves, "As soon as consciousness is regained, devotees sometimes collapse from the sheer weight, but strength seeps
  in with time" [22].
- **The tongue.** Kali's protruding tongue is iconographic [3] and on every print in §5; I found no source that describes
  it in a medium's trance. [UNCONFIRMED] Do not show it.

### 3.4 How the family and the room respond

- [FACT as description] They wait for the deity, answer her and bring her their troubles: "Immediately the assistant would
  respond to her questions. From the gathering one or two would slowly pour down their sorrows and miscries [sic]" [18].
- Neem is put into the medium's hand [18]; in a Sri Lankan Kali procession "villagers throw water to cool the medium's
  body. In exchange they receive margosa leaves" [19].
- Children are brought for blessing: babies are placed in the arms of the costumed deities (Tamil Nadu) [21].
- Sacred ash and kumkum: in a night-long Kali drama, "The Kalis return to a calm state" and then hand out sacred ash and
  kumkum to the audience [20]; at Thaipusam "holy ash is once again applied on the forehead before a priest chants
  prayers" [22], and in Singapore devotees "receive sacred ash from the temple" at the end [14].
- **Not found** [UNCONFIRMED]: camphor offered to the medium herself (camphor is offered to the image [18]); a lime put
  into her hand (neem is documented); touching her feet; sprinkling turmeric water to bring her back (Wikipedia mentions
  "pots of water mixed with turmeric and neem leaves" in Mariamman worship, uncited [52]). Any of these may be true in
  practice; none is sourced here.

### 3.5 How the goddess is addressed

The words are certain; their use as an address in trance is attested only partly. *Ammā*: "Mother" (also an exclamation)
[17]. *Āttāḷ*: "Mother", with *āttā* its colloquial form (the Lexicon's "Vul.") [17]. *Tāy*: "Mother" [17], so *tāyē*,
"O Mother". *Ammaṉ*: "Goddess" [17]. The call-and-answer "Om! Sakthi! Om!" [18]. [DESIGN] In a Singapore Tamil family
speaking English, the natural address to the goddess in the lady is "Amma" (or "Aatha"), the same word her children
use for her; a scene can use that doubling, carefully, so the player always knows which mother is meant.

### 3.6 The goddess's presence against an oppressing spirit (the case file's own shape)

- [BELIEF, reported] Two kinds of possession, Puducherry [18]: "Ritual persons and the mediums are believed to be
  possessed by the first category of the supernatural entities. Such possessions are legitimate, solicited for the
  purpose of alleviating human problems. On the contrary, the possession by the unwanted and the malicious spirits ... are
  considered to be unexpected and unsolicited intrusion of the spirits into the lives of the humans ... It is often
  reported to be a special form of illness." The deity-possessed healer then faces the intruding spirit: "She/he makes
  the malicious spirits speak out, express its origin/identity, characters, grievances and purpose of possessing the
  individual. And finally the healer orders the vacation of the spirit from the individual."
- [BELIEF, reported] Mediums are held to be "god persons ... who are wished by the supernatural divinities to save people
  and the world" [18].
- [FACT] The same study records that in some healing centres the expulsion is rough: "the healer may happen to use
  violence and harsh oral abuses ... The physical violence with a whip, fire stick spears inevitably will do harm the
  possessed individual" [18]. The game shows none of that (§7): Grandmaster Pho's blessing is his own (R-B).
- [DESIGN] The case file's sequence is the documented shape seen from the inside: her goddess comes (a legitimate, sought
  presence), and the intruder, which cannot stay beside her, cries out and is forced away. Kali is the force that frees
  her. The Thai frame says the same with other words (*thewadaa* and *phii*, §1.3).

### 3.7 A respectful depiction of her trance (what the sources support)

**May show [DESIGN, from the sources above]:** a change in her breathing (a deep, audible breath or yawn); stillness, then
her back straightening; eyes opening wide and fixed; a fine tremor; her hands lifting (open, or in a gesture), not
clawing; her head held up; possibly a few words in a changed voice, low and sure (the "clear words" that show the deity
has come); her daughter recognising it ("It's Amman") and answering her with respect; afterwards sacred ash on her
forehead, a long breath out, tiredness, and perhaps no memory of it. **Must not show:** a medium's trance played as a
seizure, as madness or as a scare; mockery of her face or voice; a lolling tongue, blood or any gore on her; anyone
beating or striking her (some healing centres do; this story does not); self-mortification (piercing, nail beds,
fire), which belongs to other rites [18][22] and not to this one; animal sacrifice (an Amman procession in the
sources involves it [20]; this flat does not); the goddess drawn as a figure (a sacred image is never guessed).

## 4 · A medium who stops serving; a vow left unfulfilled

**What the words say.** *Nērtti-k-kaṭaṉ*: "Vow made to a deity; Offering in fulfilment of a vow" [17]; *kaṭaṉ*: "Duty,
obligation" and "Debt" (its first two senses) [17]. The Tamil word itself frames a vow as something owed. [FACT]

**What Singapore's heritage record says.** At Thaipusam "Devotees who have made vows will decide on the type of
thanksgiving offering" [14]: the vow is fulfilled as thanks for a prayer answered. [FACT as description]

**What the ethnographies report (all [BELIEF], each of its own community):**
- The goddess calls her mediums: they are "wished by the supernatural divinities to save people" [18]; in Thailand a
  first possession "is involuntary and frightening" [25].
- In a Sri Lankan Tamil village, the goddess denied her due respect brings affliction, and the festival cools her into
  grace [19]; Mariamman's grace is described as "fierce" [54].
- In Thai traditional medicine, neglecting the yearly homage to the teachers is regarded as *phit khru* (ผิดครู,
  offending the teacher): "หากละเลยถือว่า “ผิดครู”" [32]; in northern (Lanna) belief, illness comes from supernatural
  beings, "ผีบรรพบุรุษ ผีป่า รวมถึงเทพเจ้าต่าง ๆ ที่ไม่พอใจต่อการประพฤติปฏิบัติของมนุษย์" (ancestor spirits, forest spirits
  and deities displeased with human conduct: my translations) [33].

**What I did not find, said plainly.** (a) No reliable source on what Tamil Hindus in Singapore or Malaysia believe
happens to a medium who stops serving Kali. (b) No source at all for the idea that a medium who stops leaves a "space"
that a lesser spirit can occupy. (c) No Singapore source on an unfulfilled *nērtti-k-kaṭaṉ* bringing misfortune. [UNCONFIRMED]

**What follows for the game [DESIGN].** The case file's sentence, "because of past karma and matters left unresolved,
the spirit had continued to oppress her", is Grandmaster Pho's perception, and the game should keep it his: spoken as
what he sees, not as a law of her religion. It must never say or imply that Kali punished her for stopping, sent the
spirit, or allowed it out of displeasure; that would make the goddess the oppressor, which the sources on her devotees'
own understanding contradict [1][2][3][7]. Nor should any line suggest that a devotee who steps back from service will
be harmed: that turns a belief into a threat to players who practise it. The thread the sources do support is gentler:
her story was left unfinished, the goddess she served is the one who cuts karma's bonds [3], and it is in her goddess's
presence that the spirit can no longer stay.

## 6 · The music of an Amman trance

### 6.1 The instruments

| Instrument | What the sources say | Sources |
|---|---|---|
| **Uṟumi** (*urumi*, *urumee*) | a "double-headed, hourglass-shaped drum" whose "deep, resonant growl ... mimics the roar of a tiger" (Singapore). Body of jackwood; skins cowhide in India, goatskin in Malaysia, tightened by cords with sliding loops; slung horizontally from the shoulder. The left head is struck with a small rattan stick; the right is "rubbed or scraped with a larger stick to produce the iconic 'moaning' sound, which plays a crucial role in inducing trance states during certain rituals"; the head is oiled to smooth the friction. Three pitches, high, medium and bass, the medium drum leading; "continuous rolling rhythms and complex beats". In worship it is "closely associated with ... Lord Murugan, Goddess Kali, and Lord Ayyappan"; its sound "is believed to channel divine energy ... and facilitate inner cleansing and the expulsion of negative spirits"; performances "are often linked to trance-like states". The Lexicon: played "with a curved stick in order to produce a characteristic humming sound" | [35] (news), [34] (gov, Malaysia), [17] |
| **Uṟumi mēḷam** (the ensemble) | several *uṟumi* of different pitches; "Traditional instruments such as the Thavil and Nadaswaram are often included"; in Singapore it "has found its own identity and is in several ways distinguishable from its roots in Southern India" | [34], [35] |
| **Pambai** | "A kind of drum or tabour" (Lexicon); with the *udukkai*, "vigorously played" as a healer goes into trance; "pambai and parai drums" carry an Amman possession procession | [17], [18], [20] |
| **Udukkai** | "Small drum tapering in the middle" (the hourglass hand drum) | [17], [18] |
| **Thavil** and **nadaswaram** | *tavil*, "A kind of two-headed drum"; *nāka-curam*, "A kind of clarionet, one of the chief musical instruments" (a double reed); together they accompany the *karagattam* "in praise of the goddess Mariamman" | [17], [12] |
| **Parai** | the frame drum of an Amman procession | [20] |
| **The bell** | camphor is offered "with a bell ringing" | [18] |
| **Kuravai** (women's ululation) | "Chorus of shrill sound made by women by wagging the tongue, uttered on festive occasions" | [17] |

### 6.2 The songs and the voices

*Kummi*: "Dance with clapping of hands to time and singing, especially among girls" [17]. *Tālāṭṭu*: the lullaby,
"usually ending in tālēlō" [17]; a lullaby is also sung to the Amman herself (the Mariamman *tālāṭṭu*; [UNCONFIRMED] in
an opened scholarly source). The litany "Om! Sakthi! Om!", leader and assembly, 108 or 1008 times [18]. In Malaysian
*uṟumi* performance: devotional chants "repeated during processions" and Tamil hymns (for Murugan; Thevaram,
Thiruppugazh), Kavadi songs, and folk forms including "Kummi Paatu" [34].

**Singapore context.** Public processions need a permit under the Public Order Act, and Thaipusam's carried a condition
that "prohibited the use of musical instruments (other than at certain fixed points)"; a 2015 court challenge to it
failed [36]. [DESIGN] Live drumming in an HDB flat at night would be out of place; in this chapter the music is the
score's, suggesting the goddess's sound, never a band in the corridor.

### 6.3 A brief for a short instrumental piece (her trance) **[DESIGN]**

These are composition suggestions built on §6.1-§6.2, not documented performance practice:
1. **The room before** (about 10 s): a small bell, once or twice, as at a camphor offering [18]; a low sustained tone
   under it; the lamp's quiet.
2. **The call** (about 20 s): an *udukkai* pulse, soft, steady; a *pambai* pair answering it; the pulse tightening a
   little at a time (the sources say "vigorously played" and "rolling rhythms"; the gradual rise is a design choice).
3. **The goddess comes** (about 20 s): the medium *uṟumi* enters with its rubbed moan, the high and bass drums joining
   beneath, the whole rolling; a short *nadaswaram* phrase above it, once, not a melody to hum.
4. **The cry**: everything stops on the spirit's cry (the cry is the spirit's sound, made separately, never a drum).
5. **After** (about 10 s): one bell; her breath; the lamp. Perhaps one held reed note, fading.

Keep it under a minute and a half; levels and mix by the house's sound rules.

### 6.4 What would be disrespectful to imitate **[DESIGN]**

- Giving the goddess's sounds (the *uṟumi*'s moan, the *pambai*, the bell, "Om Sakthi") to the spirit, or using them as
  stingers and jump scares: they belong to her and her devotees.
- Real mantras, the goddess's names or temple songs voiced as menace, distorted, reversed or slowed for horror.
- Sampling recordings of real temple rites or published devotional songs (sacred, and someone's copyright).
- Women's *kuravai* used as a scream: it is a sound of festivity [17].
- Caricatured Tamil speech or "Indian horror" film tropes (a cackling Kali, a sitar sting).
