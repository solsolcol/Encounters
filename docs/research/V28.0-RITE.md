## READ FIRST: the short answers

1. **The fight is the game's own, and the casebook's verbs are gather and bind.** In S1·14 Grandmaster Pho draws the
   presences from the air "like a vacuum", blows them into a black bag and ties it; a FOLLOWER holds the Naga stick,
   which trembles "as if it were spitting fireballs" (a likeness, in the follower's words); the student is in trance
   throughout, protected by his own Lersi, chanting. Nobody throws fire and nothing is destroyed. Master Z's own
   summary of all his files: "Nothing in this archive was ever destroyed or driven out. It was named, respected,
   bounded, and left alone." [1] So the tag team (Chad's R3: his word, and his to ask for) can stand on belief only
   if its fire **chases and herds** and its finish is **the bag tied**: never a spirit burned up or killed. His own
   words for the fire already say it: fireballs "to chase away dark negative energy and entities" (R3).
2. **The canon has exactly this shape, and it is the strongest ground the fight has.** At Uruvelā a nāga "emitted
   smoke", then "emitted fire"; the Buddha "entered the fire element and he, too, emitted flames", having resolved to
   "overpower this dragon, using fire against fire, but without harming it in the slightest way"; in the morning he
   "put it in his almsbowl": "Here is your dragon, Kassapa, his fire overpowered by fire." [5] Fire against fire, with
   "a mind of good will", no harm done, and the being CONTAINED at the end. It is the black bag's story. The Pali
   word for that fire, *tejo*, is also the word for power and majesty (§1.5): fire that is power need not burn.
3. **The Naga protects.** ORST's own entry for the Buddha image sheltered by the Naga says he coils round the Buddha's
   body "เพื่อป้องกันลมฝน" (to keep off wind and rain) [3]; the canon has Mucalinda encircle him "with seven coils" [5];
   Thai scholars state the belief plainly: "พญานาคมีหน้าที่ปกป้องค้[ำ]ชูพระพุทธศาสนา" (the Naga's duty is to protect and
   uphold Buddhism) [10]. Udon Thani has its own Naga, the Phaya Si Suttho of Kham Chanot, once the community's
   guardian spirit [12]. **A Naga-headed staff as a named ritual object: NOT FOUND** in any source reached. What is
   documented: Isan hermits "ถือไม้เท้า" (carry a staff) [15]; the hermit Ta Fai is drawn "holding a walking stick" [14a];
   and at Grandmaster Pho's own Wai Kru the Naga staffs are in his photographs [2].
4. **Naga fireballs (บั้งไฟพญานาค) are an offering, not a weapon, and they are red-pink, silent and smokeless.**
   "มีสีแดงอมชมพูออกสีบานเย็น หรือสีแดงทับทิม ไม่มีควัน ไม่มีเขม่า ไม่มีเปลว ไม่มีเสียง ไม่มีกลิ่น"; they rise from 1–30 m
   above the water to about 50–150 m in 5–10 seconds and go out whole, never arcing down like a firework [13a]. In
   the legend the Nagas breathe them "บูชาพุทธคุณ หรือพุทธบูชา" (in homage to the Buddha) at the end of Lent [13a]. The
   cause is disputed in Thailand (gas; or tracer rounds and flares from the far bank) [13b][13c]. So: the staff's fire
   in the Naga's own colour (ruby to pink-red), rising and silent; **gold kept for Pho's own wave** (the house rule
   already gives gold to him alone).
5. **The fan: what it is cannot be told from any source.** No source reached describes holy water sprinkled with a
   fan. Three candidates and one exclusion (§2.2): the **peacock-feather fan** that is in his own Wai Kru's photographs
   (in a MONK's hands there) [2]; a plain **palm-leaf hand fan**, which "ใช้กันทั้งพระและคฤหัสถ์" (monks and laypeople
   both used) [22], and which, gilded, is among the King's own regalia [23]; a leaf bundle spread like a fan. **Not**
   the monk's long-handled ตาลปัตร: ORST gives it to monks, "สำหรับพระภิกษุถือบังหน้าในพิธีกรรม" [3]. And the sentence
   itself can be read two ways (the fan was blessed; or the water was). Ask Chad for a photograph (the plan's open
   question 3) before it is modelled in Pho's hand.
6. **The bag and the string are documented in kind, in Udon Thani itself.** A 2016 rite in Nong Han district: spirits
   "ยัดใส่กระบอกไม้ไผ่ นำผ้าขาวปิดปากกระบอกไม้ไผ่ แล้วนำสายสิญจน์ผูกมัดไว้" (put into bamboo tubes, the mouths closed with
   white cloth, then tied with sai sin), afterwards burned behind the temple's crematorium [27]. Older folk practice:
   "a new unused earthen pot", sealed with "a piece of cloth with mystical characters", then "He may bury the pot or
   submerge it" [26]. A **black** bag, and **drawing in** by breath: the casebook only. Stage both exactly as it
   says, and do not explain the black.
7. **Disposal stays off screen.** The casebook withholds it ("disposed of according to the practice"), and Master Z

## 1 · The Naga ritual stick

### 1.1 The words
- **FACT (ORST [3]).** ไม้เท้า: "ไม้หรือสิ่งอื่น ๆ สำหรับถือยันตัวหรือเดิน" (a stick or other thing held to lean on or walk
  with). ตะพด: "ไม้ถืออย่างหนึ่งใช้ป้องกันตัว" (a hand stick used to defend oneself). คทา: "ตะบอง" (a club, a mace). The
  King's staff is ธารพระกร: "เป็นไม้ชัยพฤกษ์ หุ้มทองคำตลอด" (of chaiyaphruek wood, covered in gold throughout), "เหมือนกับ
  ไม้เท้าพระภิกษุ" (like a monk's staff) in a prince-scholar's words [23]; an encyclopedia page gives its meaning as "to
  guide the king's steps on the path of justice and equality" [23] **(fetch tool; that page's own source for it is a
  travel site)**. A staff, at its highest, is for guiding, not striking.
- **FACT (ORST).** นาค ๑: "งูใหญ่มีหงอน เป็นสัตว์ในนิยาย" (a great serpent with a crest, a creature of legend). หงอน is
  the crest or comb on the head of a fowl and some birds. The crest on the staff's head is the dictionary's own mark
  of a Naga.
- **NOT A HEADWORD:** พญานาค, บั้งไฟพญานาค. **What Grandmaster Pho's people call their staff in Thai is NOT KNOWN**
  (ไม้เท้าพญานาค is the plain description; ไม้ครู, "the teacher's stick", is used for a master's consecrated stick [16]).
  Print no Thai name for it until Chad or Master Z gives one.

### 1.2 The Naga as protector
- **FACT (canon).** Newly awakened, the Buddha sat through a seven-day storm: "Mucalinda, the dragon king, came out
  from his abode. He encircled the body of the Buddha with seven coils and spread his large hood over his head,
  thinking, 'May the Buddha not be hot or cold ...'" (Vinaya, Mahākhandhaka 3, tr. Bhikkhu Brahmali [5]); the same in
  the Udāna: "encircling the Blessed One's body seven times with his coils" [6].
- **FACT (ORST).** นาคปรก, the image of that moment: "พญานาคขดตัวล้อมพระกายไว้ถึงพระอังสา เพื่อป้องกันลมฝน" (the Naga
  coils round the body up to the shoulders, to keep off wind and rain) [3]. Thai temples put him on the roof and the
  gable: นาคสะดุ้ง (the carved Naga running down the gable), นาคเบือน (carved Naga heads "หันออกทางด้านหน้า", turned
  outward, set on roof ridges) [3].
- **BELIEF, stated by Thai scholars.** "พญานาคมีหน้าที่ปกป้องค้[ำ]ชูพระพุทธศาสนา"; people along the Mekong "เชื่อว่าพระธาตุ
  ต่าง ๆ มีพญานาคปกปักรักษา" (believe the relic stupas are guarded by Nagas) [10]. "พญานาคยังเป็นสัตว์ที่ทำหน้าที่ในการ
  รักษาและคุ้มครองพระพุทธศาสนา" (a being whose duty is to keep and protect Buddhism); its marks: "1) มีหงอน 2) มีเกล็ด
  3) มีหาง 4) มีลำตัวยาว 5) มีลักษณะคล้ายงูใหญ่" (a crest, scales, a tail, a long body, like a great snake) [11]. The
  staff Chad photographed (one head, a crest of flame-like points, scales the whole length) is that list in wood.
- **BELIEF (Udon Thani).** At Kham Chanot, Ban Dung district, the Naga Phaya Si Suttho was first the community's
  "เจ้าปู่": "ผีบรรพบุรุษผู้ให้กำเนิดสรรพชีวิต ... เป็นดวงวิญญาณผู้พิทักษ์รักษาชุมชน" (an ancestor spirit ... the guardian
  spirit of the community), later recast in Buddhist terms as a bodhisattva [12] **(fetch tool: the abstract)**.
  Another paper's list of popular belief gives him an emerald body and nine golden heads, and gives the Virūpakkha

### 1.5 "Trembling in his hands, as if it were spitting fireballs"
- **BELIEF: the Naga fireballs.** Verified on the pages themselves:
  - Sanook, 2018 [13a]: "ดวงไฟขนาดเล็กเท่าหัวแม่มือจนถึงขนาดเท่าไข่ห่านหรือผลส้ม มีสีแดงอมชมพูออกสีบานเย็น หรือสีแดง
    ทับทิม ไม่มีควัน ไม่มีเขม่า ไม่มีเปลว ไม่มีเสียง ไม่มีกลิ่น จะเริ่มปรากฏจากเหนือผิวน้ำ ตั้งแต่ระดับ 1–30 เมตร พุ่งสูงขึ้นไป
    ประมาณระดับ 50–150 เมตร เป็นเวลาประมาณ 5–10 วินาที แล้วจะดับหายวับไปในอากาศ ทั้งที่ดวงไฟยังโตอยู่ มิได้หรี่เล็กลงแล้ว
    ค่อย ๆ ดับ และไม่มีลักษณะโค้งตกลงมาเหมือนดอกไม้ไฟ". In English: a ball of light from the size of a thumb to a
    goose egg or an orange; red-pink toward magenta, or ruby red; no smoke, soot, flame, sound or smell; appearing 1 to
    30 m above the water, shooting up to about 50 to 150 m over 5 to 10 seconds, then gone in the air while still full
    size, never dimming first and never curving down like a firework.
  - MGR Online, 2012 [13b]: a doctor who studies them: "ดวงไฟหลากสี ส่วนใหญ่เป็นสีแดงหม่น ขนาดประมาณลูกมะนาวถึงผลส้ม
    ... พุ่งขึ้นเหนือน้ำ ติดไฟ ไม่มีเสียง ไม่มีเขม่า ไม่มีเปลว" (many colours, mostly dull red, lime to orange in size,
    silent, sootless, flameless); a survey he reported: red-pink 24.1 %, red 20.7 %, pink 15.9 %, green 10.3 %,
    "ลอยในแนวดิ่ง" (rising straight up); four people saw one start green and turn red as it rose. Other names: "บั้งไฟผี
    ลูกไฟพญานาค หมากลูกน้ำ แก้วเสด็จ และบั้งไฟปู่ลือ".
  - English Wikipedia [13d] **(fetch tool)**: "said to be reddish and to range in size from smaller sparkles up to the
    size of basketballs"; "They quickly rise up to a couple of hundred metres before disappearing."
- **BELIEF: why the Nagas send them.** On the day the Lent ends, when the Buddha is believed to have come down from
  the Tāvatiṁsa heaven, the Nagas of the underworld "จึงสำแดงฤทธานุภาพพ่นลูกไฟบูชาพุทธคุณ หรือพุทธบูชา" (show their
  power and breathe out balls of fire in homage to the Buddha) [13a]. **It is an act of worship.** A Thai biologist
  notes that "ตำนานพญานาคพ่นไฟรับเสด็จ ... ไม่มีในพระไตรปิฏก" (the legend is not in the Tipiṭaka) [13b]: folk, not canon.
- **CONTESTED (FACT that it is contested).** Before 1986 they were called "บั้งไฟผี" (ghost fireballs) [13c]. Proposed
  causes: marsh gas that lights by itself; tracer rounds or flares fired from the Lao bank; "แต่ยังไม่มีข้อสรุปถึงสาเหตุ
  แท้จริง" (no conclusion on the real cause yet) [13b][13c][13d]. Reported in Nong Khai and Bueng Kan [13b], Mekong
  provinces to the north and north-east of Udon Thani (the geography is general knowledge, not the source's). The
  local rule for outsiders, as Thai Wikipedia gives it: "ไม่เชื่อก็อย่าลบหลู่" (if you do not believe, do not mock)
  [13c] **(fetch tool)**.
- **FACT (canon): Nagas and fire.** The Uruvelā nāga "emitted smoke", then "emitted fire"; the Buddha answered in
  kind (READ FIRST 2) [5]. Those who watch from outside the fire hut, sure that "the dragon is harming him", are
  "dreadlocked ascetics": the canon's own matted-hair hermits [5]. And in the Buddha's victory verses, chanted all
  over Thailand: "Nandopananda was a serpent with great power but wrong views. The Lord of Sages defeated him by means
  of a display of marvels, sending his son (Moggallāna), the serpent-elder, to tame him" (*A Chanting Guide* §0060,
  verse 7 [4i]): a Naga's form on the Dhamma's side, taming a Naga. (The full story, in the *Visuddhimagga*, could not
  be read here: §11.)
- **FACT (the word): this fire is *tejo*, which is also power.** The Pali of the fire hut is "tejasā tejaṁ" (fire
  with fire) and "tejodhātuṁ samāpajjitvā" (having entered the fire element) [5]. ORST gives เตช, เตโช as "เดช, เดโช,
  อำนาจ, ความร้อน, ไฟ" (might, power, heat, fire) and เตโชธาตุ as "ธาตุไฟ" (the fire element) [3]. The Guide renders the
  same word "majesty": "Tan-tejasā", "Arahantānañ-ca tejena" [4i][4l]. In the tradition's own vocabulary a master's
  fire and a master's power are one word, which is how it can be drawn as fire and still burn nothing.
- **BELIEF: a thing with power in it.** ORST has the word for it: กายสิทธิ์, "มีฤทธิ์เดชต่าง ๆ อยู่ในตัว" (having powers
  of various kinds within itself) [3].
- **NOT FOUND:** any source describing a staff, or any ritual object, that trembles in the hand during a rite, or a
  belief that it "spits" fire. (Not searched to the end: §11.)
- **Honest note (MINE).** The casebook's words are a follower's report of a feeling and a likeness: "he could feel
  it", "as if". The same file's lesson is that "experiencing something unusual does not automatically prove what
  caused it." The game can show the follower's hands shaking and let HIM say what it felt like; it need not rule on it.

- **FACT (the tradition's own image).** The maddened elephant "like a forest fire, a flaming discus, a lightning

## 4 · "My own Lersi came in to protect me and I went into trance"

### 4.1 A practitioner's own teacher-spirit
- **FACT (northern Thailand).** "Before every ritual [the healer] will have to pay homage to the phi khru (spirits of
  the teacher) with a tray of offerings soliciting supernatural help ... He believes that the deity inspires him to
  select the right rituals"; another's "khru includes spirits of all the masters who taught him"; a third "has faith
  in the spirit of a Burmese hermit as his khru", and his specialty, "a ritual to remove the effect of black magic", is
  "knowledge he claims to have learnt from a Burmese hermit" [37]. A hermit's spirit as a living healer's teacher and
  source is documented in so many words.
- **FACT.** "Most types of knowledge, especially those that involve external powers, have to be obtained from the
  spirits of teachers who can be Indian deities, identifiable and unidentifiable masters" [37, note 3].
- **FACT (the south).** A tattoo lineage's rites turn on "แรงครู" (the teachers' force): "เน้นการบูชาเทวดา ฤาษี และครู
  อาจารย์ ... โดยเชื่อว่าอำนาจความศักดิ์สิทธิ์ของแรงครูจะสามารถทำให้ทุกอย่างสำเร็จ" (worship of the devas, the hermits
  and the teachers, in the belief that the sacred power of the teachers' force brings everything to success) [40]. Its
  yearly Wai Khru draws "ศิษยานุศิษย์นับพันทั้งคนไทย จีน มาเลเซียและสิงคโปร์" (disciples in their thousands: Thai, Chinese,
  Malaysian and Singaporean) [40]: followers of a Thai lineage who live in Singapore are documented.
- **FACT (Isan).** A Dhamma healer's pupil keeps what guards him, his "ของรักษา": on the great holy days he brings
  his offerings "มาขึ้นของรักษาของตน" (to raise them to his own khong raksa, literally "the thing that keeps him": the
  gloss is mine) [39]; faith in the craft "สามารถคุ้มครองป้องกันภยันตรายผู้ที่เรียนได้" (can protect the one who has learnt
  it from dangers) [39]. And [2]: in Maha Sarakham "หมอธรรมก็เป็นร่างทรง โดยใช้วิธีเข้าทรงพ่อแก่" (the healer is also a
  medium, through possession by Por Kae).
- **The condition, in every source: conduct.** Knowledge "will have no efficacy without moral power (khlang)", kept
  by "generosity, compassion (metta) and avoidance of inauspicious behavior" [37]; the healer who breaks his teacher's
  rules meets "ความวิบัติหรือความเสื่อมจากมนต์คาถาอาคมที่ตนร่ำเรียนมา" (ruin, or the fading of the spells he learnt), or
  worse [39]. Protection is not a possession. It is kept. (So is Master Z's own telling: "Real protection is built
  daily", S3·07.)

### 4.2 Entering while he stays aware
- **FACT.** Northern men reach their teachers "through meditation or a semi-trance like condition (khao chan) ... In
  contrast mo muang do not go into a full trance, which is exclusively in the female sphere" [37]. Aware, partly
  given over: the documented male mode.
- **FACT (ORST).** The dictionary's possession words are all of a spirit "entering": ทรงเจ้า, "ทำพิธีเชิญเจ้าเข้าสิงคน
  ทรง"; ลงผี, "ทำพิธีให้ผีเข้าสิงในตัว" [3]. None of them says the person stays aware. ประทับทรง is not a headword.
- **FACT (awareness varies).** At a Wai Khru, some who come round "จะไม่รู้สึกตัวว่าตัวเองได้แสดงอาการอะไรออกไป" (do not
  know what they did) [40]. The casebook's is the other kind: "I stayed conscious the whole time" (S1·20); "I was
  still aware inside the whole time" (S2·02).
- **What brings it (BELIEF, reported by one study and already in [2]):** it comes to "คนที่เลื่อมใสศรัทธา มีจิตตั้งมั่นใน
  ครูอาจารย์ มีจิตใจอ่อนไหวง่าย" (those with faith, a mind set firm on the teachers, a heart easily moved).
  "บางคนเห็นเพื่อนของขึ้น จึงของขึ้นตาม" (some see a friend go and follow), and "ผู้ที่ไม่เคยสักยันต์หลายคนคิดว่าเป็น
  อุปทานหมู่" (many outsiders think it mass suggestion) [40]. The game takes no side; the note records both.

### 4.3 Chanting in a language he does not know
- **CASEBOOK.** "my mouth chanting something I did not know at the time"; later named: Thep, the language "associated
  with higher beings, deities and Lersi" (S1·17).
- **FACT (the closest Thai parallel found).** In Surin: "หมอธรรมเชื่อว่าอ[ำ]นาจเหนือธรรมชาติจะมาดลใจให้พูด และรักษาเองไม่มี
  การท่องหรือเรียนบทสวดมาจากที่ใด เนื่องจากหมอธรรมเชื่อว่าอ[ำ]นาจเหนือธรรมชาติจะเป็นผู้สวดเอง เช่น คาถาไสยเวทมนต์ คาถาไร้
  พราย คาถาก[ำ]หราบผี คาถาถอนคุณไสย คาถาก[ำ]จัด คาถาเพิกภูมิ คาถาผูกและคลาย คาถามัด คาถาปิด" [38]. In English: the
  healers believe the supernatural power moves them to speak; they memorise and learn no chant from anywhere, because
  they believe the power itself is what chants: spells of the occult, "ไร้พราย" [as printed; perhaps ไล่พราย, "to drive
  off phrai"], to subdue ghosts, to remove sorcery, to clear, to lift a place, to bind and loose, to tie, to close.
  One district of Surin in lower Isan (a province, the study says, of Khmer, Lao and Kui peoples), not Udon Thani; but
  it is the casebook's sentence said by other practitioners: the power chants, not the man.
- **FACT.** In the north-east the folk spirit doctor was called "'maw devada' or devada doctor" [26]: the deva's
  doctor. The power he works by is the devas'; "Thep" is the same word (เทพ).
- **FACT (the general term; tertiary, fetch tool).** Speech-like sounds "that lack any readily comprehensible
  meaning" are called glossolalia; a linguist defined it as "meaningless but phonologically structured human
  utterance"; it is distinguished from speaking a real language one never learnt [42].
- **NOT FOUND:** "ภาษาเทพ" described in a Thai source reached (not searched to the end: §11); what Thep sounds like.

### 4.4 How it looks to others
- **CASEBOOK.** "the posture and hand movements of a Lersi" (S2·02). In S1·14 there is no struggle and no dance: he
  "stayed in that state throughout", chanting.
- **FACT (what onlookers at a Wai Khru see).** "ตัวสั่น ชักกระตุก กรีดร้อง เหงื่อไหลมากกว่าปกติ ลุกขึ้นร่ายรำ"
  (trembling, jerking, crying out, heavy sweat, rising to dance); "มือไม้เกร็ง ตาขวางและแสดงท่าทางของยันต์ที่แต่ละคนสักบน
  เรือนร่าง" (hands and limbs rigid, eyes fierce, taking the manner of the yant each wears); some "ออกท่าทางเป็นหนุมาน
  พ่อแก่ฤาษี" (in the manner of Hanuman, of Por Kae the hermit) [40]. At another: "มีการตั้งท่าคล้ายเป็นฤาษี บางคน
  แสดงออกคล้ายลิง คล้ายเสือ" (some took a hermit's stance, some were like a monkey, a tiger) [41]. That is the set
  S2·02 names: Lersi, Hanuman, Tiger, Naga.
- **FACT (the hermit's bearing in art; fetch tool).** Ta Fai: a walking stick in one hand, the other "pointing with
  the index finger" [14a]; the hermit-teacher of the stage: an old man's face, "a white moustache" [14c].
- **NOT FOUND:** a source describing the voice, the eyes or the hands of a man whose hermit-teacher has come in.
- **PRACTICE (bringing someone back; in [2]).** Helpers bring a person out of that state by patting the ears, stroking
  the nose and speaking to recall him ("พร้อมกับการพูดเตือนสติ"), then deep breaths, the eyes open (V27.0's verdict B31).
  It is how the follower, or his teacher, can bring the student back to himself when the air has cleared.
- **For the game (MINE, held to the above).** Chad's photographs set the register for his teacher's people: trance as
  "quiet devotion in daylight" [2]. So: he stands or kneels where he was; his eyes half shut; his back straightens
  like an old man drawing himself up; one hand comes up before his chest; his lips move; the voice is his own but
  lower and steadier. No shaking fit, no rolled eyes, no roar: those belong to the tattoo rites, not to S1·14.

## 5 · The order of a cleansing in a home

### 5.1 What is documented (no single "Lersi order" exists: V25.0's verdict B(4) stands, "Stage it as 'his practice'")
- **A. The monks' blessing of a house.** The thread is run "วงรอบบ้านโดยเวียนขวา" (round the house clockwise), then
  round the Buddha image's base, to the chanting monks [34]; hosts "adorn the building with holy ceremonial thread ...
  encircling a Buddha statue and the seats of the invited monks"; after the meal "The monks will chant prayers and
  sanctify the space with holy water. They may write 'yantra' (ยันตร์) with powder and paste gold leaves on significant
  locations, including on or above the front door." [35] The chief monk sprinkles while a layman carries the bowl
  behind him [2]. A house blessing includes the quail's verse, "traditionally considered protection against fire" [9].
- **B. A village cleared (Udon Thani, 2016 [27]).** (1) The disciples chant "คาถาเบิกบ้าน" (the katha that opens the
  village), calling what is bad to gather in one place. (2) A monk of the party chants a katha the report glosses as
  "ท้าวเวสสุวรรณ หรือยักษ์ ที่คุมประตูผี" (Thao Wessuwan, or the yak who keeps the ghosts' gate), called on, in the
  report's words, to "eat" the ghosts that have come out among people (the game takes the calling, never the eating).
  (3) The spirits are caught, into bamboo tubes. (4) Each mouth closed with white cloth, tied with sai sin. (5) The
  tubes burned behind the crematorium. (6) Sacred powers called into four posts "เพื่อมาปกป้องกันคนในหมู่บ้าน", set at
  the four directions. (7) Each household takes home a kha-grass cord for its front door and blessed stones to cast
  before the house and onto its roof, "เพื่อป้องกันไม่ให้ผีปอบและสิ่งไม่ดีเข้ามาในบ้านได้ทุกทาง" (so that nothing bad
  can enter by any way).
- **C. One northern healer** (already in [2]): trance or meditation, holy water, rubbing with sacred objects, advice
  to make merit [37].
- **D. Sisaket, 2019 [28].** Homage to the teachers; nine posts consecrated for the village's edge; about three
  hours; the caught spirits into a clay pot; then everyone takes holy water and "ดินทรายกรวดที่ปลุกเสกแล้ว เพื่อนำไปโปรย
  รอบบ้าน" (consecrated sand and gravel to scatter round the house). The master himself "ย้ำว่าเป็นความเชื่อส่วนบุคคล
  และที่ทำพิธีนี้เพื่อให้ชาวบ้านเกิดความสบายใจ" (stressed it is personal belief, done to put the villagers at ease);
  doctors had explained the deaths that prompted it. A researcher of such rites who was there called the pattern
  common in the north-east: a run of illness or deaths, a story built on belief, then a specialist brought in [28].
  (The game shows neither that pattern nor that critique: no practitioner is its villain.)
- **E. Isan Dhamma healers.** They "ไหว้ครู ไหว้เจ้าที่ ขอเปิดทาง" (pay homage to the teachers and to the guardian of
  the place, and ask for the way to be opened) before working [38]. After treating, "หมอธรรมจะท[ำ]พิธีป้องกันไม่ให้ภูต ผี
  ปีศาจและอ[ำ]นาจคุณไสยหวนกลับมาท[ำ]ร้ายผู้ป่วยอีก โดยการผูกแขน หรือเป่ากระหม่อมและปิดยันต์รอบ ๆ บ้านผู้ป่วย" (the healer
  does a protection so that spirits and sorcery do not come back: tying the arm, or blowing on the crown, and putting
  yant round the patient's house) [38, reviewing studies of Khon Kaen].
- **F. The casebook (S1·14).** Detect; the cleansing begins and the student's Lersi comes in; Pho goes through "every
  part of the house" drawing and bagging; the staff trembles; the air is lighter; the bag is tied; thanks.
- **G. A healer's own sorting, which puts the two chapters' rites under different heads (FACT, one study [37]).**
  Northern healing rites fall into six groups. Holy water and the blown incantation are "Group 3. Spiritual
  strengthening with incantations" ("Rot Nam Mon blessing with lustral water", "Pao Katha blowing incantations"). A
  different group is "Group 4. Attacking the spirits with katha", which holds "Thon Khut/Thon Tu removing calamity or
  black magic" and "Tat Prai breaking ties with spirits". "Group 5. Appeasing the spirits with offerings" and "Group
  6. Buddhistic rituals" ("Tham Bun making merit") are others, and "Hong Khwan calling back the life essence" is in
  Group 2. So chapter 4's water and merit, and chapter 5's breaking of ties with what was set on a house, are
  different KINDS of rite in the healers' own terms. (The names are northern Thai; Udon Thani's may differ. "Prai"
  in "Tat Prai" looks like the casebook's word, but the study gives no Thai script: not confirmed.)
- **H. Not every rite drives out.** Group 5 above is appeasement [37]; and a northern ritual master's daughter
  remembers food set out for a spirit as "ถือว่าพบกันคนละครึ่งทาง" (counted as meeting halfway) [33] (WEAK: one writer's

### 6.1 (a) His teacher-spirit shielding him: how protection looks and feels
- **CASEBOOK (all that is known of the look).** Forms that "rotated ... taking turns to block them", Lersi, Hanuman,
  Tiger, Naga, "guarding my body" (S2·02); "a barrier ... it could not pass, and it dissipated" (S1·16); "Thevada
  protection appeared briefly" (S3·03); "a golden shield of light across the doorway" (S3·04, a yant's, in Master Z's
  later perception).
- **CASEBOOK (the feel).** "like something compressing my head from every direction at once", then "Gradually the
  pressure went and I could rest" (S1·16); heaviness, then "the air in the house felt lighter" (S1·14); "he felt
  lighter, and his mind felt calmer" (S1·15).
- **BELIEF (the shapes Thai texts give protection).** A cage with a roof and ramparts (the Jinapañjara, §6.2); the
  Naga's coils and hood (§1.2); "เกราะ", armour [25]; a wall: the folk chant of "กำแพงแก้วทั้งเจ็ดชั้น" (crystal walls,
  seven deep) is widely printed (**NOT OPENED**: seen in a search index only; ORST's กำแพงแก้ว is the low wall round a
  chapel [3]); a line no spirit crosses (§6.3).
- **FACT (one scholar's gloss).** A northern word for such power, *kham*, was rendered "invulnerability ... magical
  power that can protect individuals from dangerous and frightening situations" [37, note 2].
- **BELIEF (a protector invited so that a rite goes well).** In the legend the Isan study retells, King Asoka,
  fearing that Māra would spoil a great celebration, went to invite the arahant Upagutta (พระอุปคุต)
  "มาปกป้องคุ้มครองให้งานสำเร็จลุล่วงไปด้วยดี" (to come and protect it, so that the work would be carried through well)
  [39]. A guardian asked to stand over a rite is an old picture; it is the student's part seen from the other side.
- **NOT FOUND:** what a hermit-teacher's protection is believed to look like to a third person.

### 6.3 (c) Sacred string as a boundary
- **PRACTICE.** Round a house, clockwise [34]; round a whole ground at a Wai Khru, "ส่วนของวงสายสิญจน์ จะเต็มไปด้วยผู้คน

### 6.7 Is a master's power ever described as a wave, a ring or a light spreading outward?
- **As a light from a centre: yes, in the language itself.** รัศมี: "แสงสว่างที่พวยพุ่งออกจากจุดกลาง" [3].
- **As a spreading: yes.** Thai SPREADS goodwill and power with one verb, แผ่: "คลี่ขยายกระจายออกไป" (to unfold and
  widen out), as in แผ่เมตตา, แผ่อำนาจ, and a Naga's hood, แผ่พังพาน [3]. The canon: the disciple "keeps pervading the
  first direction [the east] with an awareness imbued with goodwill, likewise the second, likewise the third, likewise
  the fourth. Thus above, below, & all around ... Just as a strong conch-trumpet blower can notify the four directions
  without any difficulty" [7]. The Buddha walked "enfolding all within the aura of his love and compassion" [8].
- **As a shock through the world: once, at the greatest moment.** "the earth devas cried out ... So in that moment,
  that instant, the cry shot right up to the Brahmā world. And this ten-thousandfold cosmos shivered & quivered &
  quaked, while a great, measureless radiance appeared in the cosmos, surpassing the effulgence of the devas."
  (§0020 [4b]) A cry, a quake and a radiance, spreading from one seated man: the Wheel of Dhamma set rolling.
- **As a ring: the wheel.** That discourse is the Wheel's; a wheel of marigolds stands at the centre of Grandmaster
  Pho's own offering tables in Chad's photographs [2]. A golden ring that widens from him is his altar's own sign
  (MINE: an association for the art; gold is already his colour in chapter 3).
- **As fire and light held in the mind.** Two of the ten kasiṇa, the objects on which concentration is fixed, are
  "เตโช (ไฟ)" and "อาโลก (แสงสว่าง)" (fire; light) [3]. What the commentaries say a meditator can do with them was not

## 7 · What chapter 5's rite must do differently from chapter 4

Chapter 4 (docs/V27.0-E4C4-PLAN.md, as built) showed a Thai blessing to release a spirit, with holy water, in a
Singapore flat at night. So that the player does not see the same scene twice:

| | Chapter 4 (S1·13) | Chapter 5, case two (S1·14) | Chapter 5, case three (S1·15) |
|---|---|---|---|
| What is worked on | A PERSON: a spirit on a woman's back | A HOUSE: its rooms and its air | OBJECTS: about twenty pieces on a table |
| Why it is there | "past karma and matters left unresolved" | PUT there: a human grudge, an oil spilled (Pho's belief) | Brought home by a collector, one piece at a time |
| The word for the work | "a blessing to release" | "the cleansing" | "the ritual" |
| The instruments | Holy water flicked by the student's fingers; a lamp; a light hand on her head | **Breath, a black bag, a tie, the Naga staff. No water in the case file** | Holy water with **the fan**, in Pho's hand; then the bag and **sacred string** |
| The student | Chooses to open and close his sight, at a price | His own Lersi comes to him; trance throughout; chanting what he does not know | Recognises the smell at once ("I caught it too") |
| The sense | Sight (Spirit Vision) | **Smell**, from the lift | Smell, known this time |
| The others | Wanderers come for a share of merit, sent off "with a sigh", given merit | Nine, not there by their own wish; gathered and BOUND | Presences that "came away", "angry", "hungry" |
| The end | She stands. Release, then a dedication | "The air in the house felt lighter." Containment; the bag leaves | The smell goes; the pieces sealed away |
| Gold | Lent by an amulet, at Chad's word, for one sequence | Pho's own, his alone | none needed |
| The chant | Khandha close; "Dukkhappattā ..." | The Victor's Cage; "Rakkhaṁ bandhāmi"; a wordless murmur | To Chad: the Mora Paritta, or the address to the spirits |
| The household's stake | Her health, a bar, a loss | **None in the case file.** Nobody is attacked | The collector's own choice |
| The lesson | Do not jump to "spiritual" | Experience is not proof; sensitivity is not mastery | Understand before you possess |

**In plain words:**
1. **No holy water in the fight.** Water is chapter 4's verb and case three's. Case two is breath, bag and staff. A
   northern study files the two apart in the healers' own terms: holy water under "Spiritual strengthening with
   incantations", "Tat Prai breaking ties with spirits" under "Attacking the spirits with katha" (§5.1 G [37]).
2. **Not a release with a sigh: a gathering and a binding.** In belief these nine did not come hungry for merit; a
   spirit of the kind ORST calls โหงพราย is "ผีที่เขาปลุกเสกไว้ใช้" (a spirit someone has spelled into use) [3]. They are
   as much the grudge's victims as the devotee is. They are taken OUT of the house and out of that use; they are not
   punished. (Whether S1·14's nine are of that kind, and who they are in belief, is the phrai note's: V28.0-PRAI.md.)
3. **Not a person in a chair with a bar over her head.** The flat's air is what changes: the stench, the weight, the
   light. The measure of the fight is the house getting lighter room by room.
4. **Not his sight: his nose, and then his teacher's body.** Third person is itself the difference: for once the
   player SEES his teacher work, and sees himself protected.
5. **Not the altar's lamp and the family's goddess.** No other tradition's sacred things are in play here; if the
   devotee's home has an altar, it is greeted and left alone.
6. **Case three is the quiet one.** One table, one fan, one bag, one string. If case two is the climax, case three
   must not try to be a second one.
7. **The ending cannot be "she stood up".** It is a man thanking them and the case being "closed", with the student
   told what his nose did and did not prove.

9. The household told: make merit, keep the precepts, mend what was done to others, see a doctor for what is bodily;
   and the case file's lesson.

**Must not show or say**

6. The monk's ceremonial fan in Pho's hand; any fan before Chad answers (§2.2).
7. Gold on the student's ward or on the staff's fire; white on either (v25.5).
8. A caption on the murmur; a "trance" button the player presses to let something in (§4.5).
9. The trembling staff, the smell or the lighter air stated as proof of anything (S1·14's lesson).
10. A Thai name printed for the staff, the fan or the language; "the Lersi rite"; anything of the trade.
11. Grandmaster Pho saying "anumodana", or blessing in the monks' "te" form without Chad's yes (§6.2).
12. Holy water in case two; a health bar on a member of the household; a third film of water poured for merit.
