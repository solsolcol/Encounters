# v21.21–22 · The five masters, researched again — sources, corrections, and what stays contested

Chad, 22 Oct 2026: *"make sure all the monk stories and details are fact checked and all correct and factual, this
is very important ... Make sure all monk stories are thoroughly and deeply researched first, and then refine your
stories writeups based on your deep research. They must be useful, interesting, educational, and help correctly
propagate buddha dhamma."* And on Luang Phu Tim: *"it was actually made even earlier than stated, go research the
whole 2515 and 2517 debate. The powder would have been as early as 2505 or even earlier ... don't mention [the
association] or [market matters] in the details, the details should purely be factual and useful educational content
of the monk's story itself."*

How it was done: one researcher per master, each told to verify EVERY claim of the v21.10 text in English and Thai
sources (two independent sources wherever they exist), to find what each master documentably TAUGHT, and to draft the
twelve fields; the drafts were then tightened, and each handed to a second, independent checker told to try to refute
it sentence by sentence before it shipped. Legends stay in, framed as legends. No prices, no trade, no collectors'
disputes, no "LP". The v21.10 record (docs/research/V21.10-MASTERS.md) stands as the earlier pass; this supersedes it
where they differ.

## 1 · Luang Pu Thuad (หลวงปู่ทวด) — Somdet Chao Phako

**What v21.10 had wrong**
- "Sai Buri, in Kedah" — เมืองไทรบุรี IS Kedah (the old Thai name); English Wikipedia's equation with Sai Buri district
  in Pattani is wrong.
- "His amulets" — they are amulets IN HIS IMAGE, first made in 1954 by Ajahn Tim Dhammadharo of Wat Chang Hai, about
  270 years after his death.
- "Worn ... on journeys" — the documented belief is protection from danger in general (แคล้วคลาด), and mental
  comfort and refuge (a Chulalongkorn thesis cited by the MCU study).
- "Two legends made him famous" — his nationwide fame came with the 1954 amulets (Sarakadee; Siam Rath).
- Missing: almost all of his biography was compiled and first printed in the 1950s, when Wat Chang Hai's abbot traced
  its old stupa to the "Somdet Chao Phako" of Wat Phako's Ayutthaya-era records — an identification historians still
  treat as unproven. No likeness from his lifetime exists (the gallery's picture is a photograph of a statue).

**Dhamma**: no sermon or writing of his survives; the popular lists of "his teachings" have no provenance and are not
used. What is documented is what the tradition teaches THROUGH him (a PSU MA thesis on Wat Phako, 2014): patience in
study, gratitude to teachers and parents, mercy (the sea made sweet for the men who had wronged him), refusing reward.
The Lanka riddle's seven missing syllables — สัง วิ ธา ปุ กะ ยะ ปะ — are the real mnemonic for the Abhidhamma's seven
books (Dhammasaṅgaṇī, Vibhaṅga, Dhātukathā, Puggalapaññatti, Kathāvatthu, Yamaka, Paṭṭhāna).

**Strong sources**: Phra Palad Raphin Buddhisaro, MCU ASEAN Studies Centre (2016)
<https://gps.mcu.ac.th/wp-content/uploads/2016/09/Luang-Pu-Tuad-completed.pdf> · PSU Southern Information database,
from the Fine Arts Department's 2002 Songkhla volume <https://clib.psu.ac.th/southerninfo/content/4/084dba63> and the
Wat Phako entry <https://clib.psu.ac.th/southerninfo/content/1/262707a8> · PSU library exhibition
<https://archive.clib.psu.ac.th/online-exhibition/LUANGPORTUAD/Portuad4.html> · Sarakadee, 19 Apr 2012
<https://www.sarakadee.com/2012/04/19/luangpor-tuad/> · The People (Kamphon Champaphan, 2025)
<https://www.thepeople.co/history/the-legend/55762> · Mahamakut Buddhist University on the Abhidhamma
<https://web.mbuslc.ac.th/899/> · Thai Wikipedia: หลวงปู่ทวด, วัดช้างให้ราษฎร์บูรณาราม, วัดพะโคะ,
พระครูวิสัยโสภณ (ทิม ธมมฺธโร).

**Contested, and how the text handles it**: whether the Wat Phako monk and the Wat Chang Hai stupa are the same man
(everything biographical is "tradition says"); the dates (B.E. 2125 is traditional — the lunar date fits 1581, and one
historian proposes 1588 — "by tradition"); where he died (Kedah is the majority tradition); the many variants of both
legends (only what every version shares is kept, and no king is named for the riddle).

**The second reader (v21.22)** checked every claim against the same sources and the Royal Gazette (the temple's
official name, Wat Ratchapraditsathan, raised to a royal temple in B.E. 2569) and made these changes: "two legends are
best known" is "two legends tell of his journey and his learning" (no source ranks them, and the cobra is as famous);
only the water round his foot turned sweet — "a circle about a cartwheel wide" in the Fine Arts Department's telling —
not the sea; the Lanka envoys brought the Abhidhamma as loose golden letters to be set in order and had hidden seven
(one from each bowl, in their topknots), so he FOUND the seven missing — they did not lay it out with gaps; the sentence
saying the legends are told "to honour his patience in study, gratitude to his teachers and mercy" had no source and is
gone (the sources call him a quick learner, and his gratitude belongs to his journey home), replaced by what the two
legends themselves show; the records name a "Somdet Phra Ratchamuni" who is BELIEVED to be him, so "old royal records
kept at Wat Phako tell of a senior monk, believed to be him"; Ajahn Tim "identified" the stupa as his (an
identification, not a tracing — Anan Khananurak's own account is a dream and a medium); and "no likeness from his
lifetime is known", since "survives" implied there had been one.

## 2 · Luang Phu Tim Issariko (หลวงปู่ทิม อิสริโก) — Wat Lahanrai

**The Phong Prai Kuman powder, dated by a record printed in his lifetime.** The commemorative book for his 96th
birthday and the new dining hall (16 June 1975, four months before his death; his biography from the records of the
temple's lay steward, Sai Kaewsawang, and a chapter on his amulets by Chinaphon Suksathit) says of the powder:
"ท่านทำเมื่อสิบกว่าปีมาแล้ว" — he made it more than ten years earlier: before about 1965, by the late 1950s to early
1960s. B.E. 2505 (1962) sits inside that window, which is how the text can say "around B.E. 2505 or earlier" — Chad's
dating, supported by the earliest record there is. The same chapter says the first powder pieces were pure-powder
images, later ground and mixed into the Khun Paen, Pidta and Somdet; and that by 1975 he had stopped making it:
"ท่านบอกว่าที่ทำเพราะตอนนั้นยังร้อนวิชาอยู่ เรียนรู้แล้วก็อยากลอง แต่เดี๋ยวนี้ไม่เอาแล้ว" (he made it because the
knowledge was still "hot" in him — he had learned it and wanted to try it — but now he wants none of it). The Khun
Paen pressed with the powder are placed "in the early 1970s", which holds under every account of their making; the
text names no year for them and says nothing of how they are dated or traded. The second reader (v21.22) checked the
three books from their page scans: "สิบกว่าปีมาแล้ว" from a book finished in May 1975 is B.E. 2503–2507 at the usual
reading of สิบกว่า (11–15), so KNOWN FOR keeps "around B.E. 2505 or earlier" as an estimate the record supports, and the
story says "by the mid-1960s", the most the record itself says. His words are given as the book reports them ("the book
reports him saying"), not as a quotation of his own. One later account goes further back — a Thairath column of 11 Apr
2023 quotes an unnamed disciple on a first, small making for healers' knives in B.E. 2489 (1946), and Chinaphon's 2000
book tells the knives without a date — a single late source, so left out of the text. The opposing claims that the
powder was first made in B.E. 2515 are contradicted by the 1975 book.

**What v21.10 had wrong**
- "The builder of Wat Lahanrai" — its founder was Luang Phu Sang Thao (the 1975 book: "หลวงปู่สังข์องค์นี้เป็นผู้
  ก่อตั้งวัดละหารไร่"); Luang Phu Tim was a later abbot who developed it.
- "Wat Lahanrai ... became his home" after his wanderings — he was ORDAINED there (7 June 1906) and RETURNED to it.
- "Solitary" — a mistranslation of สันโดษ, which means content with little; the 1975 book says he sat smiling to
  receive visitors all the time.
- "Younger relative of Luang Phu Sang Thao" — the 1975 book calls Sang Thao his own grandfather (the text attributes
  this to the book; one weak compilation says maternal uncle).
- "Some of the most sought-after in Thailand" — market talk, removed.

**Dhamma** (from the 1975 book, recorded in his lifetime): one meal a day for about fifty years, vegetarian, a room
with an old mat and a pillow; every night, even when ill, chanting and insight meditation from midnight until four or
five; told no one of his titles. His title, Phawanaphirat, is bhāvanā + abhirata — "one who delights in meditation"
(Pali Text Society dictionary). And his own turning away from the powder he made.

**Strong sources**: the 1975 book <https://app.box.com/v/lptim96years> · the 1983 royal cremation volume
<http://palungjit.org/attachments/lp-tim-3-pdf.89294/> · Chinaphon Suksathit's 2000 book on the Khun Paen ·
Thai Wikipedia <https://th.wikipedia.org/wiki/พระครูภาวนาภิรัต_(ทิม_อิสริโก)> · PTS dictionary, abhirata
<https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/pali_query.py?qs=abhirata&searchhws=yes>.

**Contested**: the ordination date's lunar details do not match 7 June 1906 (the text says only "1906"); the number of
rains at Wat Na Matum (2 or 3; omitted); the place of death (weakly sourced; omitted); the exact years of the Khun Paen
batches (the text says "the early 1970s").

## 3 · Somdej Toh (สมเด็จพระพุฒาจารย์ (โต พฺรหฺมรํสี))

**What v21.10 had wrong**
- "Refining and spreading the Chinabanchon" — the chant is older than him (in Lanna rites; its origin, Lanna or Sri
  Lanka, is unresolved — Rassamee Maneenil's Chulalongkorn thesis, 2006); his editing of it is tradition. Now: "a
  protective chant older than him, yet bound up with his name".
- "First of the five most prized" — a collectors' ranking of the 1950s; now "the most famous of all Thai amulets"
  (Thai Wikipedia's own wording).
- "King Mongkut, who valued his counsel" — no evidence; what the earliest biography records is that he taught the
  boy-novice prince (1817).
- "Tha Ruea" as fact — most accounts, but the earliest biography says Bang Khun Phrom (now "by most accounts").
- "Plain" teaching — undocumented; dropped.

**Dhamma** (the earliest biography, Phraya Thipkosa's, researched from 1930 — hagiography, attributed as such): the
anger sermon (asked where anger first arises, he feigned sleep until his questioner threw a spittoon, then answered
that anger has no power over its owner unless the owner is foolish and lets mindfulness slip); the charnel-ground line
at Wat Saket, "อย่าอวดกล้ากับผี อย่าอวดดีกับความตาย" — do not flaunt your courage before ghosts, nor your pride before
death; the 84,000 tablets matching the 84,000 sections of the Dhamma (Ānanda's verse, Thag 17.3); the giant Buddhas
set up so people could easily recall the Buddha; and what the Chinabanchon itself teaches — the Buddha on the head, the
Dhamma in the eyes, the Sangha in the chest: protection as refuge in the Triple Gem.

**Strong sources**: Thipkosa's biography, Thai Wikisource
<https://th.wikisource.org/wiki/ประวัติสมเด็จพระพุฒาจารย์_(โต_พฺรหฺมรํสี)> · Baker & Pasuk, Southeast Asian Studies
2(2), 2013 <https://englishkyoto-seas.org/wp-content/uploads/SEAS_0202_Baker-and-Pasuk.pdf> · Rassamee Maneenil, MA
thesis, Chulalongkorn 2006 <https://digital.car.chula.ac.th/chulaetd/24525> · SuttaCentral Thag 17.3
<https://suttacentral.net/thag17.3/en/sujato> · Silpakorn University temple database (Wat Intharawihan) · Naris
Charassatyawong, the101.world, 2023 <https://www.the101.world/somdej-to-nakasat-12/>.

**Contested**: birth year and place (Thipkosa: 1776 at Bang Khun Phrom, though his own text gives 84 years at death,
which fits only 1788 — the accepted 17 April 1788 is kept, the place hedged); ordination places; the father (legend,
"no record proves it"); the death place (one compilation says Wat Rakhang); the Chinabanchon's composer.

**The second reader (v21.22)** read Thipkosa's raw text and Wat Rakhang's own biography and made five changes: the
death place is "by most accounts" Wat Intharawihan, because Wat Rakhang's official biography says its own chanting hall
(the one contemporary record, Prince Pavares's diary, gives only the date); the anger sermon now gives his actual answer
— anger arises at the six sense doors when they meet what displeases ("โทโสเกิดในทวาร ๖ เพราะถูกกระทบกระเทือนสิ่งที่
เป็นอนิฐารมณ์") and has no power over its owner unless the owner is foolish and lets mindfulness slip (AN 1.12 agrees);
"refused royal rank" is "refused high monastic rank", since "royal" right after "his father was royal" reads as princely
rank; the 84,000 tablets match "the sections of the Dhamma" (Thipkosa's พระธรรมขันธ์; Thag 17.3 counts 82,000 from the
Buddha and 2,000 from monks); the birthplace adds "though his earliest biography says Bangkok"; and he was a monk "at
about twenty" (1807 or 1808 by different accounts).

## 4 · Luang Pu Mun Bhuridatto (หลวงปู่มั่น ภูริทตฺโต)

**What v21.10 had wrong or loose**
- "About twelve years" in the north — eleven, 1929–1940 (Maha Bua; the Phrao district record; the Wat Chedi Luang
  history); only Thai Wikipedia says twelve.
- "Ordained ... at Wat Liap" — contested: the 1950 cremation-volume biography says Wat Si Thong (now Wat Si Ubon
  Rattanaram), Maha Bua says Wat Liap. The text now says he joined Ajahn Sao at Wat Liap and was ordained on 12 June
  1893 — true under both.
- "There he met Ajahn Sao" — not established (before or after ordination); dropped.
- "Wat Pa Sutthawat" as his temple — he called himself a forest monk without a fixed residence; his last years were at
  Wat Pa Ban Nong Phue; he was carried to Wat Pa Sutthawat to die.
- "Born Mun Kaenkaew" — surnames came in 1913; "born into the Kaenkaew family".

**Dhamma**: the radiant mind — "pabhassaram idaṃ cittaṃ" (AN 1.49), which his Muttodaya opens with: the mind is
radiant by nature and defilements are passing visitors, like clouds across the sun; his "Buddho, Dhammo, Sangho" given
to hill villagers who asked for a charm against ghosts (Maha Bua: unaware they had been given a meditation subject); no
amulets (the 1950 volume: no charms, no takrut, no trade in sacred objects; holy water given reluctantly — "whether it
works depends on your conduct"); his last request, to be carried to the town so that animals would not be slaughtered
to feed his funeral crowds; generosity, virtue and meditation as "the taproot of being human".

**Strong sources**: Maha Bua, *Venerable Ācariya Mun Bhūridatta Thera: A Spiritual Biography* (2003)
<https://www.abhayagiri.org/media/books/maha_boowa_acariya_mun_a_spiritual_biography.epub> · the 2020 compilation
reprinting the 1950 cremation volume <https://grudhamma.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/book82.pdf> · Thanissaro
Bhikkhu, *A Heart Released* <https://www.dhammatalks.org/books/HeartReleased/Section0003.html> and *The Customs of the
Noble Ones* <https://www.accesstoinsight.org/lib/authors/thanissaro/customs.html> · The Nation on UNESCO, 27 Nov 2019
<https://www.nationthailand.com/news/30378815>.

**Contested**: the zodiac year (omitted); 79 or 80 years (79, Western); the attainment claims (devotional; omitted);
"founding" the tradition (Kamala Tiyavanich shows regional forest traditions before them — the text follows Thanissaro
and Maha Bua: "with his teacher Ajahn Sao, founding").

**The second reader (v21.22)** found one sentence overstated: "Amulets were not his way". His attendant Luang Ta
Thongkham's memoir (quoted in Post Today, 5 Jul 2015) says that in wartime at Nong Phue, asked by villagers, he had
takrut and yantra cloths made for about fifteen days and then stopped them as a danger to the Vinaya; the Nong Phue
memoirs in the 2020 compilation have him call such things "ของภายนอก" — external things — and give the villagers
"นะโม วิมุตตานัง นะโม วิมุตติยา" to recite instead. The story now says that, in his words. The ghost story's "charm" is
a "spell" (the biography's "magic formula"), and Story1 is worded to be true under both accounts of where and in which
order he met Ajahn Sao and was ordained. Note for anyone checking dates: Maha Bua's English edition gives his death as
"November 10, 1949" — the night of 10/11 November by Thai reckoning; 11 November (2.23 a.m., the 1950 biography) is
right.

## 5 · Luang Pu Phiboon (หลวงปู่พิบูลย์) — Wat Phra Thaen, Ban Daeng, Udon Thani

**Identity confirmed**: no other prominent "Luang Pu Phiboon"; no monastic name is recorded ("ไม่ปรากฏนามฉายา").

**What v21.10 had wrong or loose**
- "Beside an ancient stone altar" — he founded it on an ABANDONED TEMPLE SITE whose old laterite Buddha pedestal stood
  without an image, and named the temple for it ("Phra Thaen", the holy pedestal).
- "Believed born under King Mongkut" — one writer's presumption (The Isaan Record); Silpa Wattanatham: no birth date
  was recorded. Now "some writers place it".
- The detention was incomplete: both state and Sangha officials; a second cause, his Lao-style customs, during the
  reform of the Isan Sangha; an earlier detention in the late 1920s; no rebellion ever proved against him.
- Missing: his documented teaching.

**Dhamma** (the temple's memorial-book biography, from his disciple Luang Pu Nu's palm-leaf account): give up drink and
gambling, keep the precepts, chant morning and evening, and rely on the Triple Gem rather than on spirit shrines
(its own words are stronger: tear the spirit shrines down and hold to the Dhamma alone); and his life: generosity,
virtue and loving-kindness as a layman, a rice bank and a buffalo bank for the poor.

**Strong sources**: Silpa Wattanatham (เชิดชาย บุตดี, 2013) via <https://today.line.me/th/v3/article/1DX2oRE> · The
Isaan Record <https://theisaanrecord.co/2018/02/25/historical-memories-of-thai-laos-politics/> and
<https://theisaanrecord.co/2021/06/15/phi-bun-rebellion-13/> · the Community Development Department's Phibun Rak page
<https://phibunrak.cdd.go.th/th/content/page/index/id/16> · the Ministry of Culture database
<http://www.m-culture.in.th/album/view/196286/> · the temple's memorial book (weak for miracles, primary for his
teaching) <https://dhammakaya.tv/ประวัติหลวงปู่พิบูลย์-2/>.

**Contested**: the birth (unrecorded); the death date's calendar (1946, under Rama VIII, is the best reading); "around
1900" for the founding; how many detentions ("more than once").

**The second reader (v21.22)** found no factual error — every statement traces to at least one of these sources, most
to two or three — and made four changes: "no one recorded" his birth is "no record of his birth date survives"; his
teaching ends "take refuge in the Triple Gem, not in spirit shrines" (closer to the temple's record and to the
doctrine) and the record "recalls" it; the detentions are "more than once" (twice in the usual account, three times by
the present abbot's); and the district is dated both ways — a minor district from 1992 (Royal Gazette, 22 April 1992,
three subdistricts of Nong Han with its seat at Ban Daeng) and a full one from 1997 (gazette of 26 September 1997) —
since Silpa gives only 1997. The temple's legend that Bangkok found him guilty before the Ko Si Chang ordeal is left to
the beliefs paragraph; the life follows the historians, who found nothing proved.

**For Chad, outside the gallery**: the episode-1 item text for the Phiboon amulet ("Second Batch ... Made by Luang Pu
PhiBoon") — the sources say the only coin made in his lifetime is the teardrop coin of about 1942 (B.E. 2485); later
issues were made by disciples after his death. Put to him; not changed.

## 6 · What devotees believe — the fourth paragraph (v21.22)

Chad, 22 Oct 2026: *"Supernatural details and abilities of each monk can still be included in their stories, but note
them as popular belief. These details can increase intrigue in their stories."* Each master's page carries one
paragraph of the wonders told of him, every clause framed as belief ("it is told", "devotees believe", "his biography
tells", "villagers said"). Chosen for being widely told and for not touching the trade: no prices, no lottery numbers,
no wish-granting, no claims about what an amulet is worth, and nothing that repeats the life paragraphs. Each was
drafted by the master's researcher with its sources and checked against them.

**Luang Pu Thuad** — the plague in Ayutthaya ended with blessed water (the PSU Southern Information entry, from the Fine
Arts Department's 2002 Songkhla volume: "ทำน้ำพระพุทธมนต์ให้กรรมการเมืองนำไปประพรมทั่วพระนครโรคห่าก็หายขาด"; Sarakadee
p.3); the night he vanished from Wat Phako with a novice, two lights circling the temple three times and drifting south
(the PSU entry; Sarakadee p.4 tells it as ONE light on a full-moon night drifting south-east — the text keeps the PSU
telling and names no number of rounds); honoured as a bodhisatta (the PSU library exhibition, after Walailak Songsiri,
วัฒนธรรม 52(1), 2013; Sarakadee p.4, his worship chant "นะโมโพธิสัตโต…"), some as the future Buddha Maitreya (Sarakadee
p.3 — the people round Songkhla Lake, hence "some"); believed to come in dreams and visions (Sarakadee p.2–3); the
FIGURE on the first amulets — an old monk seated cross-legged on a lotus, seen in a vision — and their NAME, received
in meditation, are said to have come that way (Sarakadee p.2; Thai Wikipedia on Ajahn Tim: "ตามภาพนิมิต"); his image
believed to shield its wearer (Thai Wikipedia หลวงปู่ทวด) and guns that would not fire (Sarakadee p.2: a bank robbery in
Yala, a letter from Chumphon of 1956). The second reader (v21.22) made two changes: "the face and name on the first
amulets" is "the figure on the first amulets, and their name" (the vision was of a whole figure, and no source says
the name is printed on them), and "Above all, they believe his image shields" lost its "above all" (no source ranks
protection first). The MCU study and Komchadluek support none of the wonders; they are sources for the opening
paragraph only.

**Luang Phu Tim** — the powder made out of compassion: the spirits of the untimely dead invited to share in merit
rather than wander, said to dwell in the Khun Paen and guard their wearers (Thairath 28 Mar 2023, หมอกฤษณ์ คอนเฟิร์ม;
Thairath 16 Apr 2025 — "ขอให้มาร่วมบุญกันด้วยความเต็มใจ"; web-pra 2013); charm and goodwill, metta mahaniyom (the 1975
book, p.๖๙: "ผงนี้จัดเป็นผงเมตตามหานิยมอย่างสูง"); danger turned aside (g-pra 2007; the 1975 book pp.๕–๖); fire that
flared in the mortar and only he could quench (Thairath 2025; Thairath 8 Dec 2022, "ตำนานไฟลุกครก"); cameras that would
not work on him without his leave (the 1975 book p.๗; the 1983 cremation volume p.15); seen on alms round in Chonburi
while at his temple (the 1975 book p.๕, from the steward Sai Kaewsawang's record). Left out: the love-magic use of
the scraped powder (the 1975 book p.๖๙ — it describes harm to women), a single columnist's "best amulet in the three
worlds", and the spirits Luang Pu Du of Wat Sakae is said to have seen (its telling drags in the moulds dispute).

**Somdej Toh** — Mae Nak: at dusk by her grave at Wat Mahabut he called up her spirit, and wore a yantra-inscribed
piece of her forehead bone as a waist clasp, after which her haunting died down (the 1930 Thipkosa biography, part 10:
"พอค่ำท่านก็ไปนั่งอยู่ปากหลุม … ปีศาจในพระโขนงก็หายกำเริบซาลง" — she still came out now and then to tease a novice; the
popular ending, that he subdued her for good, is Thai Wikipedia แม่นากพระโขนง's; the earliest Mae Nak account, K.S.R.
Kulap's of 1899, does not involve him); "perhaps Thailand's most famous ghost" (the Thailand Foundation: "Thailand's Most
Famous Ghost (Love) Story"; AP, 31 Oct 2024: "one of Thailand's most famous pieces of folklore" — Thai Wikipedia says
only well known); the tiger that sat watching over him all night where he lay across its path, near Trat (Thipkosa,
parts 7–8); his tablets believed to guard against accidents, sickness, sorcery and ghosts (Thai Wikipedia
พระสมเด็จวัดระฆัง, which states it as fact — the text says "devotees believe"; Thanissaro's "Legends of Somdet Toh"); the
Chinabanchon believed to shield those who chant it regularly (Thairath, 21 Feb 2023: "ผู้ใดได้สวดภาวนาพระคาถานี้เป็น
ประจำสม่ำเสมอ"; Sanook; the Chulalongkorn thesis); the well at Wat Intharawihan whose holy water pilgrims still collect
against evil spirits (Kapook: visitors bring bags or bottles to take it home; MThai; the Silpakorn temple database;
McDaniel via Erick White's review — the well has stood inside a building since 1988, its water mixed with holy water

**Luang Pu Mun** — from Ajahn Maha Bua's biography (Forest Dhamma Books, 2003): devas came after midnight to hear him

**Luang Pu Phiboon** — sunk in the sea off Ko Si Chang for seven days and raised alive, his robes dry (the temple's

## 7 · Why each is legendary — the opening paragraph (v21.22)

Chad, 22 Oct 2026: *"It is important to also note the significance of each monk, they are all legendary in their own
rights and the description should explain that too."* Each page now OPENS on a paragraph that says, in plain words, why
this man is revered — his standing, how and where he is honoured today, what he left behind — before the life is told.
Every ranking is the source's own and hedged as the source hedges it ("among", "perhaps", "often"); nothing is about
amulets' worth or the trade. Each was drafted by the master's researcher and then checked by a second reader.

**Luang Pu Thuad** — "among the most revered monks in Thailand" (Sarakadee p.1: "เป็นที่เคารพบูชามากที่สุดรูปหนึ่งของ

Thai one, so this pairing is the page's weakest claim, kept as the Wikipedias' own); honoured especially in the south
and by Chinese Buddhists in Malaysia, Singapore and Indonesia, who cross the border to Wat Chang Hai and Wat Phako (the
