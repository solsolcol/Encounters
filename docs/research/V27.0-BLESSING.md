## READ FIRST: the short answers

1. **The casebook gives Grandmaster Pho a method of his own, and the game should use it first** [1]: chanting and
   sprinkling holy water "with a fan blessed in the traditional Thai way", the presences "screaming, coming away" as the
   water touches them (S1·15); drawing presences "from the air, like a vacuum" and blowing them into a black bag sealed
   with sacred string (S1·14, S1·15); a protective ritual and "a barrier" (S1·16); murmured words in the Thep language
   and a promise to "settle whatever fairness it believed it was owed" (S1·17). For THIS case the casebook says only
   "He performed a blessing to release the spirit and clear the karma." The student's part, in the casebook's own
   words: "My part was small; I only held the holy water" (S1·16).
2. **Holy water (nam mon, น้ำมนต์)** is water that has been through a Buddhist rite, to be bathed in, drunk or sprinkled
   [4]; in Thai practice it is made by chanting the paritta over it, the Ratana Sutta always among them [5], the altar's
   candles put out at a fixed line of that sutta [7], and wax from a candle dripped into the water by a monk [8]. It is used,
   among other things, "for the people who are possessed by evils" [5]. Laypeople make and pour it as well as monks [6].
3. **How it is sprinkled (PRACTICE):** a bundle of seven mayom leaves, used by monks "in place of cogon grass" (หญ้าคา)
   [14]; mayom or lemongrass still today [15]; "a brush or tassel-like aspergillum made from bamboo" [9]; Master Z's own
   fingers, flicked (S3·01 [1]). **Vetiver (ya faek, หญ้าแฝก) as a sprinkler: NOT FOUND.** **A fan used to sprinkle: only
   the casebook (S1·15)**; the monk's palm-leaf fan (talapat) is "held in front of the face to block out worldly
   distraction when chanting" [16], which is a different use.
4. **What is chanted over the water (PRACTICE):** the Seven Paritta (Maṅgala, Ratana, Mettā, Khandha, Mora, Vaṭṭaka,
   Dhajagga) or the Twelve [5]. The verses that speak TO spirits, and are not yet in the game: the Ratana Sutta's opening
   ("Whatever spirits have gathered here ... may you all be happy") [7][38]; the Khandha Paritta's close ("I have made
   this protection, I have made this spell. May the beings depart.") [37e]; the verses that usher beings home after a
   chanting ("May all beings: who have fallen into suffering be without suffering ...") [37g][7]. The game has already
   used Namo tassa, Itipiso, the three homage lines, Yathā vārivahā, the Mettā Sutta's "Bhūtā vā sambhavesī vā / Sabbe
   sattā bhavantu sukhitattā", Sakkatvā and Om Namah Shivaya (§1.3).
5. **The order** is documented only piece by piece (§1.9): the monks' paritta ceremony has a fixed order (candles lit at
   one line and put out at another [7]; the chanting, then the water sprinkled by the chief monk while a layman carries
   the bowl [5]); one documented healer's sequence is "going into a trance-like state or meditating, blessing with holy
   water, rubbing with sacred objects, and finally a suggestion to make merit at a Buddhist monastery" [19]. Any single
   order for the game is a composite, and must be called his practice.
6. **The assistant (PRACTICE):** "when the monk sprinkle the holy water, lay must hold the alms bowl and walk following
   by chief monk" [5]; helpers bring a person out of a trance by patting the ears lightly or stroking the nose "พร้อมกับ
   การพูดเตือนสติ" (while speaking to recall them to themselves), telling them to breathe deeply and keep their eyes open
   [22]. A student who "keeps the room" with water against wandering spirits is **NOT FOUND** outside the game: it is
   the game's own fight (the plan's R5), defensible from the casebook's own uses of water (S1·15, S3·01) but a liberty.
7. **A spirit on the back (BELIEF):** the Thai category that best fits the casebook's words (a spirit that stays with a
   person because of past karma left unresolved) is เจ้ากรรมนายเวร (chao kam nai wen, "karmic creditor"): "a ghost that
   maintains ill will towards a person due to the wrongful karma" [34], "usually appearing as a spirit who sitting on
   someone's back" [33] (**WEAK**: uncited Wikipedia). A scholar glosses the term: "our past karma rules over our lives"
   [24]. The casebook does not use the word; whether Pho saw it so is not said.
8. **Released, not destroyed (FACT for the doctrine, BELIEF for the spirit):** Thai Buddhist practice helps such beings
   with merit, not with harm: a hungry ghost "will be relieved of its hunger only if someone will make merit, by
   offering food to the monks and ritually transfer the merit to the benefit of the pret" [20]; the canon's own verses
   dedicate merit to "others, neutral or hostile" [37a] and wish that
   beings "go to a heavenly destination" [37g]. Folk exorcism also knows capture in a sealed earthen pot and beating with
   a magic rod [20]: the game must not show the second, and the casebook's word for this case is "release". Karma itself
   is not washed away by water in Theravāda teaching [5][25].
9. **Wandering spirits (BELIEF):** the pret (เปรต) "always in a hungry state", whose idiom is "asking for a share of merit"
   [20]; the สัมภเวสี (samphawesi), "the ghost of a dead person that is seeking a way to be reborn" [35f]; the ผีตายโหง
   (phi tai hong), the ghost of a violent death, "much feared" [20]. (สัมภเวสี is *samphawesi* in RTGS, from Pali
   *sambhavesī*.) In belief they gather where merit and offerings are given [27][37c][37h]; that they are drawn by a
   *disturbance* is **NOT FOUND**. What a student is told: recollect the
   Buddha when afraid (the Buddha's own instruction, SN 11:3 [39]), spread goodwill (the Mettā Sutta's story [41]),
   and, in Master Z's words, "Don't stare. Don't point. Don't challenge it. Don't shout at it." (S1·19 [1]).
10. **Words and gestures:** a few plain Thai words for him (§6), never "anumodana" (the house rule: a monk's word); a
    raised open palm, which Thai iconography reads as "protection or freedom from fear" and "the attitude of
    forgiveness" [13]; sprinkling and flicking; blowing; joined palms; no finger pointed at a presence; touch kept to
    head, hands and wrist (V25.0 staging note 7).

## 1 · How a Thai master blesses a person to release a spirit

### 1.1 Who does it
- **FACT.** Lay as well as ordained specialists do this work. An ajahn "can be either monk or layman" [44]. Northern Thai
  healers (mo muang) include laymen, monks and spirit mediums, and "Both non-specialists like elders and Buddhist monks
  and specialists like mo muang and spirit mediums can carry out some of these rituals" [19].
- **FACT.** In Isan (the north-east, where Udon Thani is), the หมอธรรม (mo tham, "Dhamma doctor") is one "ผู้ร่ำเรียนสายมนต์
  คาถาทางพุทธเวทย์และไสยเวทย์ และปฏิบัติตัวอยู่ในคุณธรรมจริยธรรม มีศีลมีธรรม ส่วนใหญ่จะเป็นผู้ชายสูงอายุ หรือเป็นพระภิกษุ"
  (one who has studied Buddhist and occult incantations and lives by morality and the precepts; mostly elderly men, or
  monks), whose *dhamma* is "เวทมนต์คาถาประเภทหนึ่ง ใช้สำหรับป้องกัน ขับไล่ภูตผี" (a kind of incantation used to protect
  against and drive away spirits) [18]. **BELIEF:** "ชาวบ้านที่อยู่ในการรักษาของหมอธรรมจะเชื่อว่าพลังอำนาจของธรรมชนะอำนาจของผี
  ทั้งปวง" (those in a mo tham's care believe the power of the Dhamma overcomes the power of all spirits) [18].
- **Caution.** These are the regional analogues of the work, not Grandmaster Pho's lineage, which is not looked up
  (V26.0 verdict, §3). Call what he does "his practice", as S1·13 does.
- **FACT.** Tambiah's classic study of Buddhism and spirit cults in north-east Thailand was made in a village of Mueang
  Udon Thani district, 1960 to 1963 [51]. (The book itself was not opened here.)

### 1.2 Holy water (nam mon)
- **FACT (definitions).** น้ำมนต์: "น้ำที่เข้าพิธีทางพระพุทธศาสนาเพื่อให้อาบ กิน หรือประพรม ... ถือกันว่าเป็นมงคล" (water that has been
  through a Buddhist rite, to bathe in, drink or sprinkle; held to be auspicious); ประพรม, to sprinkle; สิญจน์ (2), to pour
  holy water (ORST, via [3]). The act is รดน้ำมนต์ (rot nam mon) [9]; a northern healer's list names "Rot Nam Mon blessing
  with lustral water" [19].
- **FACT (uses).** "Only joyous occasion will be made of holy water or sometimes the use of holy water is for the people
  who are possessed by evils" [5]. Lustral water is used at weddings, to allay illness, to consecrate a new house and at
  funerals (Wells, cited in [6]); in home blessings its purposes are to "offer joyful mind, be happy mind eliminate
  wickedness out of home" [5].
- **FACT (who makes it).** Laypeople make and pour holy water as well as monks: it "is not their exclusive right"
  (Textor, cited in [6]). Some Thai reformers question it: Buddhadasa Bhikkhu reportedly refused to pour it [6]. (Both
  from the first page and footnotes visible on the publisher's page; the article itself was not read.)
- **FACT (origin, as Thai practice tells it).** The commentarial story of Vesālī: Ānanda, with the Buddha's alms bowl of
  water, walked the walls chanting the Ratana Sutta; "When Ānanda start reciting 'Yaṃ kiñci', the evil spirit ran away
  out from 4 direction of the City gates" and the water "was sprinkled all around the city's wall making non-humans no
  longer stay in this city, they all escaped through the city's gate" [5]. Note what the story says: they LEFT. The same
  story is told in Piyadassi's Book of Protection: Vesālī "afflicted by a famine" and "Plagued by these three fears of
  famine, non-human beings and pestilence" [40].
- **PRACTICE (how it is made today).** At a home ceremony "the host has to set up the location then arranges the Buddhist
  alter which contains a joss stick, candle, alms bowl, flowers and so on"; "monks may recite seven Paritta ... to finish
  making holy water but sometimes ... twelve" [5]. "Ratana Sutta has never been cut off from making holy water" [5].
- **PRACTICE (the candle).** "The candles on the shrine during a house invitation are lit by the senior bhikkhu or nun at
  Asevanā" (at "Asevanā ca bālānaṁ", the Maṅgala Sutta) and "The candles are put out at Nibbanti dhīrā yathā'yam
  padīpo" (the Ratana Sutta's verse "Khīṇaṁ purāṇaṁ", numbered 6 in the manual) [7]. "wax from a candle is dripped into
  it by a monk, to symbolize Enlightenment" [8].
  **UNCERTAIN:** that the candle is put out by dipping it INTO the water: commonly said, not found in a source reached.
- **PRACTICE (the vessel).** บาตรน้ำมนต์ (bat nam mon, the alms bowl of holy water) is named beside mayom leaves as the
  ordinary kit in a news report of Phra Payom's comment [15]; the commentary's Ānanda used "only stone alms bowl and
  water" [5]; a crystal
  โถน้ำมนต์ (toh nam mon) is a vessel "used in Buddhism to store nahm mon" [10]. (The game's chapter 2 already has a
  turned silver bowl, `e4khannam`.)
- **PRACTICE (the sprinkler).**
  - Mayom: "ใช้สำหรับพระสงฆ์ประพรมน้ำมนต์แทนหญ้าคา" (used by monks to sprinkle holy water in place of cogon grass), "โดยนิยมใช้
    ใบมะยม (ทั้งก้าน) 7 ใบ มัดรวมกัน" (usually seven mayom leaves, stems on, bound together); mayom is also "used in rituals to
    ward off misfortune" (ปัดรังควาน) [14].
  - "สมัยนี้ยังใช้ใบมะยม ตะไคร้" (nowadays they still use mayom leaves and lemongrass), in a report of Phra Payom's comment on a
    monk who sprayed holy water from a crop sprayer: acceptable, he said, "ยังไงก็อย่าพ่นกันจนเปียก จนแฉะ" (either way, don't
    spray people until they are drenched) [15].
  - "a brush or tassel-like aspergillum made from bamboo" [9].
  - Cogon grass (หญ้าคา, ya kha): implied as the older sprinkler by [14].
  - **Vetiver (หญ้าแฝก, ya faek): NOT FOUND** as a sprinkler in any source reached; do not use the name.
  - **A fan:** the casebook only (S1·15). No outside source describes a fan used to sprinkle holy water. The monk's
    talapat (ตาลปัตร) "is held in front of the face to block out worldly distraction when chanting" [16]: if Pho holds a
    fan, it is HIS blessed fan (the casebook's), not a monk's talapat, and the way he uses it is his own.
  - **Fingers:** Master Z's own, flicked (S3·01).
- **PRACTICE (water drunk, poured, bathed).** For sorcery a healer "จะทำน้ำมนต์ และบริกรรมคาถา ให้คนไข้ดื่ม" (makes holy water,
  reciting a katha, and has the patient drink it) (V25.0 source [7], via [3]); poured over the head (S3·07 [1]).
- **BELIEF.** Holy water is believed to cleanse "negative energies, malevolent spirits, or 'low-level' impurities that
  might unknowingly cling to a person" [30] (WEAK: lifestyle).
- **Already decided in the game:** Chad cut Lersi Chin's leaf-sprinkling and thread-tying from chapter 2 at v25.3 ("no
  need for lersi chin to sprinkle water with the leaf thing and no need for the tieing of string"). That was about
  chapter 2; a leaf bundle or a tied thread in this chapter should be put to him, not assumed.

### 1.3 What is chanted
- **PRACTICE (over the water).** The seven-paritta set chanted in making holy water, in order: "Discourse on blessing
  (mangala sutta)", "The Jewel discourse (ratana sutta)", "Discourse on loving-kindness (mettā sutta)", "Protection of
  the aggregates (khanda paritta)", "The Peacock's Prayer for protection (mora paritta)", "The body quail's Protection
  (vattaka paritta)", "Banner of protection (dhajagga paritta)"; the twelve add the Āṭānāṭiya, Aṅgulimāla, Bojjhaṅga,
  Abhaya and Jaya paritta [5].
- **PRACTICE (lay healers).** Treatment begins "ด้วยการตั้งนะโม 3 จบ ท่องคาถา แล้วลงมือรักษา" (with Namo three times, the katha
  recited, then the treatment) (V25.0 source [6], via [3]).
- **FACT (texts that speak to spirits).**
  - **The Ratana Sutta's opening**, as chanted: "Yānīdha bhūtāni samāgatāni / Bhummāni vā yāni va antalikkhe / Sabb'eva
    bhūtā sumanā bhavantu / Atho pi sakkacca suṇantu bhāsitaṁ / Tasmā hi bhūtā nisāmetha sabbe / Mettaṁ karotha mānusiyā
    pajāya / Divā ca ratto ca haranti ye baliṁ / Tasmā hi ne rakkhatha appamattā" [7]; in English: "Whatever spirits have
    gathered here, —on the earth, in the sky— may you all be happy & listen intently to what I say. Thus, spirits, you
    should all be attentive. Show goodwill to the human race. Day & night they bring offerings, so, being heedful,
    protect them." [38]. **Caption note:** this verse is NOT in *A Chanting Guide* (its §0052 gives only the six verses
    from "Yaṅ-kiñci vittaṁ" [37d]); its English is dhammatalks.org's Sn 2:1 (the site of Thanissaro Bhikkhu's
    translations; the page names no translator). The caption rule of v21.31 names the Guide, so this one is Chad's call.
  - **The Khandha Paritta's close:** "Katā me rakkhā, Katā me parittā. Paṭikkamantu bhūtāni. So'haṁ namo Bhagavato, Namo
    sattannaṁ Sammā-sambuddhānaṁ." — "I have made this protection, I have made this spell. May the beings depart. I pay
    homage to the Blessed One, homage to the seven Rightly Self-awakened Ones." [37e][7]. **Honest note:** in the text the
    "beings" are creeping creatures (snakes, scorpions, centipedes) [7]; Thai monks chant it among the seven paritta as a
    general protection [5]. Used for a spirit, it is the master's application, not the verse's subject.
  - **The verses that usher beings home** (chanted at the end of a paritta, "necessary to include ... whenever the devas
    have been invited at the beginning ... as this chant contains a line inviting them to leave again" [7]):
    "Dukkhappattā ca niddukkhā / Bhayappattā ca nibbhayā / Sokappattā ca nissokā / Hontu sabbe'pi pāṇino." — "May all
    beings: who have fallen into suffering be without suffering, who have fallen into danger be without danger, who have
    fallen into sorrow be without sorrow." ... "Gacchantu devatāgatā" — "May they go to a heavenly destination." ...
    "Rakkhaṁ bandhāmi sabbaso." — "I bind this protection all around." [37g].
  - **The invitation at the start:** "Bhummā c'āyantu devā / Jala-thala-visame yakkha-gandhabba-nāgā, / Tiṭṭhantā santike
    yaṁ" — "And the earth-devas, spirits, gandhabbas, & nāgas in water, on land, in badlands, & standing nearby: May they
    come & listen with approval" (the list includes those "around homesites & fields") [37c].
  - **The Āṭānāṭiya's purpose:** given "so that the displeased Yakkhas may be pleased, so that the monks and nuns, laymen
    and laywomen may be at ease, guarded, protected and unharmed" [40]. Even the protection against hostile non-humans
    aims to please them. (A Thai academic warning that its rite, the Bhanayak, is often turned to occult ends is V25.0
    source [34], via [3].)
  - **Why the Mettā Sutta protects:** monks frightened in a forest by tree deities were given the sutta by the Buddha;
    reciting it, "the deities became friendly" and kept the grove quiet [41] (the commentary's story, paraphrased by the
    fetch tool; its verbatim lines include "they materialized all these terrifying conditions and afflicted the monks").
  - **Recollection against fear:** "If, when you have gone to the wilderness, to the foot of a tree, or to an empty
    dwelling, there should arise fear, terror, or horripilation, then on that occasion you should recollect me thus ...
    For when you recollect me, monks, any fear, terror, or horripilation you may have will be abandoned." (Dhajagga
    Sutta, SN 11:3 [39]). Itipiso is that recollection.
  - **UNCERTAIN.** Jinapañjara: a Thai protective chant reportedly by Somdet To, said in the first person (V25.0 §5.3,
    via [3]).
- **Already used in the game** (checked in src/chapters on 17 Nov 2026): Namo tassa; Iti pi so (e3c1); "Arahaṁ
  sammā-sambuddho bhagavā" (e3c3, e4c3); Yathā vārivahā (e3c4); "Bhūtā vā sambhavesī vā, / Sabbe sattā bhavantu
  sukhitattā." (e3c5); the Sakkatvā verses and Om Namah Shivaya (e4c2). **None of the Ratana opening, the Khandha close,
  the Devatāyuyyojana, the Sabba-patti-dāna or the Tiro-kuḍḍa has been used.**
- **The anumodana-adjacent caution (V25.0 verdict B5).** The "te" ("you") blessings (e.g. "Bhavatu sabba-maṅgalaṁ ...
  Sadā sotthī bhavantu te", "may you always be well" [37g]) are the monks' blessing over a layperson; Pho is a lay master
  in white. The first-person and all-beings verses above are free of that.
- **A pun to avoid (the lesson-line rule of v11.10).** "Nibbanti dhīrā yathā'yam padīpo" — "enlightened — go out like
  this flame" [37d] is about the awakened going out into nibbāna. Do not stage the candle's going out as the spirit's
  going out: the surface fits, the meaning does not.
- **The casebook's Thep (S1·17).** If Pho murmurs words no one can make out, the wordless-murmur rule of v25.4 applies

### 1.4 Blowing (pao, เป่า)

- **FACT.** ORST เป่ากระหม่อม (the crown-blow) gives its purpose as "เมตตามหานิยมเป็นต้น" (being liked and favoured, and so on)

### 1.6 The sacred thread (sai sin, สายสิญจน์)
- **FACT.** "ด้ายดิบสีขาวที่นำมาจับทบเป็น ๓ เส้น หรือ ๙ เส้น ใช้ในพิธีทางศาสนา" (white raw cotton thread doubled into 3 or 9

- **PRACTICE.** "เส้นด้ายสีขาวยาว ๆ ที่พระถือขณะประนมมือเจริญพระพุทธมนต์" (the long white thread the monks hold, palms joined,
  while chanting); in a house blessing it is circled "เวียนขวา" (clockwise) round the house and round the base of a Buddha
  image [17]. **BELIEF:** "ถือกันว่าสามารถป้องกันอันตราย เช่น ภูตผีปีศาจ ได้" (it is held to protect against harm such as
  ghosts and demons) [17] (Thai Wikipedia: tertiary; it cites a monastic glossary of 2005).
- **PRACTICE.** Isan villagers "มักให้หมอธรรมผูกฝ้าย รดน้ำมนต์" (usually have the mo tham tie cotton thread and pour holy

- **BELIEF (a barrier).** In an old account of magicians at a grave, "a sacred thread is wound about the place and

### 1.8 Merit dedicated so it can go (kruat nam, กรวดน้ำ)
- **PRACTICE.** Kruat nam: water "slowly poured from the flask into the bowl" so "that the deceased may benefit from merit
  making" [12]. Pour from when the monks begin "Yatha..." (Yathā vārivahā), finish at "Sapphi..." (Sabbītiyo), turn the
  vessel over, then pour the water "onto the ground or at the base of a large tree" asking Mother Earth (Phra Mae
  Thorani) to witness; recipients include "karmic creditors" [29] (WEAK: lifestyle). The short verse "อิทัง เม ญาตีนัง โหตุ
  สุขิตา โหนตุ ญาตะโย" (may this merit be for my relatives; may my relatives be happy), and a dedication to
  "เจ้ากรรมนายเวรทั้งหลายทั้งปวง" (all karmic creditors), with the water afterwards poured "ที่ดินหรือตามโคนต้นไม้" (onto the
  earth or at a tree's foot) [28] (the page credits the Department of Cultural Promotion).
- **FACT (the canon's dedications, with translations, in *A Chanting Guide*):**
  - Sabba-patti-dāna: "May all beings—without limit, without end—have a share in the merit just now made ... Those who
    are dear & kind to me ... whom I have seen or never seen; and others, neutral or hostile ... If they know of my
    dedication of merit, may they themselves rejoice, and if they do not know, may the devas inform them." [37a].
  - Uddissanādhiṭṭhāna: "By this act of merit ... human beings friendly, neutral, & hostile: May all beings be happy."
    [37b]. Ākāsaṭṭhā: "May devas & nagas of great power, standing in space and on land rejoice in this merit." [37b].
  - Tiro-kuḍḍa ("Hungry Shades Outside the Walls"): "Outside the walls they stand, & at crossroads. At door posts they
    stand, returning to their old homes." ... "Idaṁ vo ñātīnaṁ hotu / Sukhitā hontu ñātayo." — "May this be for our
    relatives. May our relatives be happy!" [37h].
- **NOT FOUND:** "yat nam" (ยาดน้ำ) as an Isan term for kruat nam; do not print it.
- **Already in the game:** kruat nam with Yathā vārivahā is a film in e3c4 and a beat in e3c5. A third kruat nam would
  repeat a scene (the v17.4 rule). If merit is dedicated here, it should take another form (a spoken dedication; one of
  the verses above, captioned; or Pho's few words).

- **Documented order A (the monks' paritta ceremony at a home):** the altar set (joss stick, candles, bowl, flowers)

- **Documented order B (one northern healer, Po Liang Kaeo Ma, removing black magic and "break[ing] ties with

- **Documented order C (old folk exorcism of a possessed woman, for contrast; most of it must NOT be shown, §7):** the
  phi doctor "will ask her what phi she is and why the phi came there ... If the phi is sent by someone, the name of the

### 3.2 Karma left unresolved
- **FACT (the term).** "Chao is the highest term, hierarchically ... Nai is a mundane term used to address one's employer
  or boss ... Altogether, then, chaogam-naiwen implies that our past karma rules over our lives"; it "is often suggested
  as the reason behind one's suffering through a negative streak"; women in the case it studies "are advised to make
  merit (tambun, ทำบุญ), recite certain chants, or perform particular rituals" [24] (a scholarly article on Thai women and

- **WEAK (signs and remedies, as believed).** Signs that the karmic creditor "ยังไม่ปล่อย" (has not let go) include chronic
  health problems with no cause found; remedies: merit made in earnest, "ตั้งจิตอโหสิกรรมจริงจังจากใจ" (sincerely setting the
  mind on mutual forgiveness), not repeating the old wrong, meditation, spreading metta [26].
- **FACT (what อโหสิกรรม means).** In Thai society it is used "เพื่อไม่ให้ผูกเวรต่อกัน" (so that no enmity binds the two);
  ORST 2554 defines it "การเลิกแล้วต่อกัน การไม่เอาโทษแก่กัน" (calling it quits; not holding the offence against each other);

  sense of forgiving at all), and "real Ahosikamma does not exist" while rebirth goes on [25].
- **BELIEF (vows).** "การบนบานศาลกล่าว สิ่งศักดิ์สิทธิ์ เทวดา ถือเป็นพันธะสัญญาที่ต้องชดใช้" (a vow to sacred beings and deities is a
  bond that must be repaid); their attendant spirits "คอยติดตาม คอยทวงหนี้อย่างไม่ลดละ" (keep following and demanding the debt
  without letting up); for a forgotten vow: precepts kept for some days, chanting, metta, offerings, and food left at a
  crossroads for wandering spirits [27] (WEAK: lifestyle). In Hindu practice too, a vow is to be carried out as
  promised, and failure "may result in disaster"; stories of retribution after a broken vow are common [46] (paraphrased
  by the fetch tool except the quoted words).
- **CASEBOOK.** S1·17: a wish granted through a Yin amulet "and he had never gone back to thank or settle what he had

## 4 · Forced out, or released? How Thai Buddhist practice understands it

- **FACT (doctrine).** In Theravāda teaching beings are reborn according to their kamma; a hungry ghost is a state of
  rebirth (S2·08 [1]). The pret "will be relieved of its hunger only if someone will make merit, by offering food to the
  monks and ritually transfer the merit to the benefit of the pret" [20]. The canon's dedication reaches "others,
  neutral or hostile" [37a]; the ushering verses ask that beings in suffering, danger and sorrow be free of them and "go
  to a heavenly destination" [37g]; the Ratana Sutta asks the gathered spirits to "be happy" and "Show goodwill to the
  human race" [38]; the Khandha close asks "May the beings depart" [37e]; the Āṭānāṭiya aims that "the displeased Yakkhas
  may be pleased" [40]. Nothing in these texts destroys a being.
- **FACT.** Muecke, citing Tambiah: phii, "being lesser and often evil entities, are controlled and exorcised by Buddhist
  monks" [21]. Exorcism exists; its Buddhist form works through the Dhamma and merit.
- **FACT (two folk modes).** The northern healers' list has both "Attacking the spirits with katha" (rubbing with sacred
  objects, removing calamity, "Tat Prai breaking ties with spirits") and "Appeasing the spirits with offerings" [19]. Near
  the end of an Isan healing rite the gods are invited to leave [45] (the fetch tool's paraphrase). A pot buried or sunk
  is folk practice [20][32], and the
  casebook's black bag is Pho's way with the Prai of corpse oil (S1·14) and with presences on amulets (S1·15).
- **FACT (karma and water).** "we believed that sin or karma is a deed which can be good deed and bad deed, they cannot
  wash away by any holy water" [5]; while rebirth goes on "everybody must receive the consequences of their action" [25].
  A Chulalongkorn thesis on *kae kam* (แก้กรรม, "fixing karma") rites exists [57]; its abstract was not read.
- **CASEBOOK (this case and its neighbours).** S1·13: "release the spirit and clear the karma"; S2·12: "nothing was
  destroyed and nothing was driven out. The presence was heard, settled and released."; S2·05: merit dedicated to
  "whatever entity might be there"; S1·17: fairness promised; S3·03: "Not every issue needs a battle."
- **What this means for the game.** "Forced from her back" (the casebook's words for the moment) and "release" (its word
  for the purpose) can both be shown: the spirit tears loose, cries out, and GOES; it is not killed, burned or tortured.
  "Clear the karma" is shown as what Thai practice does with karma left between two beings: forgiveness asked between
  them (อโหสิกรรม, in its Thai sense) and merit dedicated to the one that leaves, never as water washing karma away, and
  never as a promise. A short dedication after she stands (§1.8, not a third kruat nam film) would close it the way the
  casebook's other cases close.

## 5 · Wandering and hungry spirits, and how a student keeps a room

### 5.1 The kinds (BELIEF)
- **เปรต (pret):** "the Thai pret is a very tall and very lean phi in human form ... It feeds on pus and blood and even
  that does not satisfy its hunger for its mouth is no bigger than the eye of a needle"; "A person who during his or her
  life time has done a great sin will become a phi pret"; a beggar is said to be "asking for a share of merit like the
  phi pret" [20]. Master Z's own gloss: "intense craving, hunger and deprivation ... extremely tall and thin, sometimes
  with a large stomach but an extremely narrow throat" (S2·08 [1]).
- **ผีตายโหง (phi tai hong):** "A person who dies a violent death will become a phi tai hong"; with phi pop and phi tai
  thang klom, "These three phi are much feared by the people" [20].
- **สัมภเวสี (samphawesi):** from Pali *sambhavesī*, "birth seeker": "the ghost of a dead person that is seeking a way to be
  reborn"; "stray ghost or spirit" [35f]. The Mettā Sutta's "born & seeking birth" ("Bhūtā vā sambhavesī vā") is the same
  word [37f] (already in e3c5).
- **The departed at home:** "At door posts they stand, returning to their old homes" [37h]: the canon's own picture of
  the dead coming back to their houses, met with merit, not water.

### 5.2 Why they would come (BELIEF)
- Where merit and offerings are given: food left at a crossroads "for wandering spirits" when a vow is settled [27]; the
  devas, spirits and nāgas "around homesites" and "standing nearby" invited to come and listen [37c]; the pret who asks
  "a share of merit" [20].
- **NOT FOUND:** that spirits are drawn to a *disturbance* or to a sick person "the way flies are drawn" (the plan's
  image). The documented reason is the rite itself: beings come for a share. The plan's wanderers can be read so
  ("They came for the merit, not for her") at no cost to the fight.

### 5.3 What holy water does to them, in belief
- They leave: at Vesālī "non-humans no longer stay in this city, they all escaped through the city's gate" [5].
- They come away, crying: "As the water touched them, the presences reacted — screaming, coming away" (S1·15); "reacted
  and recoiled" (S3·01).
- **FACT (a parallel the trance beat can stand on).** In a 2025 study of a Sak Yant lineage's Wai Khru, holy water was
  sprinkled over the disciples and "เมื่อลูกศิษย์เริ่มโดนพรมน้ำพระพุทธมนต์ บางคนก็เริ่มของขึ้นอีกครั้ง บางคนส่งเสียงคำราม" (as the
  disciples were touched by the holy water, some went into khong khuen again; some roared) [22]. A trance and a loud
  cry under a blessing's water are documented in Thai practice (in another rite, of another people).

### 5.4 What a student is told
- **FACT (the canon).** Recollect the Buddha when afraid, and fear "will be abandoned" [39]; recite goodwill, as the
  frightened forest monks were told to [41]; chanters of paritta are "expected to do so with a heart of love and
  compassion" [40].
- **BELIEF.** A firm mind is the protection: in khong khuen, "จิตอ่อนจะยิ่งมีอาการของขึ้นง่าย หากจิตแข็งจะไม่มีอาการของขึ้น" (a soft
  mind shows it easily; a firm mind not at all) [22]; "a calm and kind mind is the best protective shield" [31] (WEAK).
- **CASEBOOK.** Do not engage: "Don't stare. Don't point. Don't challenge it. Don't shout at it. Don't deliberately draw
  its attention" (S1·19); "Having an ability does not mean you need to challenge everything you meet" (S1·18); "not
  every spiritual encounter has to become a confrontation" (S1·20); "protection is not about fighting spirits — it is
  about creating a strong spiritual shield" (S2·11). Know your part: S1·16, S1·17, S3·07.

**Gestures that read clearly, and what each means**
- **Sprinkling / flicking:** with the fingers (S3·01), the fan (S1·15) or a leaf bundle [14] (the last two: §1.2's notes).
  The water goes TO the beings and around her; it is never thrown hard (Phra Payom: "don't soak people" [15]).
- **A raised open palm, facing out:** ปางห้ามญาติ (pahng hahm yaat) is "the position with the right hand raised and palm
  forward", which "symbolizes the Buddha's offer, to his followers, of protection or freedom from fear", "sometimes
  called 'the attitude of forgiveness'"; abhaya is "calm", "reassurance" and "no fear" [13]. **UNCERTAIN:** a search
  summary reports that a Wikipedia page on Thai Buddha iconography gives two raised hands to this name and one raised hand
  to another (not opened, not checked); the meaning of one raised palm (calm, no fear) is not in dispute. It is the
  right gesture toward a spirit: it stops, it does not strike.
- **Joined palms (añjali), the thread held in them:** the monks' way of chanting [17]; for a lay master, joined palms
  over the bowl.
- **Blowing:** onto the water after a katha (ORST เป่ามนตร์, §1.4); at a pressure (S1·18).
- **Drawing from the air:** Pho's own (S1·14), if the plan wants it.
- **Not:** a finger pointed at a presence (S1·19); the soles of the feet toward her altar or toward people ("it is rude
  to point one's feet, especially the sole, at other people, or revered objects" [42]); a grip on her head or body.
- **The wai:** "hands pressed together at chest level with a slight bow of the head" in everyday respect [42]; the
  student's to his teacher, and Pho's to her altar if the plan wants it (R-A for what her own tradition does).

## 7 · What the game may show / must not show

**May show** (each with its basis above)
1. Pho chanting Pali over a bowl of water, the verse to the spirits (§1.3, Chad's call on its caption), the Khandha
   close or the ushering verses at the release; a dedication of merit naming all beings, the released one among them
   (§1.8, §4).
2. Holy water flicked with the fingers or sprinkled with Pho's blessed fan; a bowl carried by the student behind his
   teacher (§1.2, §2).
3. The beings reacting to the water and LEAVING (Vesālī; S1·15), each release a going, not a death (§4, §5.3).
4. Candles and incense lit before the chant; respect for her own altar and lamp; nothing aimed at it (§1.7).
5. A light hand on her head or her hands, the family present (§1.5).
6. Pho speaking to the spirit fairly (S1·17's "fairness", S2·12's "heard, settled and released"), and a few Thai words
   (§6), captioned.
7. Her trance coming under the blessing and its water, and a loud cry (S1·13; the parallel in [22]); shown as R-A says
   her devotees know it.
8. The student speaking low to the frightened son: a helper "เตือนสติ" (recalling him to himself), as Thai helpers do [22];
   consoling is a ritual step in Isan healing [45].
9. The case file's lesson: doctors first; "not a promise".

**Must not show**
1. **The possessed woman beaten, pressed or cut**, or a rod or knife used on her body: the old folk method of [20]. It
   would make a practitioner harm an elderly woman (the rule that no practitioner is the villain).
2. **A spirit killed, burned, screamed to death or kept as a trophy.** The casebook's word for this case is release;
   Buddhist practice helps such beings with merit (§4).
3. **Her goddess, or her devotion, as the evil.** The thing on her back is not Mae Kali; her trance is not "demonic".
   (Outsiders have read goddess possession that way. In a Christian fishing community of Kanyakumari, Ram writes of "The
   figure of the demonised Hindu goddess", and a local schoolteacher explained: "Possession by 'Hindu' spirits is simply
   because the more fearsome Hindu idols look demon-like to our fisherpeople. So they say it is Kali who is in them."
   [49]. That is exactly the frame the game must not adopt.)
4. **Her stopping as a sin, punished.** The casebook says "matters left unresolved", no more; no line may blame her
   grief, her choice or her goddess.
5. **Karma washed away by water, a cure promised, an illness declared spiritual without the doctors** (§3.4, §4).
6. **Anything of the trade:** fees, a donation asked, an amulet sold, lottery numbers, a wish granted. (News of village
   exorcisms reports fees [32]; the game shows none.)
7. **Pho saying "anumodana"**, blessing in the monks' "te" form (put to Chad if wanted), naming a lineage or teacher, or
   claiming a power the casebook does not give him (V26.0 verdict 9).
8. **A third kruat nam film** (e3c4 and e3c5 have it); **"go out like this flame" as a picture of the spirit leaving**
   (§1.3).
9. **A finger pointed at a presence; feet toward her altar or toward people; touch beyond head, hands and wrist.**
10. **"Vetiver" or "yat nam" printed as Thai practice** (NOT FOUND), or a talapat used as a sprinkler.
11. **A leaf bundle or a tied thread without asking**, after Chad's v25.3 cut of both from chapter 2.
12. **The departed husband treated as a threat** (the plan already makes throwing water at him an error, which the
    Tiro-kuḍḍa's homecoming dead support [37h]).
