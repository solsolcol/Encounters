## 1. Where the words were

Nowhere, in one place. A take's text lived in whichever plan doc built it
(AUDIO-PLAN for chapter 1, the V4.x and V5.0 plans for the rest), the
chapter files carried a few as comments, and EIGHT takes were written down
in no document at all: chapter 2's two proximity lines and four outcome
lines, chapter 4's opening line and its proximity line. They existed only
as sound.

So every voice take in the game was transcribed (ElevenLabs Scribe, via
the connector: the takes joined with two-second gaps into four batches,
one call each — the transcript comes back as plain text with no
timestamps, but every line is a sentence or two and the order is known,
so splitting it is not a guess). Cost: about seventy cents. What it found
is section 2.

## 3. The registry — `src/voicelines.js`

One plain script registering `window.__VOICE__` (the shape `strings.js`
uses for `__TEXT__`): a `SPEAKERS` table (four: James, Ma, the auntie,
the tang-ki — name, ElevenLabs voice id, model), a `CHAPTERS` table for
the sheet's labels, and one `LINES` row per take:

```
{ id: "v2wake1", who: "james", ch: "ch2", where: "Opening film, over black",
  text: "It followed me home.", secs: 1.65 }
```

`id` is the sample name (the file `assets/audio/<id>.mp3`; chapter 1's
`voice` is the one take that is its own asset). `secs` is the measured
length of the shipped take, so a scene's timing can be checked against it
without opening the file. `where` is written for Chad in plain words;
`note` carries what a regeneration must know (shouted, whispered, retired,
not used, "the take also says the words").

**It is not shipped.** `build.py` reads `strings.js` by name and globs only
`src/chapters/`, so the registry costs the player nothing. Two tools read
it: `textsync` (below) and `chaptertest`, which now fails if any voice
take on disk (a name starting `v` or `t5` that is not a room bed) has no
row, or any row has no take, no text, no speaker, no chapter, no length.
That is what stops the registry going stale as chapters add lines — and
it is a data check inside an existing harness, so the rule that adding a
chapter must not add a harness still holds.

Two takes are in the packs and used nowhere, and the registry says so
rather than hiding them: `vchantline` (retired at v3.7) and `v3wake4`
(lost its caller when the ghost left chapter 3 at v4.3).

## 4. The sheet — two tabs from one export

`node textsync.mjs export <file>.xlsx` writes a workbook with GAME TEXT
(the four columns as before) and VOICE LINES (`ID` · `Who` · `Chapter` ·
`When it plays` · `TEXT` · `Length (s)` · `Notes`); the Drive connector
turns an .xlsx into a Google Sheet with one tab per worksheet. A CSV
export carries the voice rows too, in the four-column shape, keyed
`voice.<id>`. A line under an outcome card names the choice it follows —
read from the chapter at export time, so nobody types it twice.

`import` reads the CSV, the .xlsx (every tab), or the markdown the
connector returns, finds the TEXT column FROM EACH HEADER ROW (the two
tabs put it in different places — assuming column three would have written
"When it plays" into every take's text, which is exactly what the first
CSV draft did before the round-trip test caught it), writes voice texts
into the registry by the same targeted in-place replacement the chapters
get, and ends with the list of voice lines whose text changed. That list
is the regeneration list. An empty voice cell is refused with a warning.

Verified: export → import leaves `src/voicelines.js` byte-identical for
both the CSV and the .xlsx; a real edit to one cell lands in exactly that
row and is named in the report.

**Why the Google Sheet itself is one tab.** The connector creates a
sheet from text (the CSV, proven since v3.0) or from a workbook passed as
base64 inside a tool call. The xlsx package wrote 111 KB for these rows;
the hand-built writer above gets it to 20 KB; that is still 27 KB of
base64, which a session cannot read back in one page nor re-emit without
real risk of a corrupt byte — and a workbook off by one byte is not a
sheet with a typo, it is no sheet. So v19 was created from the CSV, with
the voice lines under the text after a divider row (an empty ID cell,
which import skips), and the two-tab workbook goes to Chad as a FILE: open
it with Google Sheets and it is a two-tab sheet in his Drive. `import`
reads either layout — every table on every tab, by its header row.

Sheet **v19** is v18 (read back first, matched row for row) plus the
voice lines. **Every sheet from here on carries them** — Chad's rule.

## 5. The child — what changes, and what he must pick

**The voice.** The workspace has no Chinese or Singaporean child voice —
the Singapore-accented voices are all adults (Colonel, 26; Kelvin; Lee;
Brendon; Louis) and the child voices are American, Indian, Japanese or
German. Four samples of one chapter-2 line ("It followed me home. Ma? Ma,
is that you? There is someone in the room.") were generated for him to
hear, on the flow *Encounters — child voice candidates for James*:

| voice | id | what it is |
|---|---|---|
| VALF | `loY1uopAz31XyhAEhNSa` | a young boy, energetic, American |
| Teddy Twinkle | `XjGYkUkzth8BPs29fmcV` | a small cartoon boy, American |
| Toru | `wGSNAQwhUbFf1J43d5Tw` | a calm Japanese boy-to-young-man, reading English |
| Aaron | `B6uUx2p7cRgxseOUyP6P` | a youthful young adult, boy-next-door |

The honest read: VALF and Teddy are children, and American; Toru is Asian
and young but Japanese; Aaron is not twelve. If none is right the routes
are a voice from the wider ElevenLabs library (search "Singapore child")
or a designed voice. His pick goes in ONE place — `SPEAKERS.james.voice`
in the registry — and every row marked `james` is regenerated.

**The story.** A twelve-year-old changes more than the pitch:
- Chapter 1's choice B — "Kick it and laugh. Superstition is for other
  people" — and its scoff read as a young man's bravado; a child's version
  is a different sentence, and the teaching under it may want a different
  weight.
- Chapter 1's opening ("this is still the fastest way home") is a man
  walking home near midnight; a child out at that hour is a question the
  brief has to answer.
- Chapter 3's auntie calls him "boy" (fits better, not worse); chapter 5's
  "Uncle. What did you see?" fits; the mother's "Go back to sleep, boy"
  fits.
- Chapter 4 is built on being ALONE in the flat with Ma working late, and
  a child provoking the presence ("Come out where I can SEE you!") is a
  different scene from a young man doing it; scene D's phone call — a boy
  ringing his mother at night — is if anything stronger.
- The stat language ("Sanity", the panic) and the title brief ("Ordinary
  places on the wrong night") are unaffected.
Those are his calls, made on the sheet: the options and the self-narration
are all on it now, both tabs.

**The plan after his review:** import the sheet (the report names every
changed line) → regenerate every `james` row with the chosen voice, plus
the changed rows of any other speaker → encode to the contract (mp3
44.1 kHz/128 kbps and the Opus copy, metadata stripped), level-match
against the pack → re-measure every take's `secs` and re-check every
cutscene against the new lengths (the overlap scan from v4.91) → packs
rebuilt → release with a fresh sheet, because the lengths on it will have
changed.
