## 1. The source (S1·10, docs/source/site-case-files.md 159–174)

*Classification: an experiment I regret · Period: during the search ·
Place: my own altar · Nature: deliberate trance — do not repeat.*

> **Hook.** The owner explained it as caring for homeless children — that
> offerings and merits give them something they never had. I saw it as
> compassion, and I kept several. Then one night curiosity got the better of
> me, and I did something I now tell everyone never to do.
>
> While I was still searching for answers, I began keeping Kumanthong. The
> way it was explained to me was as a form of care: think of it like looking
> after children who have nowhere to go, and when you make offerings and
> dedicate merits for them, you are giving them something they never had. I
> saw it as another way of making merit and showing compassion, so I kept
> several and made offerings to them regularly.
>
> Then one night, after everything else I had been through, I wanted to know
> something. What would happen if I deliberately opened myself to my own
> Kumanthong?
>
> I went through them one by one, letting my mind loosen each time. What
> happened frightened me. With each different Kumanthong I found myself
> behaving like a different child, with a completely different personality.
> My expressions changed. My mannerisms changed. Even the way I felt from
> the inside seemed to change. One after another after another.
>
> At that moment I was fascinated by it. Looking back today, I can see
> clearly how dangerous and how completely unnecessary that experiment was.
>
> **The lesson.** Even if you believe a Kumanthong belongs to you, protects
> you, and would never intentionally harm you — never deliberately invite it
> to enter or take control of your body. In traditional belief Kumanthong are
> regarded as Yin, while a living person carries Yang. Even with no ill
> intent on either side, repeatedly immersing yourself in something held to
> carry strong Yin energy is understood within that tradition as disturbing
> your own balance. And setting that aside entirely: deliberately
> surrendering control of your own consciousness is not something to
> experiment with casually, whatever you believe is on the other side. You do
> not need possession, trance or dramatic manifestations to prove a spiritual
> connection. Care for what you keep. Make merits if that is your practice.
> Treat the traditions with respect. But keep your boundaries and stay in
> control of yourself. Some lessons are only learned by living them — this is
> the one experiment I would tell everybody never to repeat.

### 2.2 Taken on his behalf (recorded, each changeable)

1. **The Golden Mile owner gives him the three** (rev 4: chapter 3's owner,
   Chad's own model `owner`, his own voice `owner3`, at his own counter under
   the old masters' photographs). Until rev 3 it was the keeper of chapter
   4's Yin shop. Chad's purpose (§2.6) moved it: the chapter clears the idea
   that Kumanthong are yin baby spirits, and a Yin shop's counter would teach
   that idea before a word was said. The owner's shop is where monks'
   blessed images are kept and honoured, so three figures a monk made belong
   there; and "the owner explained it" is the case file's own word. He is
   kind, knowledgeable and careful. He does not sell them to him: he
   entrusts them, and only after asking him to promise to care for them and
   make merit for them for as long as he lives (§2.5 1). He even WARNS him
   (*"Look after them like your own, ah. But don't play-play with them."*),
   so the harm comes only from what he chooses to do (the v21.30 rule).
   Chapter 4 is not lost: the first receipt on his cork board is the one he
   kept from chapter 4, for the young woman (§4.1 9). The Yin keeper stays
   the alternative (§2.3 1).
2. **The children are never villains, and never feared** — the v21.30 rule
   ("no practice and no practitioner is ever the villain"), extended to the
   three, and since rev 2 Chad's own (*"they should not be feared"*, §2.4).
   They are children: bossy, shy, lonely, and each was given up by someone.
   Nothing in the chapter attacks him, and nothing in it is a scare. What
   frightens is losing HIMSELF, and that comes from his own act.
3. **Every possession is his own voice.** "I found myself behaving like a
   different child." There are no child actors and no child voices: every
   word a child says comes out of HIS mouth, in Brian's voice (the adult
   him), bossy, giggling, sobbing. A grown man's voice speaking as a child is
   exactly what the case file describes: he became the child. The doubles
   make only small wordless sounds of their own (a breath of a laugh, a
   giggle in hide and seek), made as effects, never words.
4. **He is never seen from outside.** There is no rigged adult Master Zav,
   so every shot is his eyes or a cutaway to a THING (the figures, the
   drinks, the incense, the window, the clock, the phone) or to another
   person (the owner). "His face changing" is told from inside: the voice,
   the hands, the height, the gait, the colour of the world (§3). An
   optional model would open a few outside shots (§2.3, decision 6).
5. **The ghosts of the three are their own scans.** Each child "arrives" as
   a glowing, translucent double of its own figure (the scan itself — the
   v21.16 rule: the scan or nothing) that rises off its base and comes to
   him the way a child comes to be picked up: slowly, in its own warm colour,
   never rushing at the lens. There are no drawn children and no shadow
   children.
6. **Play begins at the counter, at closing time** (§4.0b, rev 4); the rest
   is at night (he comes home late, the business keeps him), from 22:50. The
   home film carries the dusks and the weeks. The incense is
   lit at 23:05 and burns through the openings. The third child's stick
   burns out while he is still inside him. He comes back at 02:47.
7. **Each possession has one thing the player does**, escalating: STAMP (the
   bossy one's tantrum through the player's own finger), PEEK (the shy one's
   peekaboo — the player opens the fingers), COME BACK (the lonely one does
   not want him to go — the player rises anyway, gently, §4.5). The player
   plays along in the first two and is heartbroken in the third.
8. **The possession films cannot be skipped the first time** a player plays
   the chapter (the "no matter what"). Once the chapter has been completed
   they can be skipped like every other film (§5, S41).
9. **The four options are four KINDS of answer**, after the experiment
   (rebuilt twice on 25 Oct: Chad's *"The four options are not distinct
   enough"*, then his greed option in place of "give them back"). Each is a
   different thing flowing between him and the three:
   - A · STAY WITH THEM — he gives HIMSELF to them, out of pity: open
     himself again, as long as they want;
   - B · TELL THE WORLD — he gives them away to STRANGERS, as a story: post
     it on the amulet forum;
   - C · WISH FOR IT ALL — he TAKES from them: talk to them every day and
     wish for everything he wants (Chad's);
   - D · CARE FOR THEM — he GIVES to them and keeps himself: keep caring,
     make merit, never open again.

   Only A opens him again. The ranks follow the lesson: A worst, C bad, B
   bad, D best (§4.7). There is no middle answer any more (§2.3 4).
10. **The drinks are a made-up red soda**, never a real brand: small 325 ml
    plastic bottles with screw caps, bendy straws in striped paper sleeves.
    Toys and sweets sit on the altar as well (a tin car, a rubber ball, a
    paper pinwheel; a saucer of sweets each), because that is how
    Kumanthong are kept. And before each child, a small cup of plain WATER,
    filled first every night: plain water, never allowed to run out, is the
    rule the Thai care guides state most firmly (§2.7). No line ties the red
    drink to blood (§2.7).
11. **The core lesson** is S1·10's own, in the second person and short (the
    v21.8 shape), with no Pali term (THE LESSON LINE: the term is Chad's to
    name). §4.10 has the draft.
12. **Ma (his mother, `mother`, Matilda's voice) leaves one voicemail in
    ending A.** She is the mother of episode 1. Her worried "Call me back,
    okay?" is the life he stops answering.
13. **The lullaby has a home: a small wooden MUSIC BOX** (rev 2). Since rev
    4 it was the shy one's: his old keeper played it for him every night for
    twenty years (ow5shy). The owner winds it on his counter as he tells the

1. **They are children who were given up.** At the counter (§4.0b) the owner
   tells each one's story as the player meets him: a family took the red one
   home for luck, and when the luck did not come fast enough they brought him
   back; the shy one's old keeper looked after him for twenty years, and when
   she passed on her family brought him back; and somebody left the little
   one at a temple gate, and nobody knows where he came from (ow5red, ow5shy,
   ow5little). That is why nobody comes for them. The player meets them as
   orphans, not as charms.
2. **Each possession shows what that child never had.** Inside each one he
   feels what the child feels ("even the way I felt from the inside seemed
   to change"), and each possession ends on that child's need, said in the
   child's own small voice:
   - the red one was never wanted ("...They didn't want me any more.");
   - the shy one hid and waited, and nobody came ("...Nobody ever comes.");
   - the little one was always left ("Everyone goes away...").

   His words when he comes back are understanding, not fear: "He wasn't
   angry. He just wanted someone to want him."
3. **Nothing is a scare.** No flicker, no slam, no curtain flapping by
   itself, no stinger, no shape at the edge of sight, no cold creeping in.
   The doubles come to him gently, in warm colours. A giggle is a game of
   hide and seek. The fear the case file names ("What happened frightened
   me") is his fear FOR HIMSELF: he nearly stayed with the little one, and
   three hours were gone. It is never fear OF them.
4. **Every ending lands on THEM.** The three bad answers each fail the
   children in a way the player feels:
   - in A, he loses himself in them, and nobody is left to look after them;
   - in B, the shy one's secret becomes a show for strangers;
   - in C, care turns into asking, and their toys end up buried under his
     wishes.

   The best answer gives them what they never had: merit in their names, a
   promise kept, and nothing asked back. Whichever is chosen, the epilogue
   opens on them as the owner first set them down, with his line: "They were
   never something to fear. Only children with nowhere to go... and what they
   needed from me was care, merit and kindness, for as long as I live." (rev
   4, §2.5 5)
5. **D is the episode's emotional peak, and it rhymes with chapter 4's merit
   ending** (Chad: *"that ending was quite emotionally beautiful"*). Chapter
   4 had a promise to the young woman, a temple at sunset, the dedication
   whispered, the monk's verse captioned, the water poured, and a pale light
   rising from the earth. D has a promise to the three ("I won't open that
   door again. But I'm not going anywhere."), the same temple at DAWN, the
   children's favourite drinks offered to the monk in their names, the same
   verse, the water, and THREE small lights rising together in the
   children's three colours: red-gold, amber and blue.
6. **The lesson's heart is Chad's**: they are not to be feared, and caring
   for them takes true loving kindness, which gives and asks for nothing
   back. The case file's own lesson stays (never invite them in; stay in
   control of yourself), and Chad's greed lesson is C's (§4.10). No Pali
   term is added (§2.3 9). Since rev 4 the lesson also says what Kumanthong
   made by monks are, and what keeping them asks (§2.6).
7. **The test for every line and every shot**, read before it is voiced or
   built: does this make the player afraid OF them? If yes, it is cut or
   rewritten. Does it make the player feel FOR them? That is the aim.

### 2.5 THE PROMISE (rev 3 — Chad: "long term commitment ... emphasized throughout this chapter"; "These kumanthongs need long term care and merits")

Chad: *"we dont want players to feel emotional and all go out to buy
kumanthongs without long term commitment, that's not the goal"*. §2.4 makes
the player feel for the three. This makes sure that feeling is never
mistaken for a reason to take one in. It governs beside §2.4.

1. **Taking them in is a promise for life, and the owner asks for it
   first.** He does not sell them to him; he entrusts them, and only after
   asking (ow5promise): *"And not for one month, not for one year. Every day
   you look after them, you keep making merit for them... as long as you
   live. If one day you really cannot, you bring them back to me, or to a
   temple. Never just leave them. Can or not?"* The PLAYER answers, with
   his own press and his hands in añjali (z5promise, §4.0b): *"Every day. I
   promise."* No price is ever shown or said. Every ending is that promise,
   kept or broken.
2. **Their stories are what greed, and a promise with no one after it,
   leave behind** (rev 4). A family took the red one home for luck and gave
   him back when it did not come fast enough. The shy one's old keeper kept
   her promise for twenty years, and when she died nobody had been asked to
   take him on. Nobody knows where the little one came from: somebody left
   him at a temple gate. The player meets them as what is left when a
   keeper's care ends.
3. **The care and the merit are shown as ongoing, never as a moment.**
   - The owner has written his rules on a small card in neat capitals, and
     it stands on his altar against the music box: *AS THEIR LUANG PHOR
     TAUGHT. EVERY NIGHT — fresh water, never let it run dry · a sweet drink
     each · one stick each · wind the box · talk to them kindly. EVERY BUDDHA
     DAY — make merit in their names. NEVER for greed. NEVER ask them for
     anything wrong. FOR AS LONG AS YOU LIVE — and if one day you truly
     cannot, bring them back. Never leave them.*
   - His cork board collects a temple's merit receipt every Buddha Day,
     beside the first one there, chapter 4's, for the young woman (the home
     film's weeks show them arriving one by one, and the calendar's days
     crossed off, none missed).
   - Play I is the same care as every night, and he says so (z5bag: *"Three
     red drinks. Three straws. Same as every night, since the day I
     promised."*).
4. **Each ending is judged by the promise.**
   - A breaks it by losing himself: the care and the merit both stop. The
     card lies face down in the dust, and the cork board's last receipt is
     weeks old.
   - B turns the three into a story, and the story makes strangers want one
     for the thrill (*"just ordered one, coming tmr"* among the replies),
     which is exactly what this chapter must never do to its players.
   - C keeps the "every day" and empties it: care made a bargain. Order
     slips go up over the merit receipts, and the owner's card is found under
     his wish slips (§4.8 C).
   - D keeps it, for years. The temple at dawn is the first of many Buddha
     Days, the receipts fill the cork board and then a second one, and the
     promise is made to outlive him: an envelope under the music box, *FOR
     WHOEVER LOOKS AFTER THEM NEXT*, with the card copied out inside, so
     nobody will ever again say "nobody comes for them".
5. **The epilogue and the lesson say it plainly.** The epilogue (z5epi0):
   *"They were never something to fear. Only children with nowhere to go...
   and what they needed from me was care, merit and kindness, for as long as
   I live."* The core: never taken in on a feeling; a promise for life, of
   care and of merit (§4.10).
6. **The test, beside §2.4 7**: does this make the player want to go out
   and get one? If it does, it is not finished. What the chapter should
   leave behind is respect for the three and the weight of the promise.

### 2.6 THE PURPOSE (rev 4 — Chad: "The purpose of this chapter is to clear misconceptions about kumanthongs being yin baby spirits")

This is what the chapter is FOR, and it governs everything: §2.4 (the heart)
and §2.5 (the promise) are two of its parts. Chad named seven things, and
each lives in more than one place, so a player meets every one of them
whatever he chooses. The facts behind them are in
docs/research/V23.0-KUMANTHONG.md, with their sources, and a line says as
BELIEF what the sources call belief.

1. **Not trapped, blood-fed spirits.** The misconception is HIS, said at the
   counter, because after chapter 4's Yin shop it is the natural one (z5yinq:
   *"Uncle... aren't these yin? Baby ghosts... the kind people trap with
   black magic, feed with blood... that turn on you if you ever stop?"*). The
   owner says yes to the true part and no to the false part (ow5yin:
   *"Children, yes. Ghosts to be afraid of, no. What you're thinking of —
   trapped with black magic, fed with blood, sent to hurt people — that's
   something else. These three came from a temple. A Luang Phor gave them a
   home there... out of compassion."*), and answers the last fear too:
   nobody forces them to stay, and if one day he truly cannot keep them, he
   brings them back (ow5monk, ow5promise). It comes back in B, where his
   story is reposted as exactly that horror; in the epilogue (*"They were
   never something to fear."*); and in the core. No other tradition is named
   or mocked: what is corrected is a mix-up about THESE three (the v21.30
   rule), and nothing denies that they are children's spirits (§2.7).
2. **A monk gave them a home, out of compassion — as the owner believes
   it.** The owner says it, and the player SEES it: as he says it, a short
   film (M) goes to where they came from — a temple hall at night, the three
   before a monk, the chant, the candles (every detail checked against the
   research note before it is built; nothing from a cemetery or a body in
   the frame). It is his account of these three, never a history of
   kumanthong (§2.7).
3. **They help their keeper a little, and the merit he makes for them is
   believed to carry them to a better birth.** The owner says it as belief
   (ow5monk: *"They can't make merit for themselves, so you make it, and you
   give it to them. We believe it helps them on, toward a better birth."*).
   He dedicates his merit to them every night as he bows (z5dedic, §4.2), a
   wish, not a claim. D's dedication asks it for them, and the monk's Yathā
   vārivahā, captioned, says it in the canon's own words (*"even so does
   that here given benefit the dead"*). That is why they are kept: first
   for their sake; whatever help they give is a gift, not a wage.
4. **Long-term care, commitment and constant merit.** All of §2.5: the
   promise asked for, and given by the player's own press; the card, which
   puts plain water first; the receipts; every night; each ending judged by
   it; D's years — and the honest answer the sources give to "for life": if
   you truly cannot, bring them back; never leave them.
5. **Never greed.** The red one is what greed did to a child: taken home for
   luck, given back when it did not come fast enough (ow5red). The owner says
   it outright (ow5never: *"Never keep them for greed... if that is why you
   want them, don't take them."*). C is greed, and what it does to him and to
   the three. The rule is about why HE keeps them: nothing suggests that
   asking for luck is someone else's foolishness (lottery wishes are made at
   Thai temple shrines too — §2.7).
6. **Never asked for anything wrong.** The owner says it (ow5wrong: *"And
   never ask them to do anything wrong. The ones from a temple, we believe,
   won't help you do wrong. Children grow like the one who raises them...
   so you keep the precepts yourself."*), and the card says it. In C his
   wanting turns on a rival (z5Cevil), and nothing answers; his reading of
   that silence is the scene's last line (z5C7: *"Nothing answered. They
   wouldn't do wrong... even when I asked them to."*). "They keep the
   precepts" is in no source, so the chapter says what the sources do say
   (§2.7).
7. **Not cheap horror.** §2.4 3 holds: no scare, no stinger, no jump. In B,
   cheap horror is what HE makes of them, and that is its harm. The case
   file's Yin and Yang stays in A, in its own words, as the reason a living
   person keeps his own balance — never as the misconception (§2.3 14).

**The test, beside §2.4 7 and §2.5 6**, for every line and every frame: does
this leave a player thinking Kumanthong are something dark, something to
use, or something to take home on a feeling? If it does, it is not finished.
What it should leave behind is children a monk gave a home to, kept for
their sake, for life, within the precepts — and never invited in.

### 2.7 WHAT THE SOURCES SAY (the research, 25 Oct — docs/research/V23.0-KUMANTHONG.md)

A researcher read Thai, English and Chinese sources in the original — two
academic studies (Megan Sinnott, 2014; Krisda Mekakad's Chiang Mai
University thesis, 2017), the Pali canon, Thai news, monastic sites, care
guides and sellers (labelled as such), and Chinese-language media — and
checked every claim in this plan. Its verdicts, and what the plan does with
them:

- **Supported, kept**: children's spirits "with nowhere to go" (Thai
  belief); the red drink with a straw, sweets and toys; talking to them and
  calling them to eat; a keeper's merit dedicated to them and the hope of a
  better birth, as belief; a family returning one when the luck did not
  come (the red one); one left at a temple (the little one); a shop owner
  who entrusts, pities and takes them back (the owner); never asking them
  for harm; a kumanthong taking over a keeper whose mind gives way (the
  possessions); greed as the wrong reason; the harm of treating them as a
  horror story; the Yathā vārivahā.
- **Belief, now said as belief**: that a monk made them out of compassion
  (the best-documented monk-made kumanthong, Luang Phor Tae of Wat Sam
  Ngam's from about 1942, were made for protection; the compassionate
  account is today's devotee belief, so it is the owner's account of THESE
  three); and that they help a kind keeper while the merit carries them to
  a better birth (well attested as belief: "a central rationale", Sinnott).
- **In no source, rewritten**: "they keep the precepts". The sources say a
  keeper must never ask one to harm anyone or for anything wrong, that the
  temple kind is believed not to lend itself to wrongdoing, and that a
  kumanthong grows like the one who raises it. The owner says exactly that
  (ow5wrong), and C's last line is his reading of a silence (z5C7).
- **Aimed more precisely**: what Thai and Chinese sources themselves call the
  dark practice is a spirit trapped or forced by spells, foetal remains,
  blood and use for harm, and the fear of "backlash" if you stop. That is
  the misconception, and it is corrected there; nothing denies that they
  are children's spirits, or that people ask them for luck.
- **Added**: plain water before each child, the rule the Thai guides state
  most firmly; and the Thai answer to "for life" — if you truly cannot,
  bring them back to the shop or a temple; never leave them.
- **Disputed, Chad's call**: the incense count (§2.3 16), "Buddha Day"
  (§2.3 17), a bedroom altar (§2.3 18), the Yin and Yang (§2.3 14).
- **Kept out of every image**: cemeteries, cremation pyres, bones, ash, oil
  or anything from a body. Real consecrations sometimes use them, and
  showing them would teach the misconception. The monk who made them is
  never named (§2.3 12).

  A is pity: the little one asked him to stay. B is the wish to be
  believed. C is the thought that if they are real, they can help. D is
  love that knows where to stop.
- **Each ending has its own world**, so the four do not look alike either.
  A is told in his BODY (the nights, a giggle that is not his), B on the

- **There is no middle answer now.** With "give them back" gone (Chad's
  call), there are three ways to fail the children and one way to care for

- **Each is judged by the promise** (§2.5 4, rev 3). He promised the owner

## 5. Engine work (all opt-in; episodes 1 and 2 never reach it; the fixture proves each)

The chapter's real engine build. Each piece gets a row in CLAUDE.md's "what
a chapter owns" table at release.

- **S37 · the `craft` event kind** — hands-on steps on a held prop, for the
  drinks and the incense. `kit.event({ kind: 'craft', steps: [{ g: 'twist',
  turns }, { g: 'slide', dir: 'down'|'side', len }, { g: 'tap', n },
  { g: 'hold', secs }, { g: 'swipe', dir }], at: () => screenPoint,
  onStep(i), onProgress(i, k), gentle: true, brief })`.
  - Gestures are read around the prop's projected point (`at`):
    - a twist is the signed angle the finger sweeps round it, either
      direction, needing at least 40 px of radius;
    - a slide is the drag projected on `dir`, as a share of `len` × the
      screen's height;
    - a swipe is a fast drag over 25 % of the width;
    - tap and hold are what they say.
  - Desktop under pointer lock: the mouse's circling for twist, its movement
    for slide and swipe, E held (twist at a steady speed, slide, hold),
    Space (tap). The pointer lock is released while it runs (v22.0 steady's
    law).
  - Each step draws its own looping hint AT the prop: a fingertip circling,
    pulling, tapping, pressing or flicking. That is the minigame rule (v18.2):
    `EV_DEMO.craft = 'twist'` for the brief, plus five hint classes in
    shell.html.
  - `gentle: true`: no grade bands, no price. The result reports `care`
    (false if any twist outran 720° a second). `kitReset` clears it; a
    scene's first frame aborts it.
  - The fixture proves: each gesture by REAL pointer input (v18.3 — a
    minigame probe drives the real input path) completes its step; a twist
    either way counts; a slide the wrong way does not; a swipe needs its
    speed; the hints are drawn.
- **S38 · the `yield` event kind** — press-and-hold to let go.
  - A ring at a world point fills while anything is held (a finger, the
    mouse, E or Space) over `fill` seconds. Released, it pauses (or drains
    at `back`). `auto: true` fills on its own after a beat.
  - `onHold(k)` every frame, so the chapter drives the loosening by k.
  - No grade, never fails. Its own picture, `press`: a fingertip pressing
    and holding as a ring fills.
  - The fixture proves the rate, the pause, auto, keys and mouse.
- **S39 · the `surface` event kind** — climb back against a pull.
  - A vertical gauge: the mark rises with a drag up (finger), the mouse up
    under lock, or rapid W / ↑ / Space; it sinks at a constant rate.
  - Pulls come every `[1.6, 2.4]` s (`onPull` lets the chapter ring the bells
    and draw the ring).
  - Under `floorK` it costs `slip` sanity, floored at `minSanity`. Above
    `goal` for 0.4 s, he is back.
  - `giveUp` after `secs` forces success at the full price, so it never
    soft-locks.
  - Graded on time (≤ 10 s full marks). It runs inside a film (v7.0: events
    run from play or from inside a film). Its own picture, `surface`.
  - The fixture proves: drag up wins, no input ends only by giveUp, slips

## 8. Camera and framing rules for this chapter

1. **Never a third-person shot of him** (no rigged adult him — §2.2 4).
   Every shot is his eyes, or a THING, or another person (the owner; the
   monk in film M and in D).
2. **The phone crop.** The lens is 72° VERTICAL and fixed, so a portrait
   phone sees a centre third of the width (docs/AUDIT-2026-09.md Part One):
   about 37° across on a 390 × 844 screen. From the mat (0.93 m away) the
   three span about 50°, so a phone sees one or two of them at a time. Every
   play beat that names a child turns to that child. Every shot that must
   hold all three pulls back to the bed (2.1 m, about 26°) or goes
   overhead. Each framing is solved numerically for both screens first
   (e3c3's `fitD` approach), then photographed.
3. **Macro** with a narrow lens (40–48°) at 0.20–0.35 m, never a wide lens
   close (the 8 cm near plane — v6.12). Every lens track states when it
   stops.
4. **Cuts across time** hide under black or a flash. A film begins on its own
   black (v4.6), except the possessions, which begin on the yield's last
   frame and must be continuous, and the counter's films M and K, which begin
   and end on the counter's own frame by a dissolve, so the counter is never
   broken by black.
5. **A turn that hands over to a walk ends where the walk begins** (v21.30).
6. **In a cutaway away from his eyes, the room looks at HIM**: the figures'
   eyes catch the lamp toward the mat (v21.30's law, in a room of statues).
7. **The hands are never disembodied** (v20.1): every pose is searched.
8. **No subtitles** but D's verse — the monk's Yathā vārivahā, captioned in
   its four lines as chapter 4 captions it (v21.31: every chant with words is
   captioned) — and M1's placeholders.
