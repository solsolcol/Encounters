# v4.2 · How the sound gets to the player

This is the memory of one change: the sound stopped being a single download
that grew with the game. Read it before touching `build.py`'s pack section,
`main.js`'s `packLoad`, or anything in `assets/audio-opus/`.

## What prompted it

At the end of v4.1 the sound pack was 95 sounds and 6.9 MB, and the closing
note to Chad called it "the obvious next thing to shrink". His reply was the
better question:

> shouldn't the sound part be streaming, cache, and on-demand too? it
> shouldnt grow like that as a single download right?

He was right about the part that mattered. Two of the three were already
true and the third was the actual problem:

- **cached** — yes, and always had been. `_headers` gives every file under
  `assets/` a year of `immutable`, and the name carries a content hash, so a
  returning player re-downloads a sound only when its bytes change.
- **on-demand** — yes, in the sense that mattered for the first frame. The
  pack was never preloaded, never awaited, and buffers decoded lazily on
  first use.
- **one file** — this was the real complaint. Every player downloaded every
  chapter's sounds. Chapter 3's twenty-three landed on people who never
  leave chapter 1, and the number only ever went up.

"Streaming" is the wrong tool and worth saying why: streaming means starting
playback before the bytes finish arriving, which is for a five-minute music
bed, not for a 40 KB door creak that has to fire on the frame you touch the
door. The fixable version of the complaint is **split downloads**, and that
is what this is.

Chad's instruction on the compression half was two sentences, and both are
constraints, not preferences: *"make sure your compression doesn't lower the
quality"* and *"it should remain stereo"*.

## What changed

Two independent things, either of which works without the other.

### A · The pack is split, and the split is computed

One shared pack, plus one per chapter. `build.py` decides which is which; no
chapter declares anything, because **adding a chapter must not add work** —
the same law that already shapes `chapter_assets()`.

The rule: **a sound belongs to a chapter when exactly one chapter can ask
for it and the engine never does. Everything else is shared.**

The bias is deliberate and it is not symmetric. A sound wrongly left in the
shared pack costs a few KB. A sound wrongly moved out of it is a cue that
plays nothing, with no error, anywhere — the exact failure CLAUDE.md warns
no screenshot can catch. So anything the analysis cannot positively
attribute stays shared.

Two things are read out of `main.js` rather than duplicated:

- **`STING_SAMPLE`**, because a scene says `sfx(t, 'gong')`, not
  `gong.mp3` — the table is how a cue becomes a file. But the table is only
  a *vocabulary*: a row proves the kind exists, not that anyone plays it. So
  the table's own span is **excluded** when scanning for what the engine
  plays, or every sample in the game would look engine-owned and nothing
  would ever split.
- **`packWarm([...])` lists**, excluded for the same reason. They are decode
  *hints*, and the floors in `warmIntroSet`/`startDecision` still name
  chapter 2's sounds by hand. Warming a name whose pack is not loaded is a
  harmless no-op, so a stale hint must not pin a sound into the shared pack
  for ever.

What that produces today:

| pack | sounds | mp3 | opus |
|---|---|---|---|
| shared | 49 | 5124 KB | 3442 KB |
| ch1 | 7 | 733 KB | 422 KB |
| ch2 | 16 | 1417 KB | 802 KB |
| ch3 | 23 | 1832 KB | 1107 KB |

The fixture chapter widens the shared pack slightly — `chtest` declares
choices `A`–`D` with the default `sayPrefix`, so it claims `vA`–`vD` and
they stay shared rather than moving to chapter 1. That is the bias working
as designed, it costs a chapter-1 player nothing (they download those
either way), and it is not worth special-casing a fixture to reclaim.

**When each pack loads.** Shared and the booting chapter's, at boot.
`setChapter()` loads the new chapter's, which covers `?ch=`, resume, and the
advance path. And `startDecision()` loads the *next* chapter's — the one
moment that is both late enough and early enough: late, because a player who
stops before ever opening the decision still has not paid for a chapter they
did not reach, which is the entire point; early, because a cutscene, a
teaching card and a rank screen come after it, so a megabyte has minutes to
land before the next chapter's opening film asks for its first line.

**Nothing below the seam changed.** `sndBuf()` is untouched. The packs
`Object.assign` into the same `packJson` it has always read, so a pack
arriving late is simply more names in the object, and a loop whose sound is
not there yet starts silently on the next frame once it is.

### B · A second encoding, chosen by decoding it

Opus, at 96 kbps stereo and 64 kbps mono: **6.9 MB → 4.5 MB**.

Everything below is why that is not a quality cut.

**Encoded from the masters, not from what shipped.** The ElevenLabs
originals for all 95 sounds survived in the session scratchpad. The mp3s
that ship today are *second* generation — master, then the measured gain
pass, then a re-encode. The Opus set is encoded from the master in one step,
so it is *first* generation. That is one fewer lossy step than the file it
replaces, not one more.

**Levels are matched, not recomputed.** Per file, the gain applied is
exactly `shipped peak − master peak`, so the mix Chad approved is
reproduced rather than re-derived from a rule. Re-deriving would have been
wrong: only chapter 3's sounds were ever produced by the measured-gain
script, and applying it to chapters 1 and 2 would have quietly remixed them.

**The masters had to be identified, not assumed.** The first pass matched by
filename and 20 of 95 came out at a different level — `fire` was 9.8 dB
quieter. The cause was not the encoding: several sounds were regenerated
during development and `raw/` holds the *earlier* take. Peak-matching cannot
detect that, because it happily rescales the wrong recording. The fix was to
identify each master by **crest factor** (peak minus mean, which is
gain-invariant) plus duration. 92 of 95 matched a true master; the other
three — `ashburst`, `gscream`, `type` — are transcoded from the shipped mp3,
which costs one generation but makes their level identical by construction.

**Verified, three ways.**

- *Spectrally, against the master.* Octave bands from 125 Hz to 8 kHz track
  within **0.1 dB**; 16 kHz is within 0.7 dB, where the mp3 master is
  already rolling off at −36 dB. Opus at 128 kbps measured identical to 96,
  which is the useful result: 96 already captures everything the source
  contains, and more bits buy nothing.
- *Against what ships today.* For `vA` the shipped second-generation mp3
  sits **1.3 dB** off the master in the top three bands. The Opus encode
  sits within 0.1. It is a more faithful copy than the live file.
- *In a browser, all 95, decoded.* Every one decodes. Every one is
  **sample-identical in length** (0 to −1 samples out of 529,200), which is
  what makes it safe for loops — a length change would drift the loop point.
  Levels are within 0.5 dB for 94 of 95.

**The one outlier, run to ground.** `doorcreak` reads 1.12 dB quieter over
the whole file, and 3 dB quieter over its first 0.4 s. Two red herrings
first: raising it to 128, 160 and 192 kbps changed the number not at all,
which rules out a bitrate ceiling; and its silent tail is 18 dB quieter in
Opus, because Opus reproduces silence where mp3 puts quantisation noise, which
explains the whole-file figure but not the first 0.4 s.

The actual cause is **DC offset**. The mp3 carries one of 0.1096; the Opus
encode carries 0.0182. High-pass both at 20 Hz and the same window measures
**−19.2 dB in each, identical to the decimal**. DC is 0 Hz — it is not sound,
it is inaudible by definition, and it costs headroom that a door creak could
have used. Opus did not lose anything here; it removed an artifact. That also
resolves what looked like a contradiction in the measurements: every octave
band from 31 Hz to 16 kHz matched within **0.2 dB** while total energy did
not, which is exactly the signature of energy sitting below the lowest band.

**Stereo stays stereo.** All 47 sound effects and loops keep both channels.
The 48 spoken lines were already mono when ElevenLabs produced them — that
is what a single voice is, and nothing was collapsed to make it so.

**Sample rate is the one contract term that moved.** Opus only encodes at
48 kHz, so the Opus set is 48 kHz where the mp3s are 44.1. This discards
nothing: it is an upsample, and Web Audio resamples every buffer to the
output device's rate regardless of what the file said.

**AAC was the obvious candidate and is disqualified.** It is ~24% smaller
than mp3 and universally supported on Apple hardware — but **the test
Chromium cannot decode it**, because Playwright's build ships without the
proprietary codecs (the same fact that gave the title video a VP9 encode).
A format the suite cannot exercise is a format the suite cannot defend, and
it would fail the same way on Chromium builds real players use. Opus is
royalty-free and decodes everywhere the tests run.

**Which is why the codec is decode-tested, not guessed.** Safari's Ogg
support is recent, and Chad's players are phone-first in a market with a lot
of older iPhones. Guessing wrong here is not a slightly bigger download, it
is a game with no sound. So a **179-byte Opus file is decoded** through an
`OfflineAudioContext` before anything is fetched, and only a browser that
really produced an `AudioBuffer` from it is given the Opus packs. Everything
else gets the mp3s — the exact bytes that ship today, untouched. An
`OfflineAudioContext` is used deliberately: it needs no user gesture, so the
answer is ready long before the first tap.

**The mp3s were not re-encoded and must not be.** They are the proven
fallback. Leaving them byte-identical is what makes "the fallback is exactly
what shipped before" a fact rather than a hope.

## What a player actually downloads

| | today | v4.2 mp3 | v4.2 opus |
|---|---|---|---|
| chapter 1 | 6830 KB | 5857 KB | **3864 KB** |
| chapter 4 adds | +its own | +0 | **+0** |

−43% for the common case, and the number stops growing with the game, which
was the actual question.

## Traps, for whoever is next here

- **The `STING_SAMPLE` span must stay excluded from the engine scan.** Include
  it and every sample looks engine-owned, the split silently collapses to
  "everything is shared", and the only symptom is that the download stops
  shrinking. `build.py` asserts coverage, not usefulness — this one would
  pass every check.
- **`packWarm` floors are hints, not ownership.** They still name chapter 2's
  sounds. Treating them as ownership pins `fan`, `clock`, `bedcreak` and
  `doorcreak` into the shared pack for ever.
- **Never re-derive levels from the gain rule.** It was written for chapter
  3 alone. Match the shipped file.
- **Never identify a master by filename.** Sounds were regenerated; `raw/`
  holds earlier takes. Crest factor plus duration is the test.
- **The pack is keyed by name and the decoder is format-agnostic.** If one
  sound ever needs to go back to mp3 while the rest stay Opus, dropping that
  one mp3 into `assets/audio-opus/` under the same stem is a legitimate
  one-file escape hatch. Nothing else needs to know.
- **`build.py` fails the build** if a chapter's cue would land in no pack it
  loads, or if a sound the engine plays has left the shared pack. Both are
  impossible while the rule holds — which is exactly why they are asserted.
